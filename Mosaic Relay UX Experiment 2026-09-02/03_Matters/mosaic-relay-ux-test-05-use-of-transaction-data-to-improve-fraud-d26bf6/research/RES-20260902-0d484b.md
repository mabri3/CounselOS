      ## Matter summary

      Mosaic Relay, a payment-infrastructure provider, plans to use transaction metadata

      device signals, payment method details, transaction amounts, merchant category,
      broad regional location, dispute outcomes, and account events. The model would

      obligations.

      ## Decision question

      Can Mosaic Relay lawfully deploy a fraud-detection model trained on cross-merchant
      transaction data to make real-time authorization and payout decisions without
      human review, and what contractual, notice, and technical safeguards must be
      in place before launch to comply with GDPR Article 22 and comparable automated-decision-making
      requirements across multiple jurisdictions?

      ## Open questions

      - Which exact identifiers are retained after de-identification (e.g., device
      IDs, IP addresses, account IDs, email hashes)?

      - What do current merchant contracts say about use of transaction data, and
      do they permit cross-merchant aggregation?

      - Are the model''s authorization and payout decisions subject to human review
      today, and do consumers currently receive notice or have opt-out rights regarding
      this data use?

      - Where will the data be processed and stored, and are cross-border transfers
      involved?

      - Is device data (device signals, device IDs, IP addresses) treated as personal
      information in the relevant locations where merchants, payers, and recipients
      are based?

      ## Likely rules and issues

      **No external authority retrieved.**

      Based on the matter facts and general legal principles, the following rules
      and issues are likely material:

      ### GDPR Article 22 — Automated individual decision-making, including profiling

      Article 22 grants data subjects the right not to be subject to a decision based
      solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects
      concerning them or similarly significantly affects them. Real-time authorization
      and payout decisions likely qualify as decisions that "similarly significantly
      affect" data subjects because they can block access to funds, delay payments,
      or terminate transactions.

      **Key issues:**

      - **Solely automated**: If there is no meaningful human involvement in the decision
      loop, Article 22 applies.

      - **Legal or similarly significant effects**: Blocking a payout or declining
      an authorization likely meets this threshold.

      - **Exceptions**: Article 22(2) permits such decisions only if they are:

      - Necessary for entering into or performing a contract;

      - Authorized by Union or Member State law; or

      - Based on explicit consent.

      - **Safeguards**: Even when an exception applies, Article 22(3) requires suitable
      measures to safeguard the data subject''s rights and freedoms and legitimate
      interests, at minimum the right to obtain human intervention, to express their
      point of view, and to contest the decision.

      ### Lawful basis for processing (Article 6)

      Cross-merchant aggregation and model training require a lawful basis. The most
      likely candidates are:

      - **Legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f))**: Fraud prevention is a recognized
      legitimate interest, but the balancing test must account for the scale of data
      combination, retention period, and impact on data subjects.

      - **Contractual necessity (Article 6(1)(b))**: May apply to fraud screening
      necessary to provide the payment service, but cross-merchant aggregation for
      model training may exceed what is strictly necessary.

      ### Controller vs. processor roles

      - **Mosaic Relay**: Likely a controller for the fraud-model training purpose
      because it determines the purposes and means of processing (combining data across
      merchants, training the model, using outputs for authorization/payout decisions).

      - **Merchants**: Likely controllers for their own transaction data collection.

      - **Fraud vendors and cloud providers**: Likely processors acting on Mosaic
      Relay''s instructions.

      This allocation requires:

      - Controller-to-controller data-sharing agreements or terms with merchants;

      - Data processing agreements (DPAs) with fraud vendors and cloud providers;

      - Clear allocation of responsibilities for transparency, data subject rights,
      and security.

      ### De-identification and pseudonymization

      Pseudonymization (Article 4(5)) does not remove data from the scope of the GDPR;
      it remains personal data. True anonymization requires that the data subject
      is not or no longer identifiable, taking account of all means reasonably likely
      to be used. Device signals, IP addresses, and account IDs are often treated
      as personal data even when pseudonymized.

      **Key issue**: The belief that aggregation and pseudonymization reduce privacy
      risk is partially correct but does not eliminate GDPR obligations. The exact
      identifiers retained after de-identification must be confirmed with engineering.

      ### Retention (Article 5(1)(e))

      Seven-year retention of the training set must be justified as necessary for
      the fraud-prevention purpose. Retention beyond what is necessary violates the
      storage-limitation principle. A documented retention schedule and deletion procedures
      are required.

      ### Transparency and notice (Articles 13 and 14)

      Data subjects must be informed about:

      - The existence of automated decision-making, including profiling;

      - Meaningful information about the logic involved;

      - The significance and envisaged consequences of such processing.

      This applies at the time data is collected (Article 13) or within a reasonable
      period if obtained from another source (Article 14).

      ### Cross-border transfers (Chapter V)

      If data is transferred outside the EEA, an adequacy decision, standard contractual
      clauses, or another transfer mechanism is required. The locations of processing
      and storage must be confirmed.

      ### Comparable requirements in other jurisdictions

      - **UK GDPR**: Mirrors GDPR Article 22.

      - **California (CCPA/CPRA)**: Requires notice of automated decision-making and
      opt-out rights for certain profiling.

      - **Other US states**: Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, and Utah have similar
      provisions.

      - **Sectoral rules**: Payment services and consumer protection laws may impose
      additional requirements.

      ## Viable paths

      ### Path 1: Full GDPR Article 22 compliance with human review

      **Description**: Implement meaningful human review for all authorization and
      payout decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects.

      **Requirements**:

      - Human-in-the-loop for all decisions meeting the Article 22 threshold;

      - Documented lawful basis (likely legitimate interests);

      - Controller-to-controller agreements with merchants;

      - DPAs with fraud vendors and cloud providers;

      - Transparency notices to data subjects;

      - Data subject rights procedures (access, rectification, erasure, restriction,
      objection, human intervention);

      - Retention schedule and deletion procedures;

      - Cross-border transfer mechanisms if applicable.

      **Pros**: Lowest legal risk; clear compliance posture.

      **Cons**: Operational burden; may slow real-time decisions.

      ### Path 2: Rely on contractual necessity or explicit consent exception

      **Description**: Structure the processing to fit within Article 22(2)(a) (contractual
      necessity) or obtain explicit consent (Article 22(2)(c)).

      **Requirements**:

      - Demonstrate that automated decisions are necessary for entering into or performing
      the payment contract;

      - Or obtain explicit consent from data subjects for automated decision-making;

      - Implement Article 22(3) safeguards (human intervention, express point of view,
      contest decision);

      - All other requirements from Path 1.

      **Pros**: May reduce need for human review in all cases.

      **Cons**: Contractual necessity is narrowly interpreted; explicit consent is
      difficult to obtain and manage at scale.

      ### Path 3: True anonymization

      **Description**: Achieve true anonymization so the data falls outside GDPR scope.

      **Requirements**:

      - Remove all identifiers, including device IDs, IP addresses, and account IDs;

      - Ensure no reasonably likely means of re-identification;

      - Document the anonymization methodology and risk assessment.

      **Pros**: GDPR does not apply to truly anonymous data.

      **Cons**: Very difficult to achieve with device signals and transaction metadata;
      may reduce model utility; re-identification risk remains.

      ### Path 4: Jurisdictional scoping and phased rollout

      **Description**: Limit initial deployment to jurisdictions with lower regulatory
      risk or where Mosaic Relay has confirmed compliance.

      **Requirements**:

      - Map jurisdictions of merchants, payers, and recipients;

      - Confirm device data treatment in each location;

      - Implement jurisdiction-specific notices and rights;

      - Phase rollout based on risk assessment.

      **Pros**: Reduces immediate compliance burden; allows learning and adjustment.

      **Cons**: May limit model training data and effectiveness; ongoing monitoring
      required.
