# What de-identification and pseudonymization standards do GDPR and major US

## Question

What de-identification and pseudonymization standards do GDPR and major US state privacy laws (e.g., CCPA/CPRA) recognize for training data, and when does pseudonymized data remain personal data?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment-infrastructure provider, wants to combine transaction metadata from multiple merchants to train a fraud-detection model. The data includes device signals, payment details, and behavioral events, with direct identifiers removed but retained for seven years. Counsel is involved because the business needs to launch this model and must determine whether the de-identification approach satisfies GDPR and US state privacy laws before processing begins.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay lawfully train fraud models on cross-merchant transaction data using pseudonymization and aggregation, or must it achieve true anonymization under GDPR and CCPA/CPRA standards, given that device identifiers remain personal data in some jurisdictions and the seven-year retention period may exceed storage-limitation requirements?
## Open questions
- Which specific identifiers (device IDs, IP addresses, account hashes) remain in the training set after de-identification, and can they be linked back to individuals using Mosaic Relay’s internal systems or vendor tools?
- Do current merchant contracts and DPAs expressly permit cross-merchant aggregation and secondary use for fraud-model training, or is this use currently prohibited?
- In which jurisdictions will the data subjects (payers/recipients) reside, and where will the training data be stored and processed (cross-border transfer implications)?
- Will the model’s real-time authorization and payout decisions be fully automated without human review, triggering GDPR Article 22 or CCPA/CPRA automated-decision requirements?
- Can Mosaic Relay implement true anonymization (irreversible de-identification) for the training set, or is pseudonymization the only feasible technical approach given the need for model updates?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on the matter facts and general legal standards, the following rules likely apply:
### GDPR standards
- **Pseudonymization vs. anonymization**: Pseudonymized data remains personal data under GDPR Recital 26 and Article 4(5) if the controller or a third party can re-identify individuals using additional information or reasonable means. True anonymization requires irreversible de-identification such that re-identification is impossible using all means reasonably likely to be used.
- **Device identifiers**: IP addresses, device IDs, and cookie identifiers are expressly considered personal data under GDPR (Recital 30), even when pseudonymized, if they can be linked to a natural person.
- **Storage limitation**: Article 5(1)(e) requires retention no longer than necessary. Seven years for training data likely exceeds this unless justified by specific legal obligations or compelling legitimate interests that override data-subject rights.
- **Automated decision-making**: Article 22 restricts solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. Real-time authorization and payout decisions likely qualify, requiring explicit consent, contract necessity, or authorization by law, plus safeguards including human intervention rights.
### CCPA/CPRA standards
- **De-identified data definition**: CPRA (California Civil Code § 1798.140(m)) defines de-identified data as information that cannot reasonably be linked to a consumer or household, requiring technical and organizational safeguards to prevent re-identification. Pseudonymized data generally remains "personal information" unless it meets this high bar.
- **Device identifiers**: California expressly includes IP addresses and device identifiers in the definition of personal information (§ 1798.140(o)(1)).
- **Retention**: CPRA requires disclosure of retention periods and prohibits retaining data longer than reasonably necessary for the disclosed purpose.
- **Automated decision-making**: CPRA grants consumers the right to opt-out of automated decision-making technology in connection with profiling, effective January 1, 2023, with regulations still evolving.
### Cross-cutting issues
- **Controller/processor roles**: If Mosaic Relay determines the purposes and means of the cross-merchant training (rather than acting solely on merchant instructions), it likely acts as a controller (GDPR) or business (CCPA), not a processor/service provider, triggering direct compliance obligations.
- **Lawful basis**: Legitimate interests (GDPR Article 6(1)(f)) may support fraud prevention, but cross-merchant aggregation requires careful balancing tests and transparency. Consent may be required if legitimate interests are insufficient.
- **Contractual authority**: Current merchant agreements likely do not authorize cross-merchant data pooling for Mosaic Relay’s independent fraud-model training, creating a contract gap.
## Viable paths
**Path A: True anonymization (lowest legal risk)**
- Implement irreversible de-identification: remove all direct and indirect identifiers, aggregate to cohort level, apply differential privacy or k-anonymity thresholds, and prohibit re-identification contractually and technically.
- **Consequence**: Data falls outside GDPR/CCPA scope, eliminating consent, notice, and retention concerns, but may reduce model accuracy.
**Path B: Pseudonymization with controller obligations (moderate risk)**
- Retain pseudonymized data (e.g., hashed account IDs) but treat it as personal data.
- Establish lawful basis (legitimate interests for fraud prevention), update privacy notices, honor opt-outs (CPRA) and objection rights (GDPR), implement human review for automated decisions, and shorten retention to 2–3 years unless legally required otherwise.
- Amend merchant contracts to permit cross-merchant aggregation and secondary use for fraud modeling.
**Path C: Merchant-specific models (highest feasibility, lowest utility)**
- Train separate models per merchant without cross-merchant data pooling.
- **Consequence**: Avoids cross-merchant aggregation issues but loses network-effect fraud detection benefits.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Identifier reversibility**: If engineering confirms that device IDs and IP addresses are irreversibly hashed with no key retention, the data may qualify as anonymized under GDPR Recital 26, removing most obligations.
- **Contractual permissions**: If merchant agreements already grant Mosaic Relay broad rights to use transaction data for "service improvement" or "fraud prevention," Path B becomes viable without contract amendments.
- **Human review**: If product teams confirm that all automated authorization/payout decisions currently include meaningful human review, Article 22 and CPRA automated-decision restrictions may not apply.
- **Jurisdiction scope**: If data subjects are exclusively outside the EU/UK and California, GDPR and CPRA may not apply, though other state laws (Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut) impose similar de-identification standards.
- **Retention justification**: If fraud-model retraining requires seven years of data to detect long-term patterns (e.g., sleeper fraud), a documented legitimate-interest assessment might justify extended retention under GDPR, though this remains high-risk.
## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, counsel should verify:
1. **Technical de-identification standards**: Obtain written confirmation from engineering on whether re-identification is technically possible, including whether hash keys or lookup tables exist.
2. **Contract review**: Audit current merchant agreements and DPAs for data-use restrictions, cross-customer aggregation prohibitions, and retention limits.
3. **Jurisdiction mapping**: Confirm where payers/recipients are located to determine applicable laws (GDPR, UK GDPR, CPRA, VCDPA, CPA, CTDPA).
4. **Automated-decision audit**: Verify whether human review exists for all model-driven authorization and payout decisions, and document the review process.
5. **Retention schedule**: Validate the business necessity for seven-year retention against industry standards (typically 2–5 years for fraud models) and document the justification.
**Recommendation**: Pursue Path A (true anonymization) if technically feasible; otherwise, proceed under Path B with updated contracts, privacy notices, and a shortened retention period (3–4 years maximum), while implementing human-in-the-loop review for all automated decisions. Do not rely solely on pseudonymization as a compliance shield given that device identifiers remain personal data in key jurisdictions.
