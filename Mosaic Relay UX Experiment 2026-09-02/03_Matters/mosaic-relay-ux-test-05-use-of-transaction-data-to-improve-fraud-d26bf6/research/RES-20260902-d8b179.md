# Privacy and data-protection analysis for Mosaic Relay's use of cross-merchant

## Question

Privacy and data-protection analysis for Mosaic Relay's use of cross-merchant transaction metadata (device signals, payment method details, transaction amounts, merchant category, broad regional location, dispute outcomes, account events) to train a fraud-detection model. Address: (1) lawful basis for cross-merchant aggregation and secondary fraud-model training; (2) controller vs processor role allocation across Mosaic Relay, merchants, fraud vendors, and cloud providers; (3) whether de-identification/pseudonymization is sufficient to avoid personal-data obligations given device data is personal in some jurisdictions; (4) seven-year retention justification and deletion procedures; (5) automated decision-making and human-review requirements for real-time authorization and payout decisions; (6) cross-border transfer compliance; (7) contract language with merchants and vendors; (8) notice and opt-out rights for consumers. Jurisdictions: multiple/global, primarily North America and Europe (GDPR, US state privacy laws, CCPA/CPRA, etc.).

## Working Analysis

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, wants to combine transaction metadata from multiple merchants to train a fraud-detection model that will drive real-time authorization and payout decisions. The data includes device signals, payment method details, transaction amounts, merchant category, broad regional location, dispute outcomes, and account events; direct identifiers would be removed and the training set retained for seven years. Counsel is involved because the business needs a legal answer before a planned launch, and the proposal raises unresolved questions about lawful basis, controller/processor roles, de-identification sufficiency, retention, automated decision-making, cross-border transfers, and consumer rights across multiple jurisdictions (primarily North America and Europe).
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay lawfully aggregate transaction metadata across merchants to train a fraud-detection model used in real-time authorization and payout decisions, and if so, what contractual, notice, retention, de-identification, and human-review conditions must be in place before launch to satisfy GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and other applicable US state privacy laws?

## Likely rules and issues
### 1. Lawful basis for cross-merchant aggregation and secondary fraud-model training
**GDPR (EU/EEA/UK):** Fraud prevention is a recognized legitimate interest under Recital 47, but using personal data collected for payment processing to train a cross-merchant model is a *secondary purpose* that requires a compatibility assessment under Art. 6(4). The more merchants and data subjects involved, the harder it is to argue compatibility without additional safeguards. If the data is not fully anonymized, Mosaic Relay needs its own lawful basis (likely legitimate interest, documented via a legitimate-interest assessment) and must ensure the original collection purpose (payment processing/fraud services for a specific merchant) is compatible with the new training purpose. Consent is generally impractical at scale and may not be freely given in a payment context.
**CCPA/CPRA (California):** Using personal information collected for one purpose (payment processing) for a new purpose (cross-merchant model training) may constitute a "secondary use" requiring notice and, in some cases, opt-out rights. The CPRA's "sharing" definition is broad; even if Mosaic Relay does not "sell" data, cross-merchant aggregation could be characterized as sharing for cross-context behavioral advertising if not carefully structured. Service-provider/contractor status is critical: if Mosaic Relay is a service provider to merchants, it cannot use the data for its own purposes (including model training) without explicit contractual authorization and consumer notice.
**Other US state laws (Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, etc.):** Similar secondary-use and purpose-limitation principles apply. Most require that processing be compatible with the original purpose or that consumers receive notice and an opportunity to opt out.
### 2. Controller vs. processor role allocation
- **Merchants** are likely controllers of their customers' (payers') data for payment processing.
- **Mosaic Relay** is likely a processor (or service provider/contractor under CCPA) when providing payment and fraud services to merchants. However, if Mosaic Relay uses the data for its own cross-merchant model training, it likely becomes a **controller** (or a "business" under CCPA) for that secondary purpose. This dual role is common but must be documented.
- **Fraud vendors and cloud providers** are likely sub-processors (or service providers) acting on Mosaic Relay's instructions. Their contracts must flow down data-protection obligations.
- **Model-governance staff** are internal actors; their access must be logged and limited.
**Key issue:** If Mosaic Relay is a processor for merchants, it cannot unilaterally decide to use merchant data for its own model training. It needs explicit authorization in the merchant agreement or DPA, and merchants (as controllers) must have a lawful basis and provide notice to their customers.
### 3. De-identification/pseudonymization sufficiency
**Pseudonymization is not anonymization.** Under GDPR, pseudonymized data is still personal data (Art. 4(5), Recital 26). Device IDs, IP addresses, and account IDs are personal data in the EU and in California (where "personal information" includes unique identifiers and internet activity). Removing direct identifiers (names, emails) but retaining device IDs, IP addresses, or account IDs likely leaves the data within the scope of privacy laws.
**True anonymization** requires that re-identification be reasonably impossible, considering all means reasonably likely to be used (GDPR Recital 26; CCPA's "deidentified" definition requires technical and organizational safeguards and a public commitment not to re-identify). Given the richness of the proposed dataset (device signals + payment method + amounts + merchant category + location + dispute outcomes + account events), re-identification risk is material, especially when combined across merchants.
**Practical consequence:** Mosaic Relay should assume the training set is personal data unless it can demonstrate true anonymization. This means all privacy obligations (lawful basis, notice, retention limits, data-subject rights, DPIA) apply.
### 4. Seven-year retention justification and deletion procedures
**GDPR:** Storage limitation (Art. 5(1)(e)) requires that personal data be kept no longer than necessary. Seven years is a long period for fraud-model training. Justification would need to show that fraud patterns evolve slowly and that older data remains materially useful. Even then, the retention period must be documented, and data must be deleted or anonymized at the end. Financial-record retention obligations (e.g., AML, tax) may justify some retention, but those obligations typically apply to transaction records, not to derived training sets.
**CCPA/CPRA:** Retention must be reasonably necessary and proportionate to the purpose. Seven years may be defensible for fraud prevention, but the business must document the rationale and honor deletion requests (subject to exceptions).
**Deletion procedures:** Must be able to delete or de-identify individual records in response to data-subject requests (GDPR Art. 17; CCPA deletion right). If the training set is truly anonymized, deletion requests do not apply, but the burden of proving anonymization is high. If pseudonymized, Mosaic Relay must be able to link records back to individuals (via the key) to honor deletion requests, which undermines the claim of de-identification.
### 5. Automated decision-making and human review
**GDPR Art. 22:** Data subjects have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects. Real-time authorization and payout decisions likely qualify (they affect access to funds and services). Exceptions exist (contract necessity, explicit consent, or legal authorization), but even then, safeguards are required: the right to obtain human intervention, express one's point of view, and contest the decision.
**Practical requirement:** If the model's decisions are fully automated and have significant effects, Mosaic Relay (or the merchant, as controller) must offer human review on request, provide meaningful information about the logic involved, and explain the significance and consequences. This is a high bar for real-time payment decisions.
**CCPA/CPRA:** The CPRA introduces rights related to automated decision-making technology (ADMT), including the right to opt out and the right to access information about the logic. Regulations are still being finalized, but businesses should prepare for disclosure and opt-out obligations.
**US state laws:** Colorado and Connecticut have similar automated-decision provisions.
### 6. Cross-border transfer compliance
**GDPR:** Transfers of personal data from the EEA/UK to the US or other third countries require an adequacy decision (e.g., EU-US Data Privacy Framework, if the US entity is certified) or appropriate safeguards (Standard Contractual Clauses, Binding Corporate Rules). Transfers to fraud vendors and cloud providers must be mapped and covered by SCCs or other mechanisms.
**Data localization:** Some jurisdictions (e.g., certain US states for specific data types, or non-EU countries) may impose localization or additional transfer restrictions.
**Practical step:** Map where data is collected, processed, stored, and accessed (including by vendors and cloud providers), and ensure transfer mechanisms are in place.
### 7. Contract language with merchants and vendors
**Merchant agreements / DPAs must:**
- Grant Mosaic Relay the right to use transaction data for cross-merchant fraud-model training (if merchants are controllers, they must authorize this secondary use and confirm they have a lawful basis and have provided notice to their customers).
- Allocate controller/processor roles clearly (Mosaic Relay as processor for payment services; Mosaic Relay as controller or joint controller for model training, or as a service provider with explicit authorization).
- Specify retention periods, deletion procedures, and data-subject rights handling.
- Address sub-processor authorization (fraud vendors, cloud providers).
- Include audit and security obligations.
**Vendor agreements (fraud vendors, cloud providers) must:**
- Impose data-protection obligations consistent with Mosaic Relay's obligations to merchants.
- Prohibit vendors from using data for their own purposes.
- Require deletion or return of data at the end of the relationship.
- Address cross-border transfers.
### 8. Notice and opt-out rights for consumers
**GDPR:** Controllers (merchants, and Mosaic Relay if it is a controller for model training) must provide transparent information about the processing (Art. 13/14), including the purposes, lawful basis, retention period, recipients, and data-subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection). If legitimate interest is the basis, the notice must explain the interest and the balancing test. Data subjects have the right to object to processing based on legitimate interest (Art. 21).
**CCPA/CPRA:** Businesses must provide a notice at collection describing the categories of personal information collected and the purposes. If data is used for a new purpose (model training), a new notice may be required. Consumers have the right to opt out of the "sale" or "sharing" of personal information. Even if Mosaic Relay does not sell data, cross-merchant aggregation could be characterized as sharing. The CPRA also grants the right to limit the use of sensitive personal information (which may include certain payment data).
**Practical challenge:** Mosaic Relay is a B2B infrastructure provider and may not have a direct relationship with payers and recipients. Notice obligations likely fall on the merchants (as controllers), but Mosaic Relay must ensure merchants provide adequate notice and honor opt-outs. This requires contractual commitments and operational cooperation.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Proceed as controller with explicit merchant authorization and consumer notice (highest risk, highest utility)
- Amend merchant agreements to grant Mosaic Relay the right to use transaction data for cross-merchant fraud-model training.
- Require merchants to provide notice to their customers and obtain any necessary consents or honor opt-outs.
- Conduct a DPIA (GDPR) and legitimate-interest assessment.
- Implement human review for automated decisions with significant effects.
- Limit retention to the shortest defensible period (e.g., 3–5 years, not 7, unless justified).
- Implement robust deletion and data-subject rights procedures.
- Map and document cross-border transfers; implement SCCs or rely on adequacy.
**Pros:** Maximizes model utility and business value. **Cons:** Highest legal and operational burden; requires merchant cooperation and consumer-facing changes.
### Path B: Proceed as service provider/processor with limited own-use rights (moderate risk, moderate utility)
- Structure the arrangement so Mosaic Relay acts as a service provider (CCPA) or processor (GDPR) for merchants, and model training is performed on behalf of merchants collectively (e.g., as a joint initiative or industry utility).
- Merchants remain controllers; Mosaic Relay processes data only on their documented instructions.
- Requires merchants to have lawful basis and provide notice; Mosaic Relay's role is limited.
**Pros:** Reduces Mosaic Relay's direct obligations. **Cons:** Merchants may be unwilling to take on the controller burden for a cross-merchant model; may not be feasible if Mosaic Relay wants to own the model.
### Path C: True anonymization before training (lowest risk, potentially lower utility)
- Invest in true anonymization (not just pseudonymization) before data enters the training set.
- Use techniques such as differential privacy, k-anonymity, or aggregation to ensure re-identification is not reasonably possible.
- If data is truly anonymized, privacy laws do not apply, and no lawful basis, notice, or data-subject rights are required.
**Pros:** Eliminates most privacy obligations. **Cons:** Anonymization is hard to achieve and may reduce model accuracy; re-identification risk must be continuously assessed; regulators are skeptical of anonymization claims.
### Path D: Delay launch pending further fact-gathering and contract review (prudent interim step)
- Do not launch until: (1) merchant contracts are reviewed and amended; (2) exact identifiers retained are confirmed; (3) human-review and opt-out mechanisms are confirmed; (4) cross-border transfers are mapped; (5) DPIA and legitimate-interest assessment are completed.
**Pros:** Avoids launching with unresolved legal risk. **Cons:** Delays business objective.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Exact identifiers retained:** If only truly anonymous aggregates are retained (no device IDs, IPs, account IDs, or linkable pseudonyms), the analysis shifts toward Path C (anonymization). If linkable identifiers are retained, Paths A or B are required.
- **Merchant contract terms:** If existing contracts already permit cross-merchant aggregation and secondary use, the path is easier. If they prohibit it, renegotiation is required.
- **Human review and opt-out mechanisms:** If human review is already in place for automated decisions, GDPR Art. 22 compliance is easier. If not, significant product changes are needed.
- **Cross-border transfer map:** If data stays within the EEA or within the US, transfer compliance is simpler. If data moves between the EEA and US, SCCs or DPF certification is required.
- **Jurisdictional scope:** If the merchant and consumer base is primarily US-only, GDPR does not apply, and the analysis focuses on CCPA/CPRA and state laws. If EU data subjects are involved, GDPR applies in full.
- **Retention justification:** If the business can demonstrate that fraud patterns require seven years of data (e.g., long-tail fraud schemes), retention may be defensible. If not, a shorter period (3–5 years) is safer.
