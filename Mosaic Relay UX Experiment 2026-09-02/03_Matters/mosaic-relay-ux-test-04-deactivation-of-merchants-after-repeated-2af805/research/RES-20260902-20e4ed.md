# What post-termination refund and dispute-response obligations apply to a payment

## Question

What post-termination refund and dispute-response obligations apply to a payment processor under card network rules and U.S. law?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, plans to deactivate merchants whose chargeback rate stays above a defined threshold for three consecutive months, after warnings, a required remediation plan, and restrictions on new payment methods. The merchant agreement permits suspension for excessive disputes but does not define notice content, cure period, or appeal process; intake answers supplied a 30-day cure with written appeal and marketplace override only via documented exception. Existing refunds and dispute responses would remain available for 90 days after deactivation. Counsel is involved because deactivation implicates card-network monitoring obligations, acquiring-bank requirements, and post-termination refund and dispute duties; no launch date is set, with an assumed initial U.S. launch covering Visa and Mastercard programs through acquiring-bank partners.
## Decision question
Before launch, can Mosaic Relay structure the 90-day post-deactivation window so that deactivated merchants can still process refunds and respond to chargebacks — and must it — given that card-network rules and U.S. consumer law may require refund and dispute capabilities to survive termination longer than 90 days, and what reserves, system access, and customer communications must accompany deactivation to avoid network fines, acquirer liability, and consumer-protection exposure?
## Open questions
- Do Visa and Mastercard rules (as passed through Mosaic Relay's acquiring-bank agreements) impose any minimum post-termination dispute-response or refund-processing period, and does a 90-day window satisfy it given chargeback windows can run 75–120+ days from transaction or expected delivery date?
- How will refunds and dispute responses be funded and executed after deactivation — reserve/holdback, continued limited dashboard access, or manual Support handling — and does the merchant agreement authorize the reserve needed?
- For recurring-services and physical-goods merchants, do late-arriving chargebacks (e.g., for services not yet rendered or goods not yet delivered at deactivation) extend obligations beyond 90 days, and how are pending authorizations and in-flight subscriptions handled?
- What customer-facing communication, if any, is required or advisable when a merchant is deactivated (particularly for active subscriptions), and who sends it — Mosaic Relay, the merchant, or the marketplace customer?
- Are there reporting obligations to networks or acquiring banks upon deactivation (e.g., merchant termination listings such as MATCH), and what triggers them?
## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of card-network operations and U.S. law; it is not retrieved or verified authority and must be confirmed against current network rules and Mosaic Relay's acquirer agreements.
Likely applicable frameworks:
- **Card network rules (Visa/Mastercard), as flowed down through acquiring banks.** Chargeback rights do not end when a merchant is terminated. Cardholders retain dispute rights tied to the transaction date (and, for some reason codes, to expected delivery/service dates), which commonly extend well beyond 90 days. Networks expect the acquirer — and by contract, the processor and merchant — to remain able to receive and respond to retrieval requests and chargebacks after termination. Liability for post-termination chargebacks typically shifts to the acquirer/processor if the merchant cannot respond or pay, which is why acquirers impose reserves. A hard 90-day cutoff for dispute responses is likely shorter than the practical chargeback tail for physical goods and recurring services.
- **Merchant termination reporting.** Acquirers are generally required to report merchants terminated for cause (including excessive chargebacks) to network terminated-merchant files (commonly known as MATCH under Mastercard, with a Visa equivalent). Listing has serious consequences for the merchant's ability to obtain processing elsewhere, so accuracy, timing, and the contractual basis for the listing reason code matter, and inaccurate listing is a known source of merchant litigation.
- **U.S. consumer law.** For card transactions, Regulation Z (Truth in Lending) gives cardholders billing-error and claims-and-defenses rights that operate at the issuer level and are not extinguished by merchant termination; the practical effect is that chargebacks will keep arriving. For recurring services, the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) and state automatic-renewal laws bear on how subscriptions must be wound down and how consumers are notified. State UDAP statutes create exposure if deactivation strands consumers who paid for undelivered goods or services without a refund path.
- **Contract law.** The merchant agreement permits suspension for excessive disputes but is silent on notice content, cure, and appeal. Post-termination refund/dispute mechanics, reserve rights, and survival language should be added or confirmed before launch; the 30-day cure with written appeal adopted in intake should be reflected in the notice templates and, ideally, the agreement itself.
## Viable paths
1. **Extend or soften the 90-day window (recommended shape).** Keep the dashboard fully available for 90 days, but preserve a manual, Support-mediated refund and dispute-response channel for the full chargeback tail (e.g., 120–180 days, or transaction-date-plus-network-limit), funded by a rolling reserve sized to the merchant's trailing chargeback volume. This matches network expectations and reduces acquirer pushback.
2. **Keep 90 days as designed, with a reserve backstop.** Proceed with the 90-day window but hold a reserve covering estimated post-window chargebacks, and route late disputes to Support for manual handling. Lower build cost, but requires acquirer sign-off and clear merchant-agreement reserve authority; risk sits with Mosaic Relay if the reserve is undersized.
3. **Segment by merchant type.** Apply the standard window to digital-goods merchants (shorter dispute tail) and an extended window or larger reserve to physical-goods and recurring-services merchants, where delivery and service horizons push chargebacks later. More operationally complex but better calibrated to actual liability timing.
## Facts that could change the answer
- The actual post-termination dispute-handling and reserve clauses in Mosaic Relay's acquiring-bank and processor agreements — these control what the networks require of Mosaic Relay in practice.
- Whether the merchant agreement authorizes post-termination reserves/holds and survival of refund/dispute obligations; if not, an amendment or updated terms flow is needed before launch.
- The mix of merchant verticals in scope and their average delivery/fulfillment horizons, which drive the realistic chargeback tail.
- Whether deactivation will be reported to terminated-merchant files, and under what reason code.
- Whether marketplace customers' own terms with their sellers impose separate wind-down or buyer-protection obligations.
