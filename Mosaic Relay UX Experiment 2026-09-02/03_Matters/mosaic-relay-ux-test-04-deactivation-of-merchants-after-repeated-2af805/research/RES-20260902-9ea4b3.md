# Visa and Mastercard chargeback monitoring program thresholds (Visa VAMP /

## Question

Visa and Mastercard chargeback monitoring program thresholds (Visa VAMP / VFMP, Mastercard Excessive Chargeback Merchant program), notice and cure expectations, post-deactivation refund and dispute response obligations, and reporting or customer communication duties for merchants deactivated for excessive chargebacks.

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay (a fictional test company) plans to deactivate merchants whose chargeback rate exceeds a defined threshold for three consecutive months, with warnings starting after month one, a required remediation plan, restrictions on new payment methods, and processing disabled after month three absent a documented exception. The merchant agreement permits suspension for excessive disputes but does not define notice content, cure period, or appeal rights, and existing refunds and dispute responses would remain available for 90 days post-deactivation. Counsel is involved because the program touches card-network monitoring obligations, acquiring-bank relationships, contractual suspension rights, and merchant/consumer communications. Intake answers set a 30-day cure with written appeal, marketplace override only via documented exception, and an assumed initial U.S. launch covering Visa and Mastercard programs through acquiring-bank partners.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay launch a U.S. three-strikes chargeback deactivation program — internal threshold plus Visa/Mastercard monitoring levels, warnings after month one, 30-day cure with written appeal, deactivation after month three, and 90-day post-termination refund/dispute access — under its current merchant agreement, or must it amend notice, cure, and appeal terms and align thresholds with network program rules before rollout?
## Open questions
- What are the current operative Visa (VAMP/VFMP) and Mastercard (ECM/HECM) thresholds and timelines as passed through by Mosaic Relay's acquiring banks, and do the internal thresholds sit below them with enough buffer?
- Does the merchant agreement's suspension clause require amendment (or a policy incorporated by reference) to support defined notice content, a 30-day cure, and a written appeal process?
- How are successfully rebutted friendly-fraud disputes, pending authorizations, and consumer-law complaints counted in the metric, and is that counting defensible if a merchant challenges deactivation?
- What post-deactivation obligations run to cardholders (refunds, dispute responses within the 90-day window) and to networks/acquirers (closure reporting, MATCH or equivalent listing), and who funds residual chargeback exposure?
- Can marketplace customers contractually override a deactivation, and does any consumer-facing communication duty arise when a merchant with recurring services is deactivated?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The internal search surfaced only this matter's own prior research packet drafts/finals, which are not authority and are not relied on here. The following is model-only analysis synthesized with matter facts; treat every network-rule statement as needing verification against current program guides.
**Card-network monitoring programs (the controlling external constraint).** Visa and Mastercard each operate merchant chargeback monitoring regimes that identify merchants exceeding dispute-rate and dispute-count thresholds, impose escalating consequences (warnings, remediation requirements, fines assessed through the acquirer, and ultimately acquirer-level pressure to terminate), and historically include programs such as Visa's fraud/chargeback monitoring (VFMP/VDMP, now consolidated under VAMP) and Mastercard's Excessive Chargeback Merchant (ECM) and High Excessive Chargeback Merchant (HECM) programs. Key structural points that shape the design:
- Thresholds are typically expressed as a ratio (disputes to transactions) plus a minimum dispute count, measured monthly, with "early warning" tiers below the enforcement tier. Mosaic Relay's internal trigger should sit at or below the networks' early-warning levels so merchants are remediated before network identification, because network-identified merchants can generate per-month fines and acquirer scrutiny of the whole portfolio.
- Networks generally count disputes at the point a chargeback is initiated, not after representment resolves — so "exclude successfully rebutted friendly fraud" is defensible as an internal fairness adjustment but will not match how networks count. The intake answer leaning toward both network thresholds and an internal threshold is workable only if the internal metric is at least as conservative as the network count.
- Acquirers pass program obligations through contract. The operative requirements for Mosaic Relay are in its acquiring-bank agreements and the network rules incorporated there, not in the network rules directly.
**Contract authority and process.** The merchant agreement permits suspension for excessive disputes but is silent on notice content, cure, and appeal. A deactivation program that adds a defined 30-day cure and written appeal is more process than the contract currently promises — that is generally safe (giving more process than required rarely breaches), but the program documents must not create a contractual amendment by accident. The cleaner path is a published risk/acceptable-use policy incorporated by reference, plus an amendment or notice mechanism for existing merchants. Deactivation decisions should be documented, consistently applied, and reviewable, because arbitrary or pretextual termination is the main litigation exposure (breach, tortious interference with the merchant's customer relationships, and in some states unfair-practices theories).
**Post-deactivation obligations.** The 90-day window for refunds and dispute responses aligns with the practical reality that chargebacks arrive weeks to months after the underlying transaction (card network dispute windows commonly run 90–120 days from transaction or expected delivery date, longer for some recurring/service categories). Issues to frame:
- Mosaic Relay (and its acquirer) remains exposed to incoming chargebacks on pre-termination transactions after deactivation; the merchant agreement's reserve/hold rights should be exercised to cover trailing liability, and the 90-day access window should be described as a minimum, with discretion to extend for late-arriving disputes.
- Recurring-service merchants raise a specific problem: deactivating processing mid-subscription can trigger a wave of "services not provided" disputes and consumer complaints. The program should require a wind-down plan for recurring merchants (notify subscribers, stop future billing before cutoff).
- MATCH (Mastercard's terminated-merchant database) listing: acquirers typically must list merchants terminated for cause, including excessive chargebacks. A network-program-driven deactivation may therefore land the merchant on MATCH, which is a significant consequence the termination notice should disclose.
**Reporting and communications.** Expect acquirer-level reporting duties when a merchant enters or exits a network monitoring program and upon termination for cause. Direct consumer communication is generally the merchant's duty, not Mosaic Relay's, but marketplace customers may need notice rights under their platform agreements so they can manage their own sellers. The intake answer (marketplace override only with documented exception approval) is sound; the exception process should be written, time-limited, and logged to keep the "consistent process" fact true.
## Viable paths
1. **Launch with policy-layer process (fastest).** Publish a chargeback-monitoring policy incorporated into the merchant agreement by reference: internal threshold set below network early-warning levels, month-one warning, remediation plan, 30-day cure with written appeal, month-three deactivation, 90-day post-termination access, MATCH disclosure in the termination notice. Amend the agreement's suspension clause at next renewal cycle. Risk: existing merchants may argue the policy conflicts with the silent agreement; mitigate with advance notice and a grace period.
2. **Amend first, launch second (cleanest).** Amend the merchant agreement to define notice content, cure, appeal, and post-termination obligations before any deactivation. Slower, but eliminates the contract-gap argument and lets Mosaic Relay add express MATCH-listing consent and reserve rights for trailing chargebacks.
3. **Pilot on new merchants only.** Apply the full program to new merchants under updated terms while running warnings-only for legacy merchants. Lowest dispute risk; weakest portfolio-cleanup effect.
## Facts that could change the answer
- The actual thresholds and counting rules in the current Visa/Mastercard program guides and acquirer pass-through terms (could force the internal threshold up or down).
- Whether the merchant agreement's amendment clause permits unilateral policy updates (determines whether Path 1 is available).
- Whether any merchants are in the EU/UK or sell to consumers there (consumer-protection and payment-services rules could add notice or complaint-handling duties; intake assumed U.S.-first).
- Whether consumer-law complaints (e.g., Reg E/Reg Z disputes routed as complaints rather than chargebacks) sit inside or outside the metric — affects both fairness and network alignment.
- Whether acquiring banks impose their own earlier termination triggers that would override the three-month design.
