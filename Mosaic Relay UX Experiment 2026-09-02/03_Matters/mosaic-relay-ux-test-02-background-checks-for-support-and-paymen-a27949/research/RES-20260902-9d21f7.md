# What are the FCRA pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notice requirements for

## Question

What are the FCRA pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notice requirements for consumer-report-based employment decisions?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a fictional payment-infrastructure company, plans to require background checks for employees and contractors who can view payment data, change payout controls, approve refunds, or investigate disputes. The program would collect consent, send identifying information to a background-check vendor, and block system access until checks complete, with results categorized as clear, review, or ineligible. Counsel is involved because the company has staff in California, New York, Texas, Ireland, and the UK, and needs to know which checks are lawful, what notices and authorizations are required, and how to handle disputes. Checks would occur before access is granted, every three years, and after certain role changes.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay lawfully run criminal-record, credit, sanctions, identity, and employment-history checks on staff in CA, NY, TX, Ireland, and the UK who handle payment data or payout controls, and if so, what specific notices, consents, lookback limits, and adverse-action steps must the company follow before blocking access or re-categorizing a worker as ineligible?
## Open questions
- Which of the vendor's five search types (criminal, identity, sanctions, credit, employment history) does the business actually intend to run for each role category?
- Do contractor agencies already perform background checks, and will Mosaic Relay rely on those or run its own?
- What lookback periods does the business want for each search type, and are those periods lawful in each jurisdiction?
- What retention and deletion periods will apply to raw reports, decision records, and audit logs?
- Is the three-year recheck cadence and role-change recheck a business preference, and has it been validated against legal limits in each jurisdiction?

## Likely rules and issues
### United States — FCRA framework (CA, NY, TX)
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-generated analysis based on general knowledge of US employment-screening law.
The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq., governs any background check run by a third-party consumer reporting agency (CRA) for employment purposes. The matter record already assumes the vendor is a CRA subject to FCRA (assumption the internal record).
**Pre-adverse-action requirements (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A)):**
- Before taking any adverse action (including denying access, reassigning, suspending, or terminating) based in whole or in part on a consumer report, the employer must provide the individual with:
1. A copy of the consumer report.
2. A copy of the CFPB's "Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act" document.
- This must happen **before** the adverse action is finalized. The purpose is to give the individual a meaningful opportunity to review the report and dispute inaccuracies.
- There is no federally mandated minimum waiting period between pre-adverse-action notice and final adverse action, but FTC guidance and case law generally treat 5 business days as a reasonable floor. Some state and local laws impose longer waits (see below).
**Adverse-action requirements (15 U.S.C. § 1681m(a)):**
- After taking the adverse action, the employer must provide:
1. Notice of the adverse action.
2. The name, address, and phone number of the CRA that supplied the report.
3. A statement that the CRA did not make the decision and cannot explain why it was made.
4. Notice of the individual's right to obtain a free copy of the report from the CRA within 60 days.
5. Notice of the individual's right to dispute the accuracy or completeness of the report with the CRA.
**Standalone disclosure and authorization (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(A)):**
- Before procuring the report, the employer must provide a clear and conspicuous standalone disclosure that a consumer report may be obtained for employment purposes, and must obtain written authorization. The disclosure cannot be buried in an employment application or combined with extraneous liability waivers.
**State and local overlays:**
- **California:** The Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (ICRAA, Cal. Civ. Code § 1786 et seq.) and the Consumer Credit Reporting Agencies Act (CCRAA, Cal. Civ. Code § 1785 et seq.) impose additional requirements. California's Fair Chance Act (Cal. Gov. Code § 12952) prohibits employers with 5+ employees from asking about criminal history before a conditional offer of employment, requires an individualized assessment before rescinding based on criminal history, and mandates specific pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notices with at least 5 business days for the individual to respond. California also restricts credit checks for most positions under Cal. Lab. Code § 1024.5, permitting them only for certain roles (managerial, law enforcement, positions with access to confidential financial information, etc.). Whether payment-operations roles qualify for the credit-check exemption is a fact-specific question.
- **New York:** The New York Fair Chance Act (NYC Admin. Code § 8-107(11-a)) applies to employers in New York City and requires a similar conditional-offer-first structure, individualized assessment using the Article 23-A factors, and a 5-business-day response window. New York State's Stop Credit Discrimination in Employment Act (N.Y. Lab. Law § 740-b) restricts credit checks for most positions, with exemptions for certain roles involving financial responsibility. Whether payment-operations roles qualify is again fact-specific.
- **Texas:** Texas has no state-level ban-the-box law for private employers and no state restriction on credit checks beyond FCRA. Federal FCRA requirements apply without additional state overlay for private employers.
### Ireland and United Kingdom — GDPR / UK GDPR
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-generated analysis.
- Criminal-record data is "special category" data under GDPR Art. 9 / UK GDPR Art. 9, requiring a lawful basis under Art. 6 plus an Art. 9 condition (most likely explicit consent or substantial public interest, depending on the jurisdiction and role).
- In Ireland, the Data Protection Act 2018 implements GDPR. The Data Protection Commission has issued guidance indicating that criminal-record checks should be proportionate and limited to roles where they are genuinely necessary.
- In the UK, the Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR apply. The ICO has published guidance on criminal-offence data in employment. Basic DBS checks are available for any role; standard and enhanced checks are restricted to eligible roles. For a payment-infrastructure company, basic checks are likely the appropriate level for most roles.
- Both jurisdictions require transparency (privacy notices), purpose limitation, data minimization, and retention limits. The proposed three-year recheck cadence and indefinite retention of raw reports would need justification.
- Consent for background checks in the employment context is problematic under GDPR because of the imbalance of power; reliance on legitimate interests or legal obligation is generally preferred where available, but criminal-record data still requires an Art. 9 condition.
### Cross-cutting issues
- **One global policy with carve-outs:** The matter record confirms the business wants a global framework with jurisdiction-specific carve-outs where law requires (the internal record). This is the right approach. The US FCRA process, California and New York fair-chance requirements, and EU/UK data-protection requirements are sufficiently different that a single uniform process would either over-comply in some jurisdictions (creating unnecessary cost and delay) or under-comply in others (creating legal risk).
- **Credit checks:** These are the most legally restricted search type. California and New York both restrict credit checks for employment. Ireland and the UK do not have specific statutory bans, but credit checks must still satisfy GDPR proportionality. The business should justify why credit checks are necessary for each role category.
- **Managers seeing reports:** The business does not want managers to see detailed reports (the internal record). This is consistent with data-minimization principles and FCRA's confidentiality expectations. Access should be limited to HR, Legal, and Security on a need-to-know basis.
- **Blocking access before check completion:** This is operationally straightforward for new hires but more complex for current employees and contractors. For current staff, blocking access before a recheck could constitute an adverse action if it effectively suspends the person from their duties, triggering FCRA pre-adverse-action requirements.
## Viable paths
1. **Tiered global framework:** Adopt a global policy that sets the baseline (consent, vendor use, access blocking, categorization) with jurisdiction-specific annexes for CA, NY, TX, Ireland, and UK. Each annex specifies which search types are permitted, lookback periods, notice requirements, waiting periods, and retention rules. This is the most practical path and aligns with the business's stated preference.
2. **Role-based search matrix:** Map each of the four access categories (view payment data, change payout controls, approve refunds, investigate disputes) to the minimum set of searches that are lawful and necessary. For example, sanctions screening may be justified for all four categories given the payment-infrastructure context, while credit checks may only be justifiable for roles that change payout controls or approve refunds above a threshold.
3. **Phased rollout:** Start with new hires and access grants (where the conditional-offer framework in CA and NY is most naturally applied), then extend to current staff rechecks with appropriate notice and transition periods.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Which search types the business selects:** If credit checks are dropped, the CA and NY credit-check restrictions become moot. If criminal-record checks are included, fair-chance and GDPR special-category requirements apply.
- **Whether contractor agencies already run checks:** If agencies perform equivalent checks, Mosaic Relay may be able to rely on those (with appropriate contractual safeguards) rather than running duplicative checks, reducing cost and legal exposure.
- **Lookback periods:** Longer lookback periods increase the risk of picking up stale or legally protected information (e.g., convictions older than 7 years in California under ICRAA, or spent convictions under UK rehabilitation-of-offenders law).
- **Whether any roles are legally required to be screened:** If any payment-regulation or card-network rule mandates screening for specific roles, that provides a stronger legal basis and may affect the necessity analysis.
- **Whether the vendor is actually a CRA:** If the vendor is not a CRA under FCRA, different rules apply. The matter record assumes CRA status but this should be confirmed.

2. **Run a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction search-type matrix** with employment counsel in CA, NY, TX, Ireland, and the UK to confirm which searches are permissible for each role category and what lookback limits apply.

5. **Prepare a GDPR/UK GDPR data-protection impact assessment (DPIA)** for the EU/UK processing, covering lawful basis, Art. 9 condition, minimization, retention, and data-subject rights.
6. **Decide on the contractor-agency reliance question** and, if relying on agency checks, ensure the agency contract includes appropriate screening standards, audit rights, and indemnification.
7. **Define retention and deletion schedules** for raw reports, decision records, and audit logs, with jurisdiction-specific limits.
