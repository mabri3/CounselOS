      ## Matter summary

      Mosaic Relay, a fictional B2B payment-infrastructure provider, plans to require
      background checks for employees and contractors who can view payment data, change
      payout controls, approve refunds, or investigate disputes. The program would
      collect consent, send identifying information to a vendor, and block system
      access until checks are complete, with results categorized as clear, review,
      or ineligible. Counsel is involved because the company operates in California,
      New York, Texas, Ireland, and the UK, and the proposed checks implicate divergent
      rules on criminal history, credit reports, data privacy, and adverse action.
      The business wants to launch a single global policy with checks recurring every
      three years, but has not yet selected which of the vendor’s five search types
      (criminal-record, identity, sanctions, credit, employment-history) to run.

      ## Decision question

      Which specific background checks (identity, sanctions, criminal-record, credit,
      employment-history) can Mosaic Relay lawfully require for each of the four access
      categories (view payment data, change payout controls, approve refunds, investigate
      disputes) across California, New York, Texas, Ireland, and the UK, and what
      notices, review standards, and dispute processes must accompany them before
      access is blocked?

      ## Open questions

      - Which specific searches does the business intend to run for each role, and
      what lookback periods are intended for each search type?

      - Do contractor agencies already perform equivalent background checks, and will
      Mosaic Relay rely on those or run its own?

      - What are the exact technical permissions and data-access levels for each of
      the four stated categories (to validate whether criminal-record or credit checks
      are proportionate to the risk)?

      - What retention and deletion periods will apply to raw reports, decision records,
      and audit logs in each jurisdiction?

      - Has the vendor confirmed it will supply FCRA-compliant notices and adverse-action
      letters for US-based individuals?

      ## Likely rules and issues

      **United States (Federal/FCRA):** The Fair Credit Reporting Act governs background
      checks performed by third-party vendors. It requires standalone disclosure and
      written authorization before procurement, and a two-step adverse-action process
      (pre-adverse notice with report copy and Summary of Rights, followed by adverse-action
      notice after a reasonable waiting period) if access is denied based on the report.
      Blanket “ineligible” categorizations without individualized review create disparate-impact
      risk under EEOC guidance.

      **California:** The Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (ICRAA) and
      Consumer Credit Reporting Agencies Act (CCRAA) impose stricter standards than
      FCRA, including specific notice content and a checkbox on the authorization
      form for investigative reports. Credit checks are prohibited for most positions
      except specific managerial or financial-institution roles (Labor Code § 1024.5).
      “Ban-the-box” rules (Fair Chance Act) delay criminal-history inquiries until
      after a conditional offer for applicants; for current employees, checks must
      be job-related and consistent with business necessity.

      **New York:** The Fair Credit Reporting Act (Article 25) and Stop Credit Discrimination
      in Employment Act (SCDEA) restrict credit checks to positions with specific
      fiduciary or security duties. The New York City Fair Chance Act (if applicable)
      imposes additional procedural requirements. Criminal-history checks require
      individualized assessment under Article 23-A of the Correction Law, considering
      factors like the nature of the crime, time elapsed, and job relatedness.

      **Texas:** No state “ban-the-box” law for private employers, but FCRA applies.
      Texas Business & Commerce Code § 20.05 mirrors FCRA but allows certain additional
      disclosures. Credit checks are generally permissible but subject to FCRA adverse-action
      rules.

      **Ireland:** The Data Protection Act 2018 and GDPR require a lawful basis for
      processing criminal-record data (Article 10 GDPR), which generally requires
      explicit consent or a legal obligation. The National Vetting Bureau Act 2012
      restricts vetting to specific roles (primarily those involving children or vulnerable
      adults); general employment vetting is not permitted through the NVB, though
      private background checks are possible subject to strict proportionality and
      data-minimization requirements. Credit checks are uncommon and likely disproportionate
      for non-financial roles.

      **United Kingdom:** The Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974 and GDPR/UK GDPR
      limit criminal-record checks. Basic DBS checks are available for any role, but
      Standard or Enhanced checks are restricted to specific exempted roles (e.g.,
      working with vulnerable populations). For general payment-operations roles,
      only Basic checks are likely lawful. Credit checks require careful justification
      under the UK GDPR’s legitimate-interests or consent bases and are restricted
      by the Financial Services and Markets Act for certain regulated roles.

      **Cross-cutting issues:**

      - **Proportionality:** Criminal-record and credit checks are likely disproportionate
      for “view payment data” roles unless access includes ability to modify account
      settings or initiate transfers.

      - **Data minimization:** Blocking access until checks are complete may violate
      GDPR principles if less intrusive means (e.g., supervised access, limited permissions)
      are available.

      - **Contractors:** If agencies already perform checks, Mosaic Relay may be able
      to rely on those under a “joint controller” or “processor” agreement, but must
      verify the scope and recency of the agency checks.

      ## Viable paths

      **Path A: Global baseline with local carve-outs (Recommended)**

      - **Baseline (all jurisdictions):** Identity verification and sanctions screening
      for all four access categories. These are generally lawful globally and proportionate
      to the security risk.

      - **Criminal-record checks:** Add only for roles with “change payout controls”
      or “approve refunds” authority, and only after a documented role-and-permission
      matrix shows necessity. Use Basic DBS (UK), private vetting (Ireland), and state-compliant
      criminal checks (US) with individualized review.

      - **Credit checks:** Exclude from the default bundle. Revisit only for specific
      roles with documented financial fiduciary duties, and only after separate CA/NY
      legal review.

      - **Employment-history:** Verify only where necessary to confirm experience
      for sensitive roles; avoid blanket verification for all access levels.

      **Path B: Uniform global policy (Not recommended)**

      - Apply the same five-search bundle globally. This violates CA/NY credit-check
      restrictions, UK/Irish proportionality rules, and likely GDPR data-minimization
      principles. High risk of regulatory enforcement and discrimination claims.

      **Path C: Role-based tiering**

      - **Tier 1 (View only):** Identity + sanctions only.

      - **Tier 2 (Change/Approve/Investigate):** Identity + sanctions + criminal-record
      (where lawful) + employment-history.

      - **Tier 3 (Financial authority):** Add credit check only if legally permissible
      and documented as necessary.

      ## Facts that could change the answer

      - **Contractor agency practices:** If agencies already perform FCRA-compliant
      or equivalent checks, Mosaic Relay may avoid duplicative vetting by relying
      on agency certifications, reducing GDPR and FCRA exposure.

      - **Specific technical permissions:** If “view payment data” includes only masked
      or read-only access without ability to export or modify, criminal-record checks
      may be disproportionate even in the US.

      requiring shorter periods.

      avoid continuous-monitoring violations in some jurisdictions.
