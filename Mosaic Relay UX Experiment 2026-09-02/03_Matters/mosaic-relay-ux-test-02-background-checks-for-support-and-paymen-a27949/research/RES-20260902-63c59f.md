# What are the GDPR/UK GDPR and Irish data-protection requirements for

## Question

What are the GDPR/UK GDPR and Irish data-protection requirements for processing criminal-record data in employment screening?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment-infrastructure provider, plans to require background checks for employees and contractors who can view payment data, change payout controls, approve refunds, or investigate disputes. The program would collect consent, send identifying information to a vendor, and block access until checks complete, with results categorized as clear, review, or ineligible. Counsel is involved because the company operates in California, New York, Texas, Ireland, and the United Kingdom, and the business wants one global policy despite varying local rules on criminal-record data, credit checks, and privacy. Checks would occur before access and every three years, with additional checks after role changes.
## Decision question
Which of the vendor's five search types (criminal-record, identity, sanctions, credit, employment-history) can Mosaic Relay lawfully run for each of the four access categories across California, New York, Texas, Ireland, and the United Kingdom, and what notices, review standards, and dispute processes must accompany them before the program launches?
## Open questions
- Which specific searches does the business intend to run for each role, and what lookback periods are planned?
- Do contractor agencies already perform background checks, and will Mosaic Relay rely on those or run its own?
- What retention and deletion periods will apply to raw reports, decision records, and audit logs?
- What are the exact local notice, consent, and adverse-action requirements in each jurisdiction, especially for criminal-record and credit data?
- Which roles or access levels legally require screening, and what is the documented business necessity for each search type?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on the matter file and general legal principles, the following rules and issues are likely material:
### GDPR / UK GDPR / Irish data-protection requirements for criminal-record data
Processing criminal-record data in employment screening is heavily restricted under GDPR and UK GDPR. Criminal-conviction data is "special category" data under Article 10 GDPR, requiring both a lawful basis under Article 6 and an additional condition under Article 10. In the employment context, the most likely lawful bases are legal obligation (where screening is required by law) or substantial public interest (where authorized by member-state law). Consent is generally not considered freely given in employment relationships due to power imbalance and is therefore unreliable.
Ireland's Data Protection Act 2018 implements GDPR and adds specific conditions for processing criminal-record data, including requirements for safeguards and, in some cases, ministerial regulations. The UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 similarly restrict criminal-record checks to roles where they are legally required or authorized, with strict necessity and proportionality tests.
Key obligations include:
- **Lawful basis and condition**: Identify and document the specific Article 6 basis and Article 10 condition for each role and jurisdiction.
- **Necessity and proportionality**: Demonstrate that criminal-record checks are necessary for the specific role and that no less intrusive means exist.
- **Data minimization**: Collect only the minimum data needed; avoid blanket checks across all roles.
- **Transparency**: Provide clear privacy notices explaining what data is collected, why, how it is used, retention periods, and data-subject rights.
- **Security and access controls**: Limit access to criminal-record data to those with a strict need to know; managers should not see detailed reports.
- **Retention and deletion**: Define short retention periods tied to the purpose; delete or anonymize data once no longer needed.
- **Data-subject rights**: Honor rights of access, rectification, erasure, restriction, and objection, subject to legal limitations.
- **Cross-border transfers**: If data is transferred outside the EEA/UK, ensure appropriate safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses, adequacy decisions).
- **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)**: Likely required given the systematic processing of special-category data and access-control decisions.
### US state-law variation
California, New York, and Texas have distinct rules:
- **California**: Ban-the-box (Fair Chance Act) restricts criminal-history inquiries before a conditional offer; credit checks are heavily restricted under Labor Code § 1024.5; the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA) impose notice and data-minimization duties.
- **New York**: New York City Fair Chance Act and New York State Human Rights Law restrict criminal-history inquiries; credit checks are restricted under New York Labor Law and NYC Stop Credit Discrimination in Employment Act.
- **Texas**: Generally more permissive but still subject to FCRA and general anti-discrimination laws.
### FCRA adverse-action and notice obligations
For US-based individuals, the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) requires:
- Standalone disclosure and written authorization before obtaining a consumer report.
- Pre-adverse-action notice with a copy of the report and the CFPB Summary of Rights before taking adverse action.
- Adverse-action notice after the decision, including the CRA's contact information and dispute rights.
- Reasonable procedures to ensure accuracy and allow disputes.
### Credit checks
Credit checks are restricted in California and New York and are generally not necessary for the stated roles unless there is a documented, role-specific business reason (e.g., fiduciary responsibility). They should be excluded from the default bundle.
### Retention and minimization
Sensitive background-check data should be retained only as long as necessary for the access decision, any dispute window, and legal audit requirements. Separate deletion rules should apply to raw reports, decision records, and audit logs.

## Viable paths
1. **Global framework with jurisdiction-specific carve-outs** (recommended in the matter file): Adopt a baseline of identity verification and sanctions screening for all four access categories, then add criminal-record and employment-history checks only where a role-and-permission matrix shows clear, proportionate need. Exclude credit checks by default. Use centralized HR/Security/Legal review; managers receive status only.
2. **Uniform minimal bundle**: Run only identity and sanctions checks globally, deferring criminal-record, credit, and employment-history checks until local-law review is complete. This minimizes immediate legal risk but may not meet business or regulatory expectations for high-risk roles.
3. **Jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction rollout**: Launch first in Texas (least restrictive), then California, New York, Ireland, and the UK after completing local notices, lawful-basis documentation, DPIAs, and adverse-action workflows. This delays global launch but reduces compliance risk.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Which searches the business selects**: If credit checks are dropped, California and New York compliance becomes simpler. If criminal-record checks are included, GDPR/UK GDPR Article 10 analysis and DPIAs become critical.
- **Contractor-agency practice**: If agencies already perform equivalent checks, Mosaic Relay may rely on those (with contractual assurances) rather than running duplicate checks, reducing data-processing burden.
- **Lookback periods**: Shorter lookback periods (e.g., 3–5 years) are more defensible under proportionality and minimization principles than longer or indefinite periods.
- **Retention periods**: Shorter retention (e.g., 90 days for raw reports, 1–2 years for decision records) reduces GDPR/UK GDPR risk.
- **Specific role definitions**: If "view payment data" includes only read-only access to aggregated reports, criminal-record checks may be harder to justify than for roles that can unilaterally change payout controls or approve large refunds.
- **Local legal requirements**: If any jurisdiction legally mandates screening for certain roles (e.g., financial-services regulations), the lawful basis shifts from legitimate interests to legal obligation, simplifying GDPR analysis but adding sector-specific rules.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, counsel should:
1. **Verify current primary authority** for each jurisdiction: GDPR Article 10 and member-state implementations (Ireland Data Protection Act 2018), UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018, FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.), California Fair Chance Act and Labor Code § 1024.5, New York Labor Law and NYC Fair Chance Act, and any relevant financial-services regulations.
2. **Complete a DPIA** for the EU/UK processing, documenting lawful basis, necessity, proportionality, safeguards, and data-subject rights.
3. **Draft or review standalone notices and authorizations** for each jurisdiction, ensuring FCRA-compliant disclosure and GDPR-compliant privacy notices.
4. **Confirm the vendor's FCRA compliance** and data-processing agreements, including cross-border transfer safeguards (SCCs, UK addendum).
5. **Define the role-and-permission matrix** with business and security teams to document which roles require which checks and why.
6. **Establish the adverse-action and dispute workflow**, including pre-adverse-action notices, dispute windows, and correction processes.
7. **Set retention and deletion schedules** for raw reports, decision records, and audit logs, with automated deletion where possible.
8. **Train HR, Security, and managers** on the centralized review process and the prohibition on managers viewing detailed reports.
