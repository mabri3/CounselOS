      every three years thereafter.

      ## Decision question

      Which of the vendor''s five search types (criminal-record, identity, sanctions,
      credit, employment-history) can Mosaic Relay lawfully run for each of the four
      access categories in California, New York, and Texas, and what notices, authorizations,
      and adverse-action steps must precede any decision to block access or take adverse
      action?

      ## Open questions

      - Which specific roles or access levels legally require screening versus merely
      benefit from it?

      - What lookback periods does the business intend for each search type, and are
      those periods lawful under each jurisdiction''s limits?

      - Do contractor agencies already perform equivalent checks, and will Mosaic
      Relay rely on those or run its own?

      - What retention and deletion periods will apply to raw reports, decision records,
      and audit logs?

      - Is the three-year recheck cadence a business preference or a legal requirement,
      and does it comply with jurisdiction-specific rules?

      ## Likely rules and issues

      ### California

      **Criminal-history checks.** California''s Fair Chance Act (Gov. Code § 12952)
      prohibits employers with five or more employees from asking about criminal history
      before a conditional offer of employment. After a conditional offer, employers
      may conduct a criminal-history check, but they must make an individualized assessment
      before rescinding the offer or taking adverse action. The assessment must consider
      the nature and gravity of the offense, the time elapsed, and the nature of the
      job. Employers must provide written notice of the disqualifying conviction,
      a copy of the report, and at least five business days to respond before making
      a final decision. Certain convictions (e.g., arrests not leading to conviction,
      sealed or expunged records, marijuana convictions older than two years) cannot
      be considered at all under Labor Code § 432.7 and related provisions.

      **Credit checks.** California Labor Code § 1024.5 severely restricts the use
      of consumer credit reports for employment purposes. Employers may only use credit
      reports for specific positions: managerial positions, positions in the state
      Department of Justice, sworn peace officers, positions for which the information
      is required by law, positions involving regular access to bank or credit-card
      information, positions that are signatories on the employer''s bank account,
      positions involving access to confidential or proprietary information, and positions
      involving regular access to $10,000 or more in cash. Even when permitted, the
      employer must provide written notice identifying the specific basis for the
      credit check and obtain written consent. The notice must also inform the individual
      of the source of the report and their right to obtain a free copy.

      **Ban-the-box and local rules.** Several California cities (San Francisco, Los
      Angeles, Richmond, Oakland, Berkeley, Compton, East Palo Alto) have local ban-the-box
      ordinances that may impose additional restrictions or procedural requirements
      beyond state law.

      ### New York

      **Criminal-history checks.** New York State''s "ban-the-box" law (Executive
      Law § 296(16)) prohibits employers from asking about criminal history on initial
      applications. New York City''s Fair Chance Act (Admin. Code § 8-107(11-a)) goes
      further: employers cannot inquire about criminal history until after a conditional
      offer of employment. After a conditional offer, employers must conduct an individualized
      assessment using the factors in Correction Law Article 23-A (nature of the offense,
      time elapsed, age at offense, rehabilitation, job-relatedness, etc.). Employers
      must provide written notice of the disqualifying conviction, a copy of the report,
      and at least three business days to respond before taking adverse action. New
      York law also prohibits consideration of sealed convictions, youthful offender
      adjudications, and certain older convictions.

      **Credit checks.** New York City''s Stop Credit Discrimination in Employment
      Act (Admin. Code § 8-107(24)) prohibits employers from using consumer credit
      history for employment decisions, with narrow exceptions for positions requiring
      security clearance, positions with signatory authority over $10,000 or more,
      positions involving regular access to trade secrets or national security information,
      and certain law-enforcement and financial-regulatory roles. New York State law
      (Gen. Bus. Law § 380-j) requires employers to provide notice and obtain consent
      before obtaining a credit report, but does not broadly prohibit credit checks.

      **Salary history.** New York State and New York City both prohibit asking about
      salary history during the hiring process, which may affect employment-history
      verification procedures.

      ### Texas

      **Criminal-history checks.** Texas has no statewide ban-the-box law for private
      employers. Employers may ask about criminal history at any stage and may consider
      any conviction, subject to federal anti-discrimination law (Title VII disparate-impact
      analysis) and the FCRA. Texas law (Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 142.001 et seq.)
      provides a negligent-hiring safe harbor for employers who conduct criminal-background
      checks that meet certain statutory requirements, but it does not mandate checks.

      **Credit checks.** Texas has no state law restricting the use of credit reports
      for employment purposes beyond the federal FCRA. Employers must comply with
      FCRA disclosure, authorization, and adverse-action requirements, but Texas does
      not impose additional substantive limits.

      **Local rules.** Austin has a ban-the-box ordinance for city contractors, but
      it does not apply to private employers generally. No other major Texas city
      has adopted a broad ban-the-box law for private employers.

      ### Federal overlay (all US jurisdictions)

      **FCRA.** The Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) applies to
      all background checks conducted by a consumer reporting agency. Before obtaining
      a report, the employer must provide a clear and conspicuous standalone disclosure
      and obtain written authorization. Before taking adverse action based on the
      report, the employer must provide a pre-adverse-action notice, a copy of the
      report, and the CFPB''s Summary of Rights. After taking adverse action, the
      employer must provide an adverse-action notice identifying the CRA, stating
      that the CRA did not make the decision, and informing the individual of their
      right to dispute and obtain a free copy of the report within 60 days.

      **Title VII.** The EEOC''s 2012 Enforcement Guidance on criminal records explains
      that blanket exclusions based on criminal history may create disparate impact
      and violate Title VII. Employers should conduct an individualized assessment
      considering the nature of the crime, time elapsed, and nature of the job.

      ## Viable paths

      **Path 1: Tiered global policy with jurisdiction-specific carve-outs.** Adopt
      a global framework that defaults to the most restrictive applicable rule. For
      criminal-history checks, delay all inquiries until after a conditional offer
      (satisfying CA, NY, and NYC), conduct individualized assessments using the most
      protective factors (NY Correction Law Article 23-A), and provide the longest
      response window (CA''s five business days). For credit checks, limit use to
      roles that fall within California''s Labor Code § 1024.5 exceptions and NYC''s
      Stop Credit Discrimination Act exceptions, and provide the most detailed notice
      required by any applicable law. This path minimizes legal risk but may delay
      hiring and limit the information available for certain roles.

      **Path 2: Jurisdiction-specific policies.** Maintain separate policies for California,
      New York, Texas, Ireland, and the UK, each tailored to local law. This allows
      maximum flexibility in Texas (where few restrictions apply) and full compliance
      in California and New York, but increases administrative complexity and the
      risk of inconsistent application.

      **Path 3: Role-based screening matrix.** Map each of the four access categories
      (view payment data, change payout controls, approve refunds, investigate disputes)
      to the specific legal justifications for each search type in each jurisdiction.
      For example, credit checks may be justified for roles with signatory authority
      or regular access to large sums (CA Labor Code § 1024.5(a)(5), (6), (7)), but
      not for roles that merely view payment data. Criminal-history checks may be
      justified for all four categories given the financial-crime risk, but the individualized
      assessment and timing rules still apply. This path requires detailed legal analysis
      but produces the most defensible and tailored program.

      **Path 4: Contractor-agency reliance.** For contractors, rely on the staffing
      agency''s background checks where the agency contractually certifies compliance
      with applicable law and agrees to indemnify Mosaic Relay. This reduces Mosaic
      Relay''s direct FCRA and state-law obligations, but requires careful contract
      drafting and may not be feasible if the business needs to control the scope
      and timing of checks.
