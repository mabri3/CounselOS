# What refund and cancellation obligations apply by product type (digital

## Question

What refund and cancellation obligations apply by product type (digital goods/subscriptions, physical goods, services) across the North America and Europe footprint?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, wants to launch a self-service refund portal for card and ACH payments that would automatically approve refund requests within 30 days for defined reasons and deny requests outside that window. The portal would serve payers, merchants, and recipients across North America and Europe, handling digital goods/subscriptions, physical goods, and services. Counsel is involved because refund and cancellation obligations vary materially by jurisdiction and product type, and the company needs to understand which laws apply before setting eligibility rules and notice wording. A limited pilot is targeted for 60 days, subject to legal review and control testing.
## Decision question
Which refund and cancellation laws by product type (digital goods/subscriptions, physical goods, services) and jurisdiction across North America and Europe must the portal's 30-day auto-approve window, defined-reason eligibility, and denial/escalation wording satisfy before the 60-day pilot launches, and what happens to the pilot timeline if the portal's current design conflicts with mandatory consumer withdrawal or refund rights in key markets?
## Open questions
- Whether Mosaic Relay acts as the merchant's agent (authorized to approve and execute refunds on the merchant's behalf) or as an independent decision-maker, which affects liability allocation and whether Mosaic Relay itself takes on refund obligations.
- Which specific countries/states/provinces within North America and Europe will be included in the initial pilot footprint, since refund and cancellation rules differ materially (e.g., EU Consumer Rights Directive vs. US state laws vs. Canadian provincial laws).
- Whether the portal will handle recurring payments or subscriptions in the pilot, given the answer was "full refunds only" but subscription cancellation rights (e.g., EU 14-day withdrawal, US state auto-renewal laws) may still apply.
- What authentication method will be used to verify payer identity before releasing funds, which affects fraud controls and compliance with payment-services and consumer-protection rules.
- Whether any merchants in the pilot sell into jurisdictions with mandatory refund windows longer than 30 days (e.g., EU 14-day withdrawal plus national extensions, certain US state or Canadian provincial rules), which would require the portal to honor longer windows for those transactions.

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The analysis below is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of consumer-protection frameworks. It is not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal research.
### North America
**United States (federal and state):**
- **No general federal right to a refund** for most consumer purchases. Refund obligations are primarily contractual or based on state law.
- **FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 435):** Requires sellers to ship within the stated time or notify the buyer and offer a refund. Applies to physical goods ordered remotely. Does not mandate a general refund window but creates refund obligations when shipment is delayed.
- **State cooling-off rules:** A few states (e.g., California, New York) have limited cooling-off periods for specific transaction types (e.g., door-to-door sales, health-studio contracts, timeshares). Most do not apply to ordinary online purchases.
- **State auto-renewal laws:** California (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17600 et seq.), New York, and others require clear disclosure, affirmative consent, and easy cancellation for automatically renewing subscriptions. Some states require refunds or prohibit charges after cancellation.
- **State gift-card and unclaimed-property laws:** May affect refund timing and treatment of unused balances.
- **ACH transactions:** NACHA rules govern unauthorized returns (typically 60 days from settlement for consumer accounts under Regulation E, 2 banking days for business accounts). The portal must not interfere with these return rights.
- **Card transactions:** Card-network rules (Visa, Mastercard) allow chargebacks for goods not received, defective goods, or unauthorized transactions. The portal must not imply that a refund request replaces chargeback rights.
**Canada (federal and provincial):**
- **Federal Competition Act:** Prohibits false or misleading representations, including about refund rights.
- **Provincial consumer-protection acts:** Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2002, British Columbia's Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act, and Quebec's Consumer Protection Act provide cooling-off periods for specific contracts (e.g., direct agreements, distance contracts, gym memberships). Ontario provides a 10-day cooling-off period for direct agreements and certain distance contracts. Quebec provides similar rights.
- **Distance-selling rules:** Some provinces require refunds for goods not delivered within a specified period.
### Europe
**European Union (harmonized minimum standards):**
- **Consumer Rights Directive (2011/83/EU):** Provides a **14-day withdrawal right** for distance and off-premises contracts for most goods and services. Consumers can withdraw without giving a reason. Traders must refund within 14 days of withdrawal, including standard delivery costs. For digital content not supplied on a tangible medium, the withdrawal right is lost if performance begins with the consumer's prior express consent and acknowledgment of loss of the right.
- **Digital Content Directive (2019/770):** Provides remedies for defective digital content and services, including repair, replacement, price reduction, or termination with refund. Applies to digital goods/subscriptions.
- **Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC):** Prohibits misleading actions or omissions about refund rights.
- **Payment Services Directive (PSD2, 2015/2366):** Governs refund rights for unauthorized or incorrectly executed payment transactions. Consumers generally have 13 months to report unauthorized transactions. Refunds for authorized transactions are governed by the underlying contract and consumer law, not PSD2.
- **National implementations:** Member states may extend the 14-day withdrawal period or provide additional rights. Some countries (e.g., Germany, France) have specific rules for certain product categories.
**United Kingdom (post-Brexit):**
- **Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013:** Mirror the EU 14-day withdrawal right for distance contracts.
- **Consumer Rights Act 2015:** Provides remedies for faulty goods, digital content, and services, including a short-term right to reject (30 days for goods), repair or replacement, and final right to reject or price reduction. Digital content must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, and as described.
- **Payment Services Regulations 2017:** Similar to PSD2 for unauthorized transactions.
### Key issues for the portal
1. **30-day auto-approve window vs. mandatory withdrawal/refund periods:**
- The EU/UK 14-day withdrawal right is **shorter** than the portal's 30-day window, so the portal's window is more generous and generally compliant for EU/UK distance contracts.
- However, some US state or Canadian provincial rules may provide **longer** windows for specific products (e.g., gym memberships, timeshares, certain subscription services). The portal must honor the longer of the contractual, statutory, or portal window.
- For **digital goods/subscriptions**, the EU withdrawal right is lost once performance begins with consent, but the Digital Content Directive still provides remedies for defective content. The portal's "not as described / defective" reason aligns with this.
2. **Defined reasons for auto-approval:**
- The selected reasons (not as described/defective, did not receive goods/services, cancellation/change of mind, duplicate or incorrect charge) map reasonably well to common refund triggers.
- **Cancellation/change of mind** is a statutory right in the EU/UK for distance contracts (14 days) but not in most of the US. The portal's 30-day window for this reason is more generous than required in the US but must be clearly disclosed as a contractual benefit, not a statutory right, to avoid misleading consumers.
- **Duplicate or incorrect charge** may trigger Regulation E (US ACH/debit) or PSD2 (EU) error-resolution rights, which have their own timelines and processes. The portal must route these appropriately and not delay statutory error-resolution rights.
3. **Denial and escalation:**
- Denying a request outside the 30-day window is generally permissible for contractual refunds, but the portal must not deny statutory rights (e.g., EU 14-day withdrawal, UK 30-day short-term right to reject for faulty goods, US state auto-renewal cancellation rights, Regulation E error claims).
- The working recommendation to route card-network disputes to the issuer/network and ACH unauthorized/error claims to the bank/processor is correct. The portal must clearly state that a refund request does not replace these rights.
- For EU/UK consumers, denying a withdrawal request within 14 days would violate the Consumer Rights Directive/UK Regulations. The portal must distinguish between statutory withdrawal (14 days, no reason needed) and contractual refunds (30 days, defined reasons).
4. **Product-type distinctions:**
- **Digital goods/subscriptions:** EU Digital Content Directive and UK Consumer Rights Act provide specific remedies. Auto-renewal laws in California and other US states require easy cancellation. The portal should allow cancellation of future recurring charges even if a refund for the current period is denied.
- **Physical goods:** FTC Mail Order Rule (US), provincial distance-selling rules (Canada), and EU/UK withdrawal rights apply. Return shipping costs and restocking fees may be regulated.
- **Services:** EU/UK withdrawal right applies but may be lost once service performance begins with consent. Some US states have specific rules for service contracts (e.g., health studios, dating services).
5. **Notice and language requirements:**
- EU/UK require pre-contractual information about withdrawal rights in clear, plain language. Notices must be in the consumer's language in many EU member states.
- US state laws may require specific disclosures for auto-renewal or refund policies.
- WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA accessibility is a good baseline and may be legally required in some jurisdictions (e.g., EU Web Accessibility Directive for public-sector sites, but good practice for private-sector consumer-facing tools).

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Launch pilot with jurisdiction-specific eligibility rules (recommended)
Configure the portal to apply the **longest applicable refund window** for each transaction based on the consumer's location and product type:
- **EU/UK distance contracts:** Honor 14-day no-questions-asked withdrawal (statutory) plus the portal's 30-day defined-reason window (contractual). Clearly label the 14-day right as statutory and the 30-day window as a Mosaic Relay/merchant benefit.
- **US/Canada:** Apply the portal's 30-day window as a contractual benefit, but flag transactions subject to longer statutory windows (e.g., auto-renewal cancellations, specific provincial cooling-off periods) and route those to the merchant or a manual review queue.
- **Digital goods/subscriptions:** Allow cancellation of future recurring charges at any time. For refunds, apply the 30-day window but recognize that EU/UK consumers may have lost the withdrawal right once performance began.
- **Physical goods:** Apply the 30-day window but ensure compliance with FTC Mail Order Rule (US) and provincial delivery rules (Canada).
- **Services:** Apply the 30-day window but recognize that EU/UK withdrawal rights may be lost once service performance begins.
**Pros:** Compliant with mandatory consumer-protection laws; reduces legal risk; maintains consumer trust.
**Cons:** Requires jurisdiction-detection logic and rule configuration; may reduce auto-approval rates for some transactions.
### Path 2: Launch pilot with uniform 30-day window and manual review for statutory rights
Apply the 30-day auto-approve window uniformly but flag all EU/UK transactions and any US/Canadian transactions involving auto-renewals, gym memberships, or other regulated products for manual review to ensure statutory rights are honored.
**Pros:** Simpler initial configuration; faster pilot launch.
**Cons:** Higher operational burden; risk of missing statutory rights if manual review is not robust; may delay outcomes for consumers with statutory rights.
### Path 3: Limit pilot to US-only transactions with no auto-renewals
Launch the pilot only for US transactions involving one-time purchases of physical goods or services, excluding auto-renewals, digital goods, and regulated products.
**Pros:** Simplest legal analysis; lowest risk.
**Cons:** Does not test the portal's ability to handle EU/UK or complex product types; limited business value.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Whether Mosaic Relay acts as the merchant's agent:** If Mosaic Relay is the merchant's agent, it may take on direct liability for refund decisions and must ensure compliance with consumer-protection laws as if it were the merchant. If it is an independent decision-maker, liability allocation may differ, but Mosaic Relay still faces regulatory and reputational risk.
- **Specific pilot jurisdictions:** If the pilot includes only US states with no specific refund laws, the analysis is simpler. If it includes EU member states, the 14-day withdrawal right and Digital Content Directive apply. If it includes Canadian provinces with cooling-off periods, those must be honored.
- **Whether the portal handles recurring payments/subscriptions:** If yes, auto-renewal laws in California, New York, and other states, as well as EU/UK subscription rules, apply. The portal must allow easy cancellation of future charges.
- **Merchant contracts:** If merchant contracts already provide refund windows longer than 30 days, the portal must honor those. If merchant contracts are silent, the portal's 30-day window may become the de facto policy, which could create consumer-protection issues if it is shorter than statutory rights.
- **Whether any merchants sell regulated products:** If merchants sell gym memberships, timeshares, health services, or other products subject to specific cooling-off or refund laws, the portal must apply those rules.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before the 60-day pilot launches, counsel should:
1. **Confirm the pilot footprint:** Identify the specific countries, states, and provinces where the portal will be available. This determines which consumer-protection laws apply.
2. **Map statutory refund/withdrawal windows by jurisdiction and product type:** Create a matrix showing the mandatory refund or withdrawal window for each product type (digital goods/subscriptions, physical goods, services) in each pilot jurisdiction. Identify any windows longer than 30 days.
3. **Review merchant contracts:** Confirm whether merchant contracts specify refund windows or delegate refund authority to Mosaic Relay. If Mosaic Relay is acting as the merchant's agent, confirm that the agency agreement covers refund decisions and allocates liability appropriately.
4. **Configure jurisdiction-detection and rule logic:** Work with product and engineering to ensure the portal can detect the consumer's location and product type and apply the correct refund window and eligibility rules.
5. **Draft consumer-facing notices:** Prepare clear, plain-language notices that distinguish between statutory rights (e.g., EU 14-day withdrawal) and contractual benefits (e.g., 30-day refund window). Ensure notices are available in the consumer's language and meet WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA accessibility standards.
6. **Define escalation and routing rules:** Confirm that the portal routes card-network disputes to the issuer/network, ACH unauthorized/error claims to the bank/processor, and statutory-rights claims to the merchant or a manual review queue. Ensure the portal does not imply that a refund request replaces these rights.
7. **Test fraud and authentication controls:** Confirm that the chosen authentication method (phone/email verification) is sufficient to prevent fraudulent refund requests while not creating unnecessary friction. Consider whether additional controls (e.g., transaction-amount limits, velocity checks) are needed.
8. **Set recordkeeping and audit-trail standards:** Confirm that the portal retains the request, transaction reference, verification method and result, eligibility inputs, reason selected, rule version, decision, timing, refund rail, merchant notice, delivery status, user-facing notice, fraud signals, overrides, and escalation outcome. Set a documented retention schedule by jurisdiction and legal-hold process.
