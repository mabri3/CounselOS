# What ACH return and card-network dispute rules govern routing of

## Question

What ACH return and card-network dispute rules govern routing of refund and unauthorized/error claims?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, is building a self-service refund portal for card and ACH payments that would automatically approve requests within 30 days for defined reasons and deny requests outside that window. The portal would serve payers, merchants, and recipients across North America and Europe, handling digital goods/subscriptions, physical goods, and services. Counsel is involved because the portal must route refund and unauthorized/error claims correctly between ACH return processes and card-network dispute channels without usurping merchant responsibility or violating network rules. A limited pilot is targeted for 60 days, subject to legal review and control testing.
## Decision question
Should Mosaic Relay structure the portal to route ACH unauthorized/error claims to bank/processor return processes and card disputes to issuer/network channels, while limiting auto-approved refunds to merchant-authorized eligible transactions, and what specific controls are required to prevent the portal from being deemed an unauthorized decision-maker or interfering with statutory dispute rights?
## Open questions
- Whether Mosaic Relay will execute refunds as the merchant’s authorized agent under a written agreement, or act as an independent decision-maker (which increases regulatory and liability exposure).
- Which specific card networks (Visa, Mastercard, etc.) and ACH regimes (Nacha, EBA, etc.) apply to the pilot markets, given the full North America and Europe footprint.
- Whether the 30-day auto-approve window aligns with the shortest applicable consumer cancellation or refund right in the target jurisdictions (e.g., EU 14-day withdrawal, state cooling-off periods).
- What authentication method will verify payer identity before releasing funds, and whether it meets the fraud-control standards required by the card networks and ACH operators.
- How the portal will handle partial refunds, recurring payments, and transactions where the merchant has already initiated a refund or the payer has filed a chargeback.

### Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on the matter facts and general regulatory frameworks governing payment infrastructure, the following rules likely control the routing of claims:
**ACH unauthorized/error claims.** Under Nacha rules (U.S.) and equivalent European frameworks (e.g., SEPA), unauthorized ACH debits and errors must be returned through the bank/processor return process, not through a merchant refund portal. The portal must not imply that a refund request replaces the payer’s right to submit an unauthorized or error claim to their bank. Mosaic Relay’s role is to route these claims to the originating bank or processor within the applicable return window (typically 60 days for unauthorized consumer debits under Nacha, shorter for errors). The portal should capture the claim details and forward them to the bank/processor without auto-approving or denying the underlying refund request.
**Card-network dispute rules.** Visa and Mastercard rules require that disputes (chargebacks) be initiated through the payer’s issuer, not through a third-party portal. The portal must not resolve card-network disputes, as stated in the facts. However, the portal can facilitate merchant refunds that may prevent chargebacks if executed before the payer files a dispute. The key issue is ensuring the portal does not interfere with the payer’s statutory right to dispute a transaction with their issuer. Auto-approved refunds must be processed as merchant refunds, not as dispute resolutions, and must include clear notice that the payer retains chargeback rights.
**Agency and authorization.** If Mosaic Relay acts as the merchant’s agent, it must have explicit written authority to approve and execute refunds on the merchant’s behalf. Without this, Mosaic Relay risks being deemed an independent decision-maker, which could trigger money-transmitter or payment-institution licensing requirements in some jurisdictions and increase liability for refund decisions. The agency agreement must specify the scope of authority, the 30-day window, the defined reasons, and the merchant’s obligation to fund the refunds.
**Consumer protection and cancellation laws.** The 30-day auto-approve window must be validated against the shortest applicable consumer cancellation or refund right in the target markets. For example, the EU Consumer Rights Directive provides a 14-day withdrawal right for distance contracts, which may require a shorter auto-approve window for EU consumers. State cooling-off periods in the U.S. (e.g., 3 days for certain door-to-door sales) may also apply. The portal must not deny a refund request that falls within a statutory cancellation period, even if it falls outside the 30-day window.
**Authentication and fraud controls.** The portal must verify the payer’s identity before releasing funds to prevent fraud and account takeover. The authentication method (e.g., phone/email verification, knowledge-based authentication, or stronger methods like two-factor authentication) must meet the fraud-control standards required by the card networks and ACH operators. Weak authentication could lead to unauthorized refunds, chargebacks, and network fines.
**Recordkeeping and audit trails.** The portal must retain detailed records of each refund request, including the transaction reference, payer verification method and result, eligibility inputs, reason selected, rule version, decision, timing, refund rail, merchant notice, delivery status, user-facing notice, fraud signals, overrides, and escalation outcome. These records must be immutable, access-controlled, and preserved for disputes and regulator requests. Retention schedules must comply with jurisdiction-specific requirements (e.g., GDPR, state data-retention laws).
### Viable paths
**Path 1: Merchant-authorized agent model.** Mosaic Relay acts as the merchant’s authorized agent under a written agreement. The portal auto-approves refunds within the 30-day window for defined reasons, executes the refund on the merchant’s behalf, and routes ACH unauthorized/error claims to the bank/processor and card disputes to the issuer/network. This path minimizes regulatory risk but requires robust agency agreements and merchant onboarding.
**Path 2: Independent decision-maker model.** Mosaic Relay acts as an independent decision-maker, approving or denying refunds based on its own criteria. This path increases regulatory exposure (e.g., money-transmitter licensing) and liability for refund decisions. It is not recommended unless Mosaic Relay obtains the necessary licenses and implements enhanced controls.
**Path 3: Hybrid model.** Mosaic Relay acts as the merchant’s agent for auto-approved refunds and as an independent decision-maker for denied requests, routing them to the merchant or another dispute process. This path balances efficiency and risk but requires clear notice to payers and merchants about the different roles.
### Facts that could change the answer
- **Agency status:** If Mosaic Relay is not the merchant’s authorized agent, the portal cannot auto-approve refunds without triggering licensing and liability issues.
- **Jurisdictional scope:** If the pilot is limited to specific states or EU member states, the applicable refund and cancellation laws may be narrower, allowing for a simpler eligibility framework.
- **Authentication method:** If the portal uses weak authentication (e.g., email verification only), the fraud risk increases, requiring additional controls or limiting the auto-approve window.
- **Partial refunds and recurring payments:** If the portal supports partial refunds or recurring payments, the eligibility and routing analysis becomes more complex, requiring additional controls and notices.
- **Merchant funding:** If merchants do not pre-fund refunds or maintain sufficient balances, Mosaic Relay may need to advance funds, increasing credit risk and requiring additional agreements.
### Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Confirm agency status:** Obtain written confirmation from each merchant that Mosaic Relay is authorized to approve and execute refunds on their behalf, specifying the 30-day window and defined reasons.
2. **Validate jurisdictional requirements:** Map the 30-day auto-approve window against the shortest applicable consumer cancellation or refund right in each target market, and adjust the window or eligibility criteria as needed.
3. **Test authentication controls:** Conduct a fraud-risk assessment of the proposed authentication method and implement additional controls (e.g., two-factor authentication) if necessary.
4. **Review card-network and ACH rules:** Confirm with the card networks and ACH operators that the portal’s routing of unauthorized/error claims and disputes complies with their rules.
5. **Draft consumer notices:** Prepare clear, accessible notices in the payer’s language that explain the refund process, the payer’s statutory rights, and the escalation path for denied requests.
6. **Implement recordkeeping:** Set up an immutable audit trail for all refund requests, decisions, and notices, with access controls and a documented retention schedule.
**Recommendation:** Proceed with Path 1 (merchant-authorized agent model) for the pilot, subject to the last-mile verification steps above. This path minimizes regulatory risk and aligns with Mosaic Relay’s business objective of lowering support volume and giving consumers faster outcomes. The lawyer’s decision should confirm the agency status, jurisdictional requirements, and authentication controls before launch.
