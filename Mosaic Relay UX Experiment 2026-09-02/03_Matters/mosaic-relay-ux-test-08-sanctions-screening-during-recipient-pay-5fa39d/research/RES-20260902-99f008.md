# What are the sanctions screening and blocking/reporting obligations under OFAC

## Question

What are the sanctions screening and blocking/reporting obligations under OFAC and EU sanctions regimes for payment providers?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, wants to screen every payout recipient against sanctions and restricted-party lists at onboarding, before each payout, and when lists update. Potential matches would queue payouts for manual review; confirmed matches would stop the payout and restrict the account. Counsel is involved because the company needs to understand its legal obligations for screening scope, blocking and rejection duties, reporting and record retention, communications to affected parties, and controls to prevent discriminatory treatment. The product team plans to show recipients only that "additional review is required" to reduce evasion risk.
## Decision question
What sanctions screening, blocking, rejection, reporting, and record-keeping obligations apply to Mosaic Relay under OFAC and EU sanctions regimes when it screens payout recipients before releasing funds, and what controls must it implement to ensure consistent, non-discriminatory treatment while managing incomplete recipient data, transliterated names, and cross-border payouts?
## Open questions
- Which specific countries and currencies will the payout screening cover, and which sanctions regimes (OFAC, EU, UN, UK, others) therefore apply?
- Which sanctions and restricted-party lists will be used (e.g., OFAC SDN, EU consolidated list, UN Security Council lists, sectoral sanctions lists)?
- What match-scoring thresholds and escalation deadlines are planned, and how will false positives be corrected?
- How will Mosaic Relay handle funds while a payout is under review or blocked (e.g., hold in suspense, return to sender, block in place)?
- What reporting and record-retention obligations apply to blocked or rejected transactions, and can merchants receive explanations for matches affecting their recipients?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on general knowledge of U.S. and EU sanctions frameworks, the following rules and issues are likely material:
### U.S. OFAC sanctions
**Screening obligations.** Payment providers that are U.S. persons or that process transactions with a U.S. nexus (e.g., U.S. dollar clearing, U.S. correspondent accounts, U.S.-origin goods or services) are generally expected to screen parties to transactions against OFAC's Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List (SDN List) and other restricted-party lists. While OFAC does not mandate a specific screening frequency or methodology for all entities, the expectation is risk-based and proportionate to the nature of the business. Pre-transaction screening is standard practice for payment providers.
**Blocking and rejection duties.** When a match is confirmed:
- **Blocking (freezing):** If the transaction involves property or interests in property of a blocked person (e.g., an SDN), U.S. persons must block the transaction and place the funds in a blocked, interest-bearing account. The funds cannot be released without OFAC authorization.
- **Rejection:** In some cases, transactions must be rejected rather than blocked (e.g., certain sectoral sanctions or where the transaction does not involve blocked property but is otherwise prohibited).
- **Reporting:** Blocked property must be reported to OFAC within 10 business days via a blocked property report. Rejected transactions must also be reported in certain circumstances. Annual reports of blocked property are required.
**Record retention.** OFAC regulations generally require records related to blocked property and rejected transactions to be retained for at least five years.
**Strict liability.** OFAC sanctions operate under a strict liability standard for civil penalties. Even inadvertent violations can result in enforcement action, though OFAC considers the adequacy of a company's compliance program as a mitigating factor.
### EU sanctions
**Screening obligations.** EU sanctions regulations require EU persons and entities, and conduct within the EU, to comply with asset freezes and restrictions on making funds or economic resources available to designated persons. Screening against the EU consolidated list is expected for payment providers operating in the EU or serving EU customers.
**Blocking and rejection duties.** When a match is confirmed:
- **Asset freeze:** Funds and economic resources belonging to, owned, held, or controlled by designated persons must be frozen. No funds or economic resources may be made available, directly or indirectly, to or for the benefit of designated persons.
- **Reporting:** EU member state competent authorities must be notified of frozen funds and any attempts to make funds available to designated persons. Reporting timelines and formats vary by member state.
**Record retention.** EU sanctions regulations generally require records to be retained for at least five years, though specific requirements vary by member state and regulation.
**No strict liability, but robust compliance expected.** EU sanctions violations generally require some degree of knowledge or negligence, but the standard varies by member state. Robust compliance programs are expected and can mitigate penalties.
### Cross-cutting issues
**Tipping off and confidentiality.** Both OFAC and EU regimes include restrictions on disclosing information that could tip off designated persons or undermine enforcement. The product team's plan to show recipients only that "additional review is required" is consistent with this concern, but the exact wording and timing of communications must be carefully managed to avoid both tipping off and unfair treatment.
**False positives and due process.** Screening systems generate false positives, especially with incomplete data and transliterated names. Legal and reputational risks arise if legitimate recipients are consistently delayed or blocked without a clear, accessible correction process. Controls should include:
- Documented match-scoring thresholds and escalation procedures
- Human review of potential matches before blocking
- A documented appeal and correction process for recipients
- Audit logs and consistent evidence standards
- Periodic review of false-positive rates and threshold tuning
**Discrimination and consistency.** Screening and blocking must be applied consistently and not discriminate on the basis of nationality, ethnicity, or other protected characteristics. Controls should include:
- Objective, documented match criteria
- Training for manual reviewers
- Periodic audits of decisions for consistency
- Clear escalation paths for ambiguous cases
**Vendor management.** If Mosaic Relay uses third-party screening vendors, it remains responsible for compliance. Contracts should address list coverage, update frequency, match logic, data security, and audit rights.
**Cross-border complexity.** Different jurisdictions may impose overlapping or conflicting obligations. For example, a payout may be subject to both OFAC and EU sanctions if it involves U.S. dollars and an EU recipient. Mosaic Relay must identify all applicable regimes and ensure compliance with each.
## Viable paths
1. **Risk-based, multi-list screening with documented thresholds.** Implement screening against OFAC SDN, EU consolidated list, and other applicable lists at onboarding, before each payout, and on list updates. Use documented match-scoring thresholds tuned to balance false positives and false negatives. Escalate potential matches to trained compliance staff for manual review before blocking.
2. **Clear blocking and rejection procedures.** When a match is confirmed, block or reject the transaction as required by the applicable regime. Place blocked funds in a segregated, interest-bearing account if required. Document the basis for each decision.
3. **Timely reporting and record retention.** Report blocked property and rejected transactions to OFAC and EU member state authorities as required. Retain records for at least five years or longer if required by specific regulations.
4. **Careful communications.** Use neutral language such as "additional review is required" to avoid tipping off. Do not disclose match details to recipients or merchants unless legally required or permitted. Provide a clear, accessible process for recipients to correct false positives.
5. **Consistency and anti-discrimination controls.** Apply screening and blocking consistently. Train reviewers, audit decisions, and monitor for patterns that could indicate discriminatory treatment. Document all decisions and the evidence relied upon.
6. **Vendor and partner coordination.** Ensure screening vendors and banking partners understand and support Mosaic Relay's compliance obligations. Address list coverage, update frequency, data security, and audit rights in contracts.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Jurisdictions and currencies.** If Mosaic Relay operates only outside the U.S. and EU and does not touch U.S. dollars or EU payment systems, OFAC and EU obligations may not apply, though other regimes (e.g., UK, UN, local) might.
- **Lists used.** If Mosaic Relay screens only against OFAC SDN and not EU lists, or vice versa, its obligations will differ. Sectoral sanctions and other restricted-party lists may impose additional requirements.
- **Match thresholds and escalation.** If thresholds are too low, false positives will increase, raising due process and discrimination concerns. If too high, false negatives increase sanctions risk.
- **Blocked-funds handling.** Whether funds are held in suspense, returned to sender, or blocked in place affects reporting and record-keeping obligations.
- **Reporting obligations.** Specific reporting timelines, formats, and authorities vary by jurisdiction and regime. Missing a deadline or filing an incomplete report can result in penalties.
- **Merchant communications.** Whether merchants can receive match explanations affects transparency, customer relationships, and potential legal exposure.
## Recommended last-mile verification
Before finalizing the screening control, counsel should:
1. **Confirm jurisdiction scope.** Identify all countries and currencies the payout product will support and map each to applicable sanctions regimes (OFAC, EU, UK, UN, local).
2. **Confirm list coverage.** Identify all sanctions and restricted-party lists to be screened and confirm that the screening vendor covers them with adequate update frequency.
3. **Review match logic and thresholds.** Work with Compliance and the screening vendor to document match-scoring thresholds, escalation procedures, and false-positive correction processes. Test the system with sample data to assess false-positive and false-negative rates.
4. **Draft blocking and rejection procedures.** Document step-by-step procedures for confirming matches, blocking or rejecting transactions, handling funds, and escalating to counsel when needed.
5. **Draft reporting and record-retention procedures.** Identify all reporting obligations (e.g., OFAC blocked property reports, EU member state notifications) and deadlines. Implement systems to track and meet these obligations. Confirm record-retention periods and storage requirements.
6. **Draft communications templates.** Prepare approved templates for recipient and merchant communications that avoid tipping off while providing necessary information and a path to correct false positives.
7. **Train staff.** Train compliance, operations, and customer support staff on screening, blocking, reporting, and communications procedures.
8. **Audit and monitor.** Implement periodic audits of screening and blocking decisions to ensure consistency, accuracy, and non-discrimination. Monitor false-positive rates and adjust thresholds as needed.
9. **Coordinate with partners.** Confirm that banking partners, processors, and screening vendors understand and support Mosaic Relay's obligations. Address compliance responsibilities in contracts.
10. **Document everything.** Maintain clear, contemporaneous records of screening logic, match decisions, blocking and rejection actions, reports filed, and communications sent. These records are critical for demonstrating compliance and defending against enforcement actions.
