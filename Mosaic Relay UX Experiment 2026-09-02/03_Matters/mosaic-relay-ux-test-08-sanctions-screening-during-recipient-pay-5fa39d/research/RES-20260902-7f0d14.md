# What are the record-retention and reporting requirements for blocked funds

## Question

What are the record-retention and reporting requirements for blocked funds and rejected transactions under applicable sanctions regimes?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, wants to screen every payout recipient against sanctions and restricted-party lists immediately before releasing funds. Potential matches would route payouts to manual review; confirmed matches would stop the payout and restrict the account. Product has asked counsel to define the legal guardrails—screening scope, blocking duties, reporting, record retention, and recipient communications—before launch. No launch date has been set, but the control design is underway now.
## Decision question
When Mosaic Relay's pre-release screening confirms a sanctions match on a payout recipient, must the company block (freeze) the funds, reject the transaction, or simply delay it—and what reports to government authorities and record-retention periods apply to each outcome across the jurisdictions and currencies the product will support?
## Open questions
- Which countries and currencies will the payout product support at launch? This determines which sanctions regimes (e.g., US OFAC, EU, UK OFSI, UN) apply and whether blocking or rejection duties differ.
- Which specific sanctions and restricted-party lists will the screening vendor check? List coverage drives both match logic and downstream reporting obligations.
- How will Mosaic Relay handle funds while a payout is in review or after a confirmed block—segregated account, return to sender, or hold in place?
- What match-scoring thresholds and escalation deadlines are planned? These affect both false-positive rates and the timeliness of any required reports.
- Can merchants receive match explanations, or is that information restricted to Compliance? This shapes the tipping-off and evasion-risk analysis.

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
The analysis below is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of sanctions compliance frameworks. It should be verified against current primary sources before reliance.
### 1. Blocking vs. rejection duties
Under most major sanctions regimes, when a screening match is confirmed against a blocked or specially designated party, the obligation is typically to **block** (freeze) the funds—not to reject or return them. Key distinctions:
- **Blocking (freezing):** The funds must be placed in a segregated, interest-bearing blocked account and may not be released, transferred, or dealt in any way without a license or authorization from the relevant sanctions authority. The blocked party retains legal title but loses access.
- **Rejection:** For some sanctions programs (e.g., certain non-blocking restrictions or where the transaction itself is prohibited but the party is not a blocked person), the correct response may be to reject the transaction and return funds to the sender. Rejection duties and reporting requirements differ from blocking duties.
- **Practical issue for Mosaic Relay:** Because Mosaic Relay is not a bank and does not hold deposits, the mechanics of "blocking" may depend on where the funds sit in the payment chain (Mosaic Relay's own settlement account, a partner bank's account, or in transit). The blocking obligation may fall on the financial institution holding the funds, with Mosaic Relay bearing a duty to identify, escalate, and not facilitate the prohibited transaction.
### 2. Reporting obligations
Most sanctions regimes impose prompt reporting duties when funds are blocked or transactions are rejected:
- **Initial blocked-property reports:** Typically required within a short window (often 10 business days in US OFAC practice) after blocking. The report must identify the blocked party, the amount, the date of blocking, and the account where funds are held.
- **Annual blocked-property reports:** Many regimes require an annual report of all blocked property held as of a specified date.
- **Rejected-transaction reports:** Some regimes require reporting of rejected transactions even where no funds are blocked, particularly where the rejection is based on a sanctions match.
- **Suspicious activity / transaction reports (SARs/STRs):** If the match also triggers AML concerns, separate suspicious-activity reporting may be required under AML frameworks, with different deadlines and content requirements.
- **Cross-border complexity:** If a payout crosses borders, multiple regimes may impose overlapping or conflicting reporting duties. The jurisdiction where the funds are held, the jurisdiction of the sender, the jurisdiction of the recipient, and the jurisdiction of Mosaic Relay's own operations may all be relevant.
### 3. Record retention
Sanctions-related records generally must be retained for a minimum period—commonly **five years** from the date of the transaction or the date of blocking, whichever is later. Records typically include:
- The screening results (including the lists checked, the match score, and the data compared)
- The basis for the match determination (confirmed vs. false positive)
- The action taken (blocked, rejected, released after review)
- Copies of any reports filed with government authorities
- Communications with the affected party and with the sanctions authority
- The false-positive correction or appeal record, if applicable
Retention periods may be longer if the matter is under investigation or if a license application is pending.
### 4. Communications to affected parties
The product team's proposal to show recipients only "additional review is required" is a common and generally defensible approach to reduce evasion risk. However:
- **Tipping-off prohibitions:** In some jurisdictions, disclosing that a transaction has been reported to a sanctions or AML authority may itself be an offense. The generic message helps mitigate this risk.
- **False-positive fairness:** Legitimate recipients who are flagged as false positives need a correction path. Prolonged unexplained holds can create contractual, consumer-protection, and reputational risk even if the sanctions analysis is correct.
- **Merchant explanations:** Whether merchants can receive match explanations depends on the regime and the nature of the information. Sharing list-match details with merchants could facilitate evasion if the merchant is complicit, but withholding all information can damage legitimate business relationships.
### 5. Anti-discrimination and consistency controls
Sanctions screening must be applied uniformly. Controls to prevent discriminatory or inconsistent treatment include:
- Documented match-scoring thresholds applied consistently across all recipients
- Human review of all potential matches before blocking (not automated blocking based on name alone)
- A documented false-positive correction and appeal procedure with defined timelines
- Audit logs of all screening decisions, overrides, and releases
- Periodic testing of screening logic for disparate impact on particular nationalities, ethnicities, or name patterns

## Viable paths
1. **Full blocking with segregated accounts:** Implement blocking at the point of confirmed match, place funds in a segregated blocked account, file initial and annual reports, and retain all records for the applicable retention period. This is the most conservative approach and aligns with OFAC-style practice. It requires Mosaic Relay to have the operational capability to segregate funds and track blocked property over time.
2. **Reject-and-return with reporting:** Where the applicable regime permits rejection rather than blocking (e.g., for non-blocking sanctions or where Mosaic Relay is not the party holding funds), reject the payout, return funds to the sender, and file any required rejected-transaction reports. This is operationally simpler but may not be available for all match types.
3. **Hybrid: block at partner bank, Mosaic Relay as screening layer:** If funds are held at a partner bank, the blocking obligation may technically fall on the bank. Mosaic Relay's role would be to screen, escalate confirmed matches to the bank, and cooperate on reporting. This requires clear contractual allocation of responsibilities with each partner bank.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Jurisdictions and currencies:** If Mosaic Relay operates only in the US, OFAC rules dominate. If it operates in the EU or UK, additional or different blocking, reporting, and retention rules apply. Some regimes (e.g., EU) may have different blocking mechanics or shorter reporting windows.
- **Lists used:** Screening against OFAC SDN alone vs. a broader set (EU consolidated list, UK sanctions list, UN list, commercial restricted-party lists) changes both the match rate and the downstream obligations.
- **Where funds sit:** If Mosaic Relay never takes custody of funds, the blocking obligation may fall entirely on partner banks, and Mosaic Relay's duty may be limited to screening and escalation.
- **Whether any recipients are in comprehensively sanctioned jurisdictions:** If payouts go to recipients in countries under comprehensive sanctions (e.g., Iran, North Korea, Cuba, Syria, Crimea region), the analysis shifts from list-matching to country-based prohibitions, which are stricter.
- **Volume and false-positive rate:** A high false-positive rate with slow manual review could create systemic delays that raise consumer-protection or contractual issues independent of sanctions law.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before finalizing advice, counsel should:
1. **Confirm the specific sanctions regimes** that apply based on the launch jurisdictions and currencies, and pull the current blocking, reporting, and retention requirements from primary sources (e.g., OFAC regulations at 31 C.F.R. Chapter V, EU Council Regulations, UK Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018 and OFSI guidance).
2. **Review the screening vendor's list coverage and update frequency** to confirm it meets the requirements of each applicable regime.
3. **Map the funds flow** to determine where blocking obligations sit (Mosaic Relay vs. partner bank) and confirm contractual allocation with each partner.
4. **Check tipping-off and disclosure rules** in each jurisdiction to validate the "additional review is required" messaging and the merchant-explanation policy.
5. **Confirm record-retention periods** for each applicable regime, as they may differ (e.g., OFAC generally requires five years; EU regimes may vary).
