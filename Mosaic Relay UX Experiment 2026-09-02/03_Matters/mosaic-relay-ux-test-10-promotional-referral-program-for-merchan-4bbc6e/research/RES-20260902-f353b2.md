      ## Matter summary

      Mosaic Relay, a B2B payment-infrastructure provider, wants to launch a six-month
      referral program offering existing merchants a $250 account credit when a referred
      business processes $5,000 in eligible volume. The program would be promoted
      through referral links, email invitations, in-product banners, customer testimonials,
      and sales staff during onboarding calls across all supported markets. Counsel
      is involved because the incentive structure, testimonial use, and multi-channel
      promotion raise FTC endorsement-disclosure, marketing-consent, consumer-protection,
      and tax questions that must be resolved before launch. No launch date has been
      set, but the business has asked legal to continue with assumptions rather than
      wait for complete facts.

      ## Decision question

      Should Mosaic Relay structure its merchant referral program as a $250 account
      credit (fee offset) or as a cash reward, and what FTC material-connection disclosures,
      testimonial-substantiation steps, and marketing-consent controls must be in
      place before the six-month program launches across all supported markets?

      ## Open questions

      - Which specific markets are "supported markets," and are any outside the United
      States? (This determines whether non-U.S. endorsement, privacy, and promotional-contest
      rules apply.)

      - Are the customer testimonials Mosaic Relay plans to use genuine, current,
      and representative of typical merchant results, and do the testimonial-givers
      have any existing material connection to Mosaic Relay beyond the referral incentive?

      - Does the $250 credit change the pricing disclosures or fee schedule shown
      to referred businesses during onboarding?

      - What is the tax characterization of the credit for both Mosaic Relay and the
      referring merchant (reduction in revenue vs. marketing expense; taxable income
      to the recipient)?

      - Are any referring or referred merchants in regulated industries (e.g., money
      services, cannabis, gambling) that would trigger additional eligibility exclusions
      or partner-contract restrictions?

      ---

      ## Likely rules and issues

      ### 1. FTC Endorsement Guides — material connection disclosure

      The core issue is the FTC''s Endorsement Guides (16 C.F.R. Part 255). Under
      those guides, when a person endorses a product or service and there is a "material
      connection" between the endorser and the advertiser — a connection that might
      materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement and that consumers
      would not reasonably expect — that connection must be clearly and conspicuously
      disclosed.

      Here, the $250 referral credit is a material connection. A referring merchant
      who recommends Mosaic Relay to another business and receives a fee credit in
      return is an endorser with a financial incentive. The Guides apply to B2B endorsements,
      not just consumer-facing ones; the relevant audience is the referred business''s
      owners and decision-makers.

      **Practical consequence:** Every referral link, email invitation, in-product
      banner, and sales-script touchpoint that communicates the referral incentive
      should include a clear disclosure that the referring merchant receives a credit
      for successful referrals. The disclosure must be difficult to miss — not buried
      in terms and conditions. For testimonials used in Mosaic Relay''s own marketing,
      if the testimonial-giver has received or expects to receive referral credits,
      that connection must be disclosed alongside the testimonial.

      ### 2. Testimonial substantiation and representativeness

      Under the Endorsement Guides and FTC Act Section 5, testimonials must reflect
      the honest opinions or experience of the endorser, and if the testimonial implies
      results that are representative of what others can expect, the advertiser must
      have substantiation for that implied claim. If results are not typical, the
      ad must disclose the generally expected results.

      **Practical consequence:** Mosaic Relay should verify that each testimonial
      it plans to use is from a real merchant, reflects genuine experience, and —
      if the testimonial implies specific results (e.g., savings, volume, speed) —
      that Mosaic Relay has data to support the implied claim or includes a clear
      "results not typical" disclosure. Displaying the number of successful referrals
      is likely fine as a factual statement, but should be accurate and current.

      ### 3. Marketing-consent requirements

      - **Email invitations:** CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email. Requirements
      include accurate header information, non-deceptive subject lines, identification
      of the message as an ad, a valid physical postal address, and a functioning
      opt-out mechanism honored within 10 business days. If referral emails are sent
      by referring merchants (peer-to-peer), Mosaic Relay should ensure its referral
      platform either facilitates compliance or restricts the feature to avoid liability
      as the entity procuring the emails.

      - **Text/SMS invitations:** If any referral invitations go by text, the TCPA
      requires prior express written consent for marketing texts. This is a higher-risk
      channel and should be excluded unless consent infrastructure is confirmed.

      - **In-product banners:** Generally lower risk, but should still carry the material-connection
      disclosure.

      ### 4. Consumer-protection / UDAP risk

      State unfair-and-deceptive-practices statutes (mini-FTC acts) mirror the federal
      disclosure requirements. The tagline "Earn rewards for growing the Mosaic Relay
      community" is puffery-adjacent and likely acceptable, but the program terms
      must clearly state the conditions (the $5,000 eligible-volume threshold, the
      six-month window, any caps, exclusions, and the non-cash nature of the credit)
      to avoid a deception claim.

      ### 5. Tax and accounting treatment

      - **For Mosaic Relay:** The credit is likely treated as a reduction in revenue
      (a sales incentive / rebate) rather than a marketing expense, but this depends
      on the accounting team''s classification under ASC 606 (consideration payable
      to a customer). This is an accounting judgment, not a legal one, but legal should
      flag it.

      - **For the referring merchant:** The credit reduces future platform fees. Whether
      it constitutes taxable income at receipt or a reduction in deductible expenses
      depends on the merchant''s own tax posture. Mosaic Relay should consider whether
      it has any 1099 reporting obligation. Under current IRS rules, payments of $600
      or more in the course of trade or business may trigger 1099-MISC/1099-NEC reporting.
      A fee credit that reduces the amount a merchant pays could be characterized
      as a payment. This is a genuine open question that needs tax counsel input.

      ### 6. Geographic restrictions

      The request says "all supported markets" but does not identify them. If any
      are outside the U.S., additional rules apply — e.g., the UK CAP Code and CMA
      guidance on incentivised referrals, EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive,
      and local anti-spam laws (CASL in Canada, GDPR ePrivacy rules in the EU). This
      is a material gap.

      ### Path A: Account-credit (fee-offset) structure — as proposed

      **Advantages:**

      - Simpler tax posture: no cash changes hands, reducing (but not eliminating)

      - Lower fraud and abuse surface: credits can only be used on the platform.
