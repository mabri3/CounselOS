# What consumer-protection and disclosure obligations apply to marketplace sellers

## Question

What consumer-protection and disclosure obligations apply to marketplace sellers and their customers under US state and federal law?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment-infrastructure provider, wants to cut marketplace-seller activation from two business days to five minutes by running automated KYB, identity verification, sanctions screening, and risk scoring during signup. Low-risk sellers would get immediate limited processing access (capped at $10,000/month with delayed payouts); higher-risk sellers stay pending. Counsel is involved because the instant model raises KYC/KYB, beneficial-owner, sanctions, disclosure, state money-transmission, and consumer-protection questions that must be answered before a planned launch. The consumer-protection piece matters because sellers and their customers can be harmed by unclear disclosures, mishandled rejections, or deceptive onboarding representations.
## Decision question
Before launch, which consumer-protection and disclosure obligations must Mosaic Relay build into the instant-onboarding flow — covering seller-facing disclosures (fees, payout timing, holds, rejection/adverse-action notices) and end-customer protections — so that the five-minute activation model does not create unfair/deceptive-practices exposure under federal and state law, and what minimum notices and records must exist before a seller's first payment?
## Open questions
- **Vendor decision logic and data sources:** Is the risk-scoring/verification output a "consumer report" under FCRA? If yes, adverse-action notices are mandatory for rejected sellers — this directly changes the build.
- **How do rejected sellers receive notice?** No process exists yet; the answer determines whether FCRA/ECOA-style notices and state UDAP-compliant explanations are required.
- **Exact seller/customer locations:** Which states' UDAP statutes and any state-specific marketplace or disclosure laws apply is undetermined (US-only assumed).
- **Who owns seller-facing disclosures — marketplace or Mosaic Relay?** CDD ownership is shared/to be allocated; disclosure responsibility must follow that allocation.
- **Data retention period:** Unset; affects recordkeeping for disclosures, consent, and adverse-action documentation.

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is generated analysis based on general legal knowledge, not retrieved or verified authority. Confirm against current statutes and rules before reliance.
### Federal consumer-protection exposure
1. **FTC Act § 5 — unfair or deceptive acts or practices (UDAP).** The core federal risk. Deceptive exposure arises if the onboarding flow misrepresents processing terms — e.g., promising "instant activation" or "immediate payouts" while imposing the $10,000 cap and delayed payouts without clear, conspicuous disclosure. Unfairness exposure arises if sellers are harmed by holds, reserves, or terminations they could not reasonably avoid and that are not outweighed by countervailing benefits. The five-minute flow must not bury material terms.
2. **FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act).** If the identity-verification or risk-scoring vendor's data qualifies as a "consumer report" and Mosaic Relay uses it to deny or limit a seller (who may be a sole proprietor or an individual beneficial owner), adverse-action obligations attach: notice of the adverse action, the consumer-reporting agency's identity, a statement that the agency did not make the decision, and notice of the right to a free report and to dispute. This is the single most consequential open dependency — it hinges on the vendor's data sources and decision logic.
3. **ECOA / Regulation B.** Applies to credit decisions. If any part of the onboarding or risk-scoring touches a credit component (e.g., a seller advancing or credit-like product), ECOA adverse-action and notice requirements could attach. Likely peripheral for pure payment processing, but must be ruled out based on the product's actual features.
4. **UDAAP (Dodd-Frank §§ 1031, 1036).** If Mosaic Relay or its partners are "covered persons" under the CFPB's jurisdiction, the prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts applies. Even if CFPB jurisdiction is uncertain, the standard is a useful design benchmark and is mirrored by state law.
### State consumer-protection exposure
5. **State UDAP / "mini-FTC" statutes.** Every state has one; many provide private rights of action and attorney's fees. These apply to representations made to sellers during onboarding and to end-customer-facing conduct. The five-minute flow's speed increases the risk that material terms (caps, holds, payout delays, termination rights) are not adequately disclosed.
6. **State money-transmission / money-services statutes.** Primarily a licensing question (handled elsewhere in this matter), but several states impose disclosure, receipt, and consumer-notice obligations on licensed or exempt money-services businesses. If Mosaic Relay's model triggers any of these, seller- and customer-facing notices may follow.
7. **State marketplace-facilitator / seller-protection laws.** A growing number of states regulate marketplace–seller relationships (payment timing, termination notice, dispute processes). Whether these reach Mosaic Relay depends on whether it is characterized as part of the marketplace's payment flow.
### Disclosure obligations to build in
8. **Seller-facing disclosures (before activation):** clear statement of the $10,000 monthly cap; delayed-payout timing; reserve/hold rights; termination and suspension rights; fees; data-use and screening consent; and the conditions for moving from limited to full access.
9. **Adverse-action / rejection notices:** a defined process for telling rejected or pending sellers why, consistent with FCRA if applicable and with general UDAP principles regardless.
10. **End-customer protections:** refund, dispute/chargeback, and error-resolution processes that flow through the marketplace and Mosaic Relay's stack; clear identification of who the customer should contact.
11. **Recordkeeping:** retention of disclosures shown, consents captured, screening results, and adverse-action notices — at least to the five-year BSA floor and any longer state requirement.

## Viable paths
- **Path A — Full FCRA-compliant build (safest).** Assume the vendor output is a consumer report; build adverse-action notices, dispute rights, and consent capture into the flow. Higher build cost, but eliminates the largest federal exposure regardless of how the vendor-logic question resolves.
- **Path B — Confirm vendor status first, then scope.** Obtain the vendor's data sources and decision logic in writing; if the output is not a consumer report, scale back to UDAP-compliant disclosures only. Faster, but leaves residual risk if the characterization is wrong.
- **Path C — Disclosure-first minimum viable compliance.** Build clear, conspicuous seller-facing disclosures (cap, payout delay, holds, termination) and a basic rejection-notice process now; defer full FCRA infrastructure pending vendor confirmation. Balances speed and protection but is the weakest on adverse-action risk.
