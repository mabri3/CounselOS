# What are the state money-transmission licensing thresholds and exemptions releva

## Question

What are the state money-transmission licensing thresholds and exemptions relevant to a payment-infrastructure provider that does not hold deposits and limits seller volume?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment-infrastructure provider that is not a bank and does not hold deposits, wants to cut marketplace-seller activation from two business days to five minutes by running automated KYB, identity verification, sanctions screening, and risk scoring during signup. Low-risk sellers would get immediate limited processing access (capped at $10,000 monthly volume with delayed payouts); higher-risk sellers would stay pending. Counsel is involved because the limited-access model raises KYC/KYB, beneficial-owner, sanctions, disclosure, consumer-protection, and state money-transmission questions that must be answered before a planned launch. The specific research question here is whether state money-transmission licensing thresholds or exemptions apply given that Mosaic Relay does not hold deposits and caps seller volume.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay lawfully activate low-risk marketplace sellers in five minutes — before full KYB completion — under a $10,000/month volume cap with delayed payouts, without triggering state money-transmitter licensing in the states where the marketplace and its sellers are located, and what minimum verification and records must be completed before the first payment if so?
## Open questions
- **Jurisdictions**: Where are the marketplace customer and its sellers located? Money-transmission law is state-by-state; no answer is possible without this. Currently assumed US-only, but no states identified.
- **Flow of funds**: Does Mosaic Relay ever take possession or control of seller funds (even briefly), or does money move directly between acquiring partner and seller? This is the single most important fact for the licensing trigger and for agent-of-payee / processor exemptions.
- **Acquiring-partner requirements**: Partner pre-activation verification requirements are under discussion; partners may impose KYB minimums stricter than any statute, which would moot the "five minutes" design regardless of licensing analysis.
- **CDD allocation**: Whether the marketplace or Mosaic Relay owns customer due diligence is unresolved ("shared / to be allocated"), which affects who must hold the records and who bears licensing/compliance exposure.
- **Vendor decision logic and adverse-action notice**: How the automated risk-scoring vendor decides, and how rejected sellers are notified, remain unknown — relevant to consumer-protection and fair-lending-adjacent exposure even if licensing is resolved.

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The supplied public research explicitly declined to answer: its corpus contained no state money-transmission statute, no definition of "money transmission," no licensing threshold, and no exemption provision, and it treated escalation as mandatory because no jurisdiction was specified. The only supplied citations — Regulation B § 1002.11(e) (state exemption process for ECOA credit transactions), FCRA 15 U.S.C. § 1681c (check-services exclusion from consumer-reporting limits), and EFTA § 913, 15 U.S.C. § 1693k (limits on requiring preauthorized EFT payment) — are federal consumer-credit/payments-adjacent provisions that do not govern state money-transmitter licensing. The supplied material also carried a staleness disclaimer (index/corpus mismatch), so even those citations should be re-verified before use.
What follows is generated analysis, not retrieved authority, and must be verified against current state statutes before launch:
1. **The licensing trigger is control of funds, not deposit-holding.** In most states, "money transmission" turns on receiving money for transmission or holding funds incident to transmission — not on being a bank or holding deposits. Mosaic Relay's "we don't hold deposits" fact therefore does **not**, by itself, avoid licensing. If Mosaic Relay (or its marketplace flow) ever takes possession or control of seller funds before payout, many states would treat that as money transmission absent an exemption.
2. **Volume caps rarely create licensing exemptions.** The $10,000/month seller cap is a risk-control tool, not a licensing shield. A small number of states have de minimis or limited-activity provisions, but most licensing regimes have no volume floor — a single transmitted dollar can trigger licensure. The cap is still useful: it limits exposure during any period of licensing uncertainty and supports partner risk discussions.
3. **The realistic exemptions to test, state by state:**
- **Agent-of-payee exemption**: If the marketplace or buyer's payment to Mosaic Relay (or its partner) satisfies the seller's obligation — i.e., payment to the agent constitutes payment to the seller — many states exempt the flow. This requires careful contract drafting between marketplace, Mosaic Relay, and seller.
- **Payment-processor / agent-of-the-payee carve-outs**: Some states exempt entities that facilitate payment for goods/services through a formal agreement with the payee.
- **Bank-partner / agent-of-licensee structures**: Operating as agent of a licensed partner or under an acquiring bank's umbrella can avoid independent licensure in some states, but partners must agree and regulators scrutinize these arrangements.
- **Marketplace/platform exemptions**: A minority of states have adopted marketplace-facilitator-style exemptions; coverage is uneven.
4. **Delayed payouts cut both ways.** Delaying payout reduces fraud and credit risk but typically *increases* the period during which Mosaic Relay (or the structure) holds funds owed to sellers — the very fact pattern licensing statutes target. The longer the delay, the harder an agent-of-payee or pure-processor argument becomes in some states.
5. **Adjacent regimes remain live regardless of licensing outcome**: FinCEN MSB registration analysis (federal, separate from state licensing), OFAC sanctions screening (already planned), beneficial-owner collection consistent with CDD expectations, state consumer-protection and UDAP exposure for seller-facing disclosures (fees, holds, reserves, payout timing), and adverse-action/notice practices for rejected sellers.
## Viable paths
- **Path A — Pure agent-of-payee / processor structure.** Draft marketplace–Mosaic Relay–seller agreements so payment to Mosaic Relay's partner satisfies the buyer's obligation to the seller, and so Mosaic Relay never holds funds for its own account. Fastest to market; requires state-by-state confirmation that the exemption covers this exact flow, and disciplined contract language.
- **Path B — Operate under partner coverage.** Structure flows so the acquiring bank or a licensed partner is the transmitter of record and Mosaic Relay acts as its agent/service provider. Slower (partner negotiations already underway), but converts the licensing question into a partner-allocation question.
- **Path C — Launch-state gating.** Launch instant onboarding only in states confirmed exempt or low-risk after a 50-state survey; keep the two-day flow elsewhere. Preserves the five-minute experience where it matters most while the broader analysis completes.
- **Path D — Obtain licenses.** A 50-state money-transmitter licensing program is a 12–24 month, high-cost effort; inconsistent with the launch timeline but may be the long-term answer if volume caps lift.
## Facts that could change the answer
- The specific states involved (a handful of states drive most of the licensing burden; some are notably aggressive or notably permissive).
- Whether Mosaic Relay ever touches or controls funds, and for how long (the delayed-payout mechanics are decisive).
- Whether the marketplace is itself licensed or exempt, and whether it will contractually stand in the flow of funds.
- Acquiring-partner requirements, which may impose pre-activation verification floors regardless of state law.
- Whether sellers are consumers, sole proprietors, or entities — this changes consumer-protection and beneficial-owner analysis.

4. **Re-run or refresh the supplied public research** — the provider flagged a stale index, and its corpus lacked any state licensing statute; a targeted pull of state money-transmission statutes (or a licensing counsel memo) is needed before counsel relies on any path above.
5. **Analyze FinCEN MSB registration separately** — federal MSB status is independent of state licensing and was not covered by the supplied material.
*Recommendation (for the lawyer's decision, not a recorded decision):* Path C (launch-state gating) combined with Path A contract structuring is the most realistic route to a five-minute experience on the current timeline, but no state should be green-lit until the 50-state survey and flow-of-funds confirmation are complete. The $10,000 cap and delayed payouts are sound risk controls but should not be represented internally as licensing exemptions.
