    ## Executive Summary


    Northstar Pay can lawfully charge late fees on both product lines, but the two
    products travel very different legal paths. For pay-in-4, the controlling question
    is the unsettled status of the CFPB''s interpretive rule treating BNPL like credit
    cards under Regulation Z **[unverified lead — must be confirmed before launch]**;
    the safe course is a conservative uniform fee at or below the lowest applicable
    state cap, a 21-day grace period, and pre-fee reminders, which satisfies the card-style
    framework if it applies and is defensible under state law if it does not. For
    longer-term installments, the analysis is state-by-state and partner-by-partner:
    the fee limit, grace period, and disclosure obligations turn on which entity is
    the true lender and which state license applies **[missing fact — partner matrix]**.
    The first-fee waiver is permissible if the criteria are objective, automated,
    and uniformly applied. Late fees and returned-payment fees must be kept distinct
    in the ledger and in customer-facing disclosures. Fees may generally be included
    in credit reporting and collection balances, subject to FCRA accuracy and collections-conduct
    limits, but the reporting question is currently a **missing fact**. The gating
    items before launch are the proposed fee amount, the finalized state/partner matrix,
    and verification of the BNPL rule status. No decision is recorded; this memo is
    a best-effort first pass and no unverified legal lead is confirmed.


    ## Scope and Assumptions


    This memo covers late fees and missed-payment treatment for the app-based pay-in-4
    product and the longer-term installment products, across the currently live pay-in-4
    states (California, Colorado, Georgia, Illinois, New York, Texas, Washington)
    **[supplied fact]** and the smaller approved-state list for longer-term products
    **[missing fact — exact list]**. The proposed policy elements — a grace period,
    pre-fee reminders, in-app and account-history fee display, and a conditional first-fee
    waiver — are **supplied facts** from the Product request. The memo rests on four
    labeled assumptions: **(A)** a bank or lending partner originates the longer-term
    product and Northstar Pay services it; **(B)** late fees are not currently credit-reported;
    **(C)** a uniform national fee is preferred over state-tiered fees; and **(D)**
    the customer-facing experience can vary by state where required. Each assumption
    is falsifiable and flagged where it matters. The proposed fee amount, grace-period
    length, waiver criteria, payment methods, NSF-fee proposal, and release date are
    **missing facts**; where they change the answer, that is stated.


    ## Pay-in-4: Legal Limits and Unsettled BNPL/Reg Z Status


    The threshold issue for pay-in-4 is whether the CFPB''s 2024 interpretive rule
    treating BNPL providers like credit-card issuers under Regulation Z is currently
    in force. **[Unverified lead — the rule''s status, including any rescission or
    litigation, must be confirmed as of the launch date.]** If it applies, the card-style
    framework would require, among other things, a grace period before a late fee
    (historically tied to a 21-day payment-due-to-fee window), limits on late-fee
    amounts (the historical card limits were $29 first / $40 subsequent, with an $8-cap
    rule in litigation — **unverified lead**), and card-style periodic disclosures.
    If it does not apply, pay-in-4 falls back to state consumer-credit and financing-law
    treatment, which varies. The practical consequence: a policy built to the card-style
    framework — 21-day grace, reminders, a fee at or below the lowest state cap, and
    clear disclosures — is compliant on either branch. Charging a fee earlier than
    21 days after the due date, or above the conservative cap, is the exposure to
    avoid. State-level treatment of pay-in-4 as a regulated installment sale or financing
    arrangement (notably California''s financing-law treatment) is an **unverified
    lead** requiring confirmation per state.


    ## Longer-Term Installments: State and Partner Analysis


    For longer-term installments, the controlling questions are which entity is the
    true lender and which state license governs each product in each state. **[Missing
    fact — the state/partner matrix is still being finalized; this is the gating implementation
    fact.]** If a bank partner is the true lender, federal preemption may displace
    state fee caps; if Northstar Pay or a nonbank partner is the lender, state law
    applies in full. New York presents the additional "Madden effect" question of
    whether a bank partner''s rate exportation survives after assignment **[unverified
    lead]**. The state regimes to confirm, each keyed to the actual license type and
    partner: California''s Financing Law fee schedules; Illinois''s Predatory Loan
    Prevention Act 36% all-in cap, including whether late fees count toward it **[unverified
    lead — this single question can determine the permissible fee]**; Texas OCCC fee
    schedules; New York licensing and its BNPL statute; and the consumer-loan statutes
    of Georgia, Colorado, and Washington. Until the matrix is final, the memo cannot
    state a per-state permissible fee; the conservative default is a uniform fee at
    or below the lowest cap among approved states.


    ## Fee Amounts and All-In Caps


    The proposed fee amount is a **missing fact** and is the single most consequential
    number in the analysis. It determines whether any state cap is breached, whether
    the Illinois all-in-cap question is material, and whether the pay-in-4 fee fits
    under the historical card-style limits. Where a state imposes an all-in cap, a
    late fee charged on top of an already-maximum-rate loan can itself render the
    loan usurious; the fee must therefore be tested against the cap inclusive of all
    other charges. The recommended approach, pending the real numbers, is a uniform
    fee set at or below the lowest applicable cap, with state-tiered pricing deferred
    to a later phase once the partner matrix and rule status are confirmed. **[Assumption
    C — uniform national fee preferred.]**


    ## Grace Period, Reminders, Notices, and Disclosures


    A 21-day grace period before any late fee is the conservative default for both
    products: it satisfies the card-style framework for pay-in-4 if the interpretive
    rule applies, and it exceeds typical state minimums for longer-term products.
    **[Missing fact — proposed grace-period length.]** Pre-fee reminders should be
    sent before the fee is charged, with the reminder stating the amount, the date
    it will be charged, and how to avoid it. Required disclosures span checkout, the
    loan agreement, the app, and account history: the fee amount, the conditions that
    trigger it, the grace period, and the customer''s options. Consistency across
    all surfaces is itself a UDAAP control — a fee disclosed at checkout but charged
    differently in the app is a complaint and enforcement pattern. State-specific
    notice forms or timing rules for longer-term products are **unverified leads**
    to confirm per state and license type. The company''s stated risk posture — clear
    disclosure of amounts due, dates, fees, and conditions before acceptance — is
    a **supplied fact** and aligns with this framework.


    ## First-Fee Waiver Criteria


    A first-late-fee waiver is permissible and complaint-reducing, but the criteria
    must be objective, automated, and uniformly applied. Discretionary or judgment-based
    waivers create fair-lending disparate-treatment risk and UDAAP consistency concerns
    (similarly situated customers treated differently). The waiver criteria are a
    **missing fact**; the memo recommends criteria such as first missed payment, account
    in good standing, and fee below a threshold, applied automatically by the servicing
    system with an audit log. The waiver should be disclosed in the fee disclosure
    itself so customers know it exists, and the criteria should be documented in a
    policy that operations cannot vary ad hoc.


    ## Failed Payments and Returned-Payment/NSF Fees


    Late fees and returned-payment fees are legally distinct and must be kept distinct
    in both the ledger and customer-facing disclosures. Charging both a late fee and
    an NSF fee for a single failed payment is a leading complaint driver and, in some
    states, impermissible stacking. **[Missing facts — payment methods in scope and
    any NSF-fee proposal.]** If an NSF fee is proposed, it must be tested against
    state NSF-fee limits (which are often lower than late-fee caps) and disclosed
    separately. The recommended default: charge either a late fee or a returned-payment
    fee for a single event, never both, and disclose which applies to which event.


    ## Credit Reporting and Collection Balances


    Late fees may generally be included in credit reporting and in balances referred
    to collections, but two limits apply: FCRA furnisher-accuracy obligations (the
    reported balance including fees must be accurate and disputed amounts investigated),
    and FDCPA and state collections-conduct rules once a fee-inclusive balance is
    referred. Whether Northstar Pay will report fees or include them in collection
    balances is a **missing fact** and a gating question for the partner contracts,
    which must allocate fee ownership between Northstar Pay and the lending partner.
    **[Assumption B — fees not currently credit-reported; falsified if reporting is
    enabled.]** The conservative default is to exclude fees from reporting until the
    accuracy workflow is confirmed.


    ## Ownership and Vendor Controls


    The state/partner matrix drives which entity owns the fee, which servicing vendor
    charges it, and which payment processor handles the failed-payment events. Partner
    and vendor contracts should specify fee ownership, permissible amounts, refund
    authority, and complaint-handling responsibility. Vendor oversight obligations
    apply to servicing and collections vendors handling fee events.


    ## Launch Checklist and Monitoring


    Minimum launch controls: (1) the state/product/partner fee-configuration matrix;
    (2) tested fee-calculation and waiver logic with audit logs; (3) a refund/reversal
