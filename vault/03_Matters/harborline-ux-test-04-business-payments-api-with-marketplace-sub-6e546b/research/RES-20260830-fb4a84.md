# First-Pass Research Packet

## Question

What material source-based issues should counsel research for Harborline UX Test — 04 — Business Payments API with Marketplace Subaccounts?

## Working Analysis

## Matter summary

Harborline (fintech operating software and risk operations; deposit accounts held at a partner bank) wants to launch an API letting software platforms accept customer payments and route proceeds to participating small businesses, with splits among the business, the platform, and Harborline fees. Harborline would manage processing, risk, reserves, returns, and support; platforms would not hold customer funds directly, and Harborline contracts with each business. Key structure decisions are open: pooled custodial vs. individual accounts, platform refund control, and reserve duration. Product wants a limited beta in four months.

## Decision question

Which funds-flow structure should Harborline approve for the Business Payments API — pooled custodial account vs. individual subaccounts at the partner bank, who may issue payment instructions and refunds, and whether the platform may deduct its fee before settlement — given that the choice determines money-transmission and payment-facilitator exposure for Harborline and the platforms, required account titles and agreements, and the four-month beta timeline?

## Open questions

- Pooled custodial account vs. individual subaccounts per business — which does the partner bank support, and what titling/ledger controls does each require?
- Will platforms be permitted to initiate refunds, and if so, under whose rules and liability?
- How long may reserves be held, and what triggers release?
- Does the platform's fee deduction occur pre-settlement (gross-to-net split) or post-settlement (pull from business account)?
- What do the card network and ACH agreements say about payment facilitator registration, sub-merchant onboarding, and chargeback liability?

## Likely rules and issues

**Money transmission (state licensing).** Harborline's receipt of customer/card/ACH proceeds and subsequent direction to businesses looks like money transmission unless an exemption applies. Key candidate exemptions: the "payment processor" / "sale of goods or services" exemption (funds are payment for goods/services sold by the businesses, transmitted to the payee or its agent) and the "authorized venture" / agent-of-payee exemption. The structure matters: if Harborline acts as agent of the payee businesses (contracting with each business, holding funds for their benefit), the agent-of-payee position is stronger. FinCEN's 2014 "exemptive guidance concerning platforms" and the payment-processor guidance (FIN-2014-G009) are the core sources. Platform-side exposure: platforms must not receive or control funds (already a stated constraint) — if a platform ever takes possession or directs funds, it may itself be an unlicensed money transmitter.

**Payment facilitator (card rules).** If Harborline onboards sub-merchants and splits card proceeds, card networks will likely treat Harborline (or the partner bank) as a payment facilitator. Visa/Mastercard PF rules require registration with the network, underwriting standards, sub-merchant disclosure of the PF's role, settlement timing rules, and chargeback liability allocation. Whether Harborline or the partner bank is the registered PF is a threshold architecture decision.

**Funds transfer / Reg E and UCC 4A.** Consumer payers using cards get Reg E error-resolution and chargeback rights; ACH consumer payments get NACHA return rights (R-codes, 60-day unauthorized return window for consumer SEC codes). Harborline must decide who bears return/chargeback losses — the business, the platform, or Harborline's reserves.

**Agency.** Harborline contracting directly with each business supports an agency-of-payee characterization; the platform should be positioned as a referral/integration service, not an agent with authority over funds. Who may issue payment instructions (platform via API vs. business directly) affects this materially.

**Stored value.** If funds sit in a pooled custodial account with a ledger balance businesses can draw against over time, regulators could characterize balances as stored value/prepaid access (BSA prepaid access rules, state money transmission). Faster settlement and clear payee-beneficial ownership of funds reduce this risk.

**BSA/AML.** Harborline (or the partner bank) must own CIP/KYB on each sub-merchant business, sanctions screening, transaction monitoring, and SAR filing. Allocation between Harborline and the partner bank must be documented (typically in the program agreement); beneficial ownership (CDD Rule) collection for entity businesses.

**Disclosures.** Sub-merchant agreement disclosures (PF role, settlement timing, fees, reserves, chargeback liability), deposit-insurance statements (funds in transit are not FDIC-insured unless pass-through insurance requirements are met — "custodial account" titling and balance records matter here), and consumer-facing receipt/error-resolution disclosures.

## Viable paths

1. **Individual subaccounts at partner bank, platform fee deducted post-settlement.** Cleanest: each business has a titled account, funds never commingled, platform never touches funds, agent-of-payee position strongest, pass-through FDIC insurance easier. Cost: more account-opening overhead, slower onboarding at scale.
2. **Pooled custodial account with per-business ledger, Harborline as agent of payees.** Faster and operationally simpler; requires careful titling (FBO businesses), documented ledger controls, and clear settlement timing to avoid stored-value characterization. Higher regulatory scrutiny risk.
3. **Partner bank as the registered payment facilitator; Harborline as program manager.** Shifts PF registration and some liability to the bank; requires bank buy-in and stronger oversight obligations on Harborline.

Recommended default for a four-month beta: Path 1 or a hybrid (individual subaccounts, post-settlement fee deduction), with Harborline as agent of payee and the partner bank as the card PF if the bank will accept it.

## Facts that could change the answer

- Partner bank's willingness to serve as registered PF and to open individual subaccounts at API speed.
- Whether platforms will demand real-time refund control (increases their funds-control exposure).
- Settlement timing (same-day vs. T+2+) — longer holds increase stored-value and money-transmission risk.
- Whether businesses can carry balances (draw later) vs. mandatory sweep to their own external accounts.
- Card network and ACH processor contract terms on sub-merchant onboarding and chargeback allocation.

## Recommended last-mile verification

- Confirm with the partner bank: account structure, titling, PF registration role, BSA/AML responsibility split, and reserve mechanics.
- Confirm with card networks/processor: PF registration path, sub-merchant disclosure requirements, chargeback liability.
- Verify current state-by-state money-transmission payment-processor exemption scope against the chosen funds flow (FinCEN guidance is federal only; state licensing is separate).
- Confirm FDIC pass-through insurance analysis for the chosen account structure with the bank.
- Have counsel review the sub-merchant agreement and platform agreement drafts against the chosen structure before architecture is finalized.

*Note: this is a first-pass issue map based on the matter record and general regulatory frameworks; the specific network rules, state licensing analysis, and partner-bank terms require verification before launch decisions are finalized.*
