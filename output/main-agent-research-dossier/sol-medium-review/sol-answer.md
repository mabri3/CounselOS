# Asset purchase of a US payments business: use of licenses and prior customer checks

## Practical answer

No. The buyer should not assume that the seller’s regulatory permissions or prior customer checks can simply be reused.

State financial-services licenses generally stay with the licensed legal entity. The supplied secondary source states that licenses cannot be transferred or acquired in an asset purchase. Thus, the buyer should treat each state and activity as unavailable to the buyer until the buyer has its own license, a confirmed exemption, or a valid operating model through another properly licensed entity. The fact that customer accounts will migrate after closing does not change which entity performs regulated activity between closing and migration.

Prior customer-identification records can be useful evidence. They are not, by themselves, a legal safe harbor for the buyer. If a bank is opening or taking on an account, 31 C.F.R. § 1020.220 requires the bank’s own written, risk-based Customer Identification Program (CIP) to let it form a reasonable belief that it knows the customer’s true identity. Formal reliance on another financial institution is allowed only if all stated conditions are met: reliance is reasonable; the other institution is subject to an anti-money-laundering program rule and regulated by a federal functional regulator; and it contracts to certify annually that it has implemented its program and will perform the specified CIP steps. Ordinary diligence access, an asset-purchase covenant, or a data file does not satisfy that reliance rule.

The recommended structure is:

1. Close only the assets and markets that can lawfully transfer without making the buyer the unlicensed operator.
2. Keep unmigrated customer activity with the seller or another duly authorized provider under a written transition arrangement, if the applicable state rules, contracts, and regulator positions permit it.
3. Migrate each customer cohort only after the receiving legal entity has the needed authority and has accepted, supplemented, or repeated the customer checks under its own documented risk rules.
4. Do not expand to a new state or promote buyer-provided regulated services there until the authority for that state, entity, product, and funds flow is documented.

If those conditions cannot be met, delay closing, carve out the affected states or activities, pause transactions in those states, or change the deal structure. A stock or membership-interest acquisition can preserve the licensed legal entity, but it may require pre-closing change-of-control approvals or notices. It is not a way to avoid the state review.

## Phase analysis

### 1. Signing and closing

The transaction team should build a matrix using the actual facts for every permission. At minimum, it should identify the license holder, license type and number, covered activity, customer location rule, product, funds flow, state, buyer operating entity, transferability, required filing, approval or notice timing, and the permitted post-close operator.

For a true asset purchase, the working rule is that the licenses do not pass to the buyer. The supplied Goodwin article states this directly. It also explains that state change-of-control rules differ. Depending on the state, they can require prior approval, advance notice, notice with a regulator option to require approval, post-closing notice, or post-closing approval. Control thresholds also differ. These change-of-control rules matter most if the parties preserve a licensed entity through an equity transaction or if the transaction gives the buyer direct or indirect control before the assets migrate. The article is a useful practice source, but it is not a substitute for the operative statute, regulation, regulator guidance, or license condition in each relevant state.

Closing before migration can be lawful only if the post-close operating facts remain lawful. The purchase agreement should not state or imply that the buyer may use the seller’s licenses. It should instead allocate the regulated functions and make required approvals, buyer licenses, exemptions, or transition arrangements conditions to the relevant transfer. A single “critical mass” closing condition based on revenue is not enough unless the excluded states and customers are operationally blocked or remain with an authorized operator. The supplied article notes that parties sometimes use critical-mass conditions or surrender a delayed low-volume license. Those are deal options, not proof that service can continue in the affected state.

**Close fallback:** If the buyer lacks authority in one or more states, use a delayed close, an excluded-asset or excluded-customer schedule, a state-by-state migration condition, or a properly reviewed transition services arrangement. If none is workable, stop service in the affected state before the buyer becomes the operator. The seller should also confirm any surrender, run-off, reporting, bonding, net-worth, and post-close duties before relinquishing a license.

### 2. Period between close and migration

The legal entity that actually accepts customer instructions, controls the account relationship, receives or holds funds, and transmits money is the key operator. Contract labels alone do not decide this. This statement is based on general payments-law analysis; the supplied materials do not contain the operative state definitions.

During this period, the transition agreement should state who:

- is the regulated service provider and appears in customer terms and receipts;
- controls the funds flow and required custodial or benefit-of-customer accounts;
- performs sanctions screening, transaction monitoring, suspicious-activity escalation, complaints, error resolution, refunds, safeguarding, record retention, and regulator reporting;
- may use customer data and for which purpose; and
- bears responsibility if a customer or state cannot migrate on time.

The buyer should have audit rights and receive records. It should not direct the seller in a way that makes the buyer the unlicensed provider. Any bank-partner or agent exemption must be tested against the exact state, activity, contracts, and funds flow. The Goodwin article notes that some payments businesses use bank FBO arrangements as a basis for a licensing position. It does not establish that such an arrangement exempts this buyer.

**Transition fallback:** Keep the customer with the seller or licensed provider until the buyer clears the cohort. If the seller cannot continue lawfully or operationally, suspend the affected functionality or exclude that cohort from the acquisition.

### 3. Customer migration and prior checks

For a receiving bank, the supplied text of 31 C.F.R. § 1020.220 supports these rules:

- The bank must use a written, risk-based CIP designed to form a reasonable belief that it knows each customer’s true identity.
- It must obtain specified identifying information before account opening, subject to stated exceptions, and verify identity within a reasonable time.
- Its procedures must address failed verification, recordkeeping, government-list checks, and customer notice.
- It may formally rely on another financial institution only when the three conditions described above are met.

Therefore, inherited records may satisfy parts of the buyer’s process if the buyer’s approved program permits their use and the records are complete, reliable, current enough for the risk, and legally available. The buyer should document a field-level gap test. It should compare the seller’s collected data, verification methods, results, discrepancies, notices, record dates, and retention evidence against the receiving entity’s requirements.

The age of a prior check is not the only test. Section 1020.220 does not set a general expiration date for identity verification. A refresh decision should instead consider missing data, unresolved discrepancies, document expiry, ownership or control changes, sanctions or fraud alerts, unusual activity, product or geography changes, and the receiving entity’s own policy. This is a risk-based interpretation of the supplied CIP rule. It is not a state-specific conclusion.

The cited CIP rule applies to banks. The file does not supply the separate Bank Secrecy Act rules for a nonbank money transmitter, beneficial-owner verification, sanctions obligations, or state KYC rules. As general legal knowledge, a nonbank money transmitter must apply its own anti-money-laundering program and other applicable requirements; it should not treat the bank CIP reliance clause as a direct safe harbor. Counsel should confirm the operative rules for the actual buyer and product before migration.

**Migration fallback:** Recollect or reverify only the missing, stale, inconsistent, or higher-risk elements. If the buyer cannot form the required reasonable belief or satisfy its own program, keep the account restricted, do not open it, or close it under the applicable procedure. Do not discard useful seller records merely because full reliance is unavailable.

### 4. New-market expansion

A seller license, a pending buyer application, or a transition arrangement for the acquired book does not authorize new buyer business in a state. Before accepting a new customer or transaction, the regulatory owner should approve a written state-by-state conclusion for the precise buyer entity, product, customer type, and funds flow. The conclusion should identify the buyer’s license, a statutory exemption, an authorized bank or agent model, or a decision not to launch.

If the buyer changes the product, customer type, funds custody, settlement path, or legal entity after closing, rerun the analysis. These changes can alter the licensing answer even in a state where the seller operated. This is general legal analysis; the supplied evidence establishes state variation but does not provide the individual state rules.

**Expansion fallback:** Geofence the state, block onboarding and transactions, or launch through a separately reviewed authorized provider. A license application in progress is not authority to operate unless the operative state rule expressly says otherwise.

### 5. Promotion and customer communications

Marketing should follow the same permission map as service launch. Promotion can create risk if it says or implies that the buyer is licensed, ready to serve, or already provides a regulated service in a state where authority is not complete. Limit pre-clearance messages to accurate corporate or transaction information. Route product claims, availability claims, rates, fees, and calls to action through legal and compliance review.

The supplied Regulation P source gives a narrow but useful transaction rule. Under 12 C.F.R. § 1016.15(a)(6), a financial institution may disclose nonpublic personal information in connection with a proposed or actual sale, merger, transfer, or exchange of all or part of a business or operating unit, without the specified notice and opt-out requirements, if the information concerns solely consumers of that business or unit. This supports sale diligence and transfer of the relevant customer data. It does not grant a license, approve account migration, permit unrelated marketing, or resolve contractual limits and state privacy law.

The CFPB page’s table of contents identifies separate rules for initial, annual, and revised privacy notices and for limits on disclosure and reuse, but the supplied passage does not state those operative requirements. As general legal knowledge, the parties should compare the seller’s and buyer’s privacy practices and give any notice required by the governing privacy rule, customer contract, or state law before a changed use or disclosure. Do not use the sale exception as the basis for broad cross-selling to people who are not yet buyer customers.

**Promotion fallback:** Use a neutral deal notice with no product solicitation, suppress affected states and customer groups from campaigns, and obtain consent where counsel concludes it is required. Apply channel-specific marketing rules before email, text, or phone outreach; those rules are outside the supplied evidence.

The Goodwin source says the licensee generally makes change-of-control filings, with cooperation from the buyer, and recommends a regularly updated filing tracker. The parties should assign this expressly rather than assume the buyer can file in the seller’s name.

## Facts that can change the answer

The present answer assumes an asset purchase, US customers, and a payments business that may conduct state-regulated money transmission. It does not assume a particular state, license, exemption, bank-partner structure, or buyer entity.

Only these missing facts require a different operative analysis:

- **Deal perimeter:** Which assets, contracts, accounts, funds, and liabilities transfer at close, and which remain for later migration?
- **Entity and activity:** Which legal entity performs each regulated act before and after migration?
- **State and license:** Where are customers located, what licenses or exemptions cover the exact product, and what do their transfer, control, surrender, and run-off rules require?
- **Account event:** Does migration continue the same legal account, assign a contract, or open a new account with a new institution?
- **Prior-check source:** Did the seller itself perform the checks, did a bank perform them, or did a vendor act as an agent? Is the evidence complete and available?
- **Reliance conditions:** If the receiving entity is a bank, is the prior institution federally regulated and subject to an AML program rule, and will it sign the contract and annual certification required by 31 C.F.R. § 1020.220(a)(6)?
- **Customer status:** How old are the checks and alerts, and are there missing fields, open alerts, discrepancies, ownership changes, expired documents, or higher-risk features?
- **Data and communications:** What did customers agree to, what privacy notices apply, and will the buyer use data only for the sale and servicing or also for promotion?

Until those facts are resolved, the safe working status is: close may proceed only with enforceable state and cohort carve-outs; migration is conditional; expansion is blocked; and promotion is limited to accurate transaction notices for cleared populations.
