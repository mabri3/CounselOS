# What U.S. consumer-protection notices and deadlines apply to card dispute

## Question

What U.S. consumer-protection notices and deadlines apply to card dispute handling (FTC Mail/Telephone Order rules, EFTA/Reg E unauthorized-use provisions, state consumer laws)?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payments-infrastructure provider, is building a dispute portal for a consumer-facing marketplace whose buyers purchase goods via card transactions. The portal would intake buyer disputes (non-receipt, unauthorized use, duplicate charges, defective goods), collect evidence, send automated messages, and route cases to the marketplace or seller. Counsel is involved because the portal sits at the intersection of card-network chargeback rules, federal consumer-protection law (EFTA/Reg E, FTC Mail/Telephone Order Rule), and state UDAP statutes, and the six-week launch timeline (target 2026-10-15) leaves little room to resolve open structural questions like merchant-of-record status and escalation defaults.
## Decision question
Should Mosaic Relay launch the consumer dispute portal by 2026-10-15 with the marketplace as merchant of record and Mosaic Relay as a routing/evidence platform only, and if so, what specific intake questions, consumer notices, response deadlines, evidence-handling rules, status labels, automated communications, and escalation paths must legal require to satisfy EFTA/Reg E, FTC, and card-network obligations while keeping Mosaic Relay outside the funds-flow and dispute-decision role?
## Open questions
- **Merchant of record / customer of record** — Not yet determined. This is the single most consequential open fact: it determines who bears chargeback liability, who must respond within network deadlines, and whether Mosaic Relay has any direct consumer-facing regulatory obligation.
- **Which card networks and regions must the initial release support?** — Not yet determined. Visa and Mastercard have different dispute reason codes, evidence requirements, and timelines; the answer shapes the portal's deadline engine and evidence checklists.
- **Do marketplace seller terms and marketplace terms allocate dispute/refund/chargeback responsibility?** — Not confirmed. If the marketplace's own terms are silent or conflict with network rules, the portal's escalation and liability-routing logic cannot be finalized.
- **When the marketplace or seller does not respond within the network deadline, what should the portal do?** — Not yet decided. The default (auto-escalate to issuer, auto-refund, hold, or close) has direct consumer-protection and financial-liability consequences.
- **Accessibility obligations for the consumer-facing portal** — Not yet scoped. Whether WCAG 2.1 AA compliance is contractually required or legally mandated (ADA Title III, state equivalents) affects design and launch readiness.

## Likely rules and issues
### Federal consumer-protection framework
**EFTA / Regulation E (12 C.F.R. Part 1005)** — Applies to unauthorized electronic fund transfers. If a buyer reports an unauthorized card transaction, Reg E imposes specific obligations on the *financial institution* (the card issuer), not directly on Mosaic Relay or the marketplace. However, the portal's design must not impede the consumer's ability to reach the issuer or assert Reg E rights. Key provisions:
- **Consumer liability caps**: $50 if the consumer notifies the issuer within 2 business days of learning of the loss/theft; up to $500 if notification occurs after 2 business days but within 60 days of the statement date; unlimited liability for transfers occurring more than 60 days after the statement date if the consumer fails to report.
- **Issuer investigation deadlines**: The issuer must investigate and report results within 10 business days of receiving the error notice (or 20 business days for new accounts). If the issuer needs more time, it may take up to 45 days (90 days for point-of-sale or foreign-initiated transfers) but must provisionally credit the consumer's account within 10 business days.
- **Portal implication**: The portal should never represent itself as the channel for asserting Reg E rights. It should include a clear notice directing consumers to contact their card issuer for unauthorized-use claims, and it should not impose any deadline shorter than the Reg E windows.
**FCBA / Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. § 1026.12–1026.13)** — Applies to credit card billing errors (which cover non-receipt, duplicate charges, and defective goods on credit cards). Key provisions:
- **Consumer right to dispute**: The consumer may assert a billing error by writing to the creditor within 60 days after the creditor transmitted the first statement reflecting the error.
- **Creditor acknowledgment and investigation**: The creditor must acknowledge the dispute within 30 days and resolve it within two complete billing cycles (not more than 90 days).
- **Claims and defenses**: Under § 1026.12(c), a cardholder may assert against the card issuer claims and defenses arising out of the transaction (subject to limits: good-faith attempt to resolve with the merchant, amount over $50, transaction in the same state or within 100 miles of the cardholder's address — the last two limits do not apply if the creditor is the merchant or controls the merchant).
- **Portal implication**: The portal should not discourage or delay consumers from exercising FCBA rights. Notices should inform consumers that they may also dispute directly with their card issuer.
**FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 435)** — Requires sellers to have a reasonable basis for shipping representations, ship within the stated time (or 30 days if no time stated), and provide prompt refund if the seller cannot ship. Key provisions:
- **30-day default shipping deadline** when no shipping time is stated.
- **Notification and consent** required for delays; the seller must notify the buyer, state the revised date, and obtain consent or cancel and refund.
- **Refund deadlines**: Refund must be sent within 7 business days of cancellation for cash/check payments; one billing cycle for credit card payments.
- **Portal implication**: For non-receipt disputes, the portal's intake should capture the promised shipping date, actual delivery date, and any delay notifications. The portal's deadline engine should flag when the 30-day window or stated shipping window has passed.
**State UDAP statutes** — Every state has an unfair-and-deceptive-acts-and-practices statute. These generally prohibit deceptive representations about dispute resolution, refund policies, and consumer rights. The portal's automated messages, status labels, and notices must not misrepresent the consumer's rights, the stage of the dispute, or the likelihood of resolution.
### Card-network rules
**Visa and Mastercard chargeback frameworks** — These are contractual obligations between the merchant (or its acquirer) and the network. They impose:
- **Reason codes** that map to the portal's dispute categories (e.g., Visa reason code 10.4 for fraud, 13.1 for merchandise not received; Mastercard reason code 4853 for cardholder dispute, 4855 for goods not received).
- **Response deadlines**: Typically 30 calendar days for the merchant to respond to a chargeback (varies by network and reason code). The portal must route disputes to the marketplace/seller with enough lead time for the merchant to meet the network deadline.
- **Evidence requirements**: Each reason code has specific compelling-evidence requirements. The portal's evidence-upload flow should map to these requirements.
- **Portal implication**: The portal is not itself a party to network rules (unless Mosaic Relay is the merchant of record), but it must be designed so that the marketplace/seller can meet network deadlines and evidence standards through the portal.
### Mosaic Relay's role and liability boundary
The critical structural issue is whether Mosaic Relay is a **service provider** (routing disputes, collecting evidence, sending messages) or has any **merchant-of-record or payment-facilitator** status that would make it directly liable for chargebacks or subject to network rules. The current assumption is that the marketplace is the merchant of record. If that changes, the entire analysis shifts.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Launch as a routing and evidence platform (recommended)
Mosaic Relay operates the portal as a neutral intake-and-routing layer. The marketplace is the merchant of record and bears all chargeback liability and consumer-protection obligations. Mosaic Relay's portal:
- Collects dispute intake using standardized categories mapped to network reason codes.
- Sends automated acknowledgment and status messages to buyers.
- Routes disputes to the marketplace or seller with a response deadline that provides sufficient buffer before the network chargeback deadline.
- Includes clear notices directing consumers to their card issuer for Reg E / FCBA rights.
- Does not make refund decisions, hold funds, or adjudicate disputes.
**Pros**: Cleanest liability boundary; fastest to launch; consistent with Mosaic Relay's business model. **Cons**: Requires the marketplace to actually respond; if the marketplace is unresponsive, the portal needs a defined escalation path (see below).
### Path B: Launch with Mosaic Relay as payment facilitator / merchant of record
Mosaic Relay takes on merchant-of-record status, which means it bears chargeback liability, must comply with network rules directly, and may have direct consumer-protection obligations.
**Pros**: More control over the dispute process; potentially better consumer experience. **Cons**: Materially higher regulatory burden; requires network registration, chargeback management capability, and potentially state money-transmitter analysis; unlikely to be achievable in six weeks.
### Path C: Delayed launch pending merchant-of-record resolution
Delay the launch until the merchant-of-record question is resolved and the marketplace's seller terms are confirmed.
**Pros**: Eliminates the most material assumption. **Cons**: Misses the 2026-10-15 target; may not be necessary if Path A is viable.

## Recommended operating model (Path A)
### Intake questions
The portal should ask, at minimum:
1. **Dispute category** (select one): Non-receipt / Unauthorized use / Duplicate charge / Defective goods
2. **Transaction details**: Date, amount, last four digits of card, order/confirmation number
3. **For non-receipt**: Promised delivery date, whether any delay notification was received, whether the buyer contacted the seller
4. **For unauthorized use**: Whether the buyer has contacted their card issuer, whether the card was lost/stolen, date the buyer discovered the charge
5. **For duplicate charges**: Details of both charges, whether the buyer attempted to resolve with the seller
6. **For defective goods**: Description of the defect, whether the buyer attempted to return the goods, whether the buyer contacted the seller
7. **Consumer contact information** and preferred language (English or Spanish)
8. **Evidence upload**: Receipts, order confirmations, shipping tracking, photos of defective goods, correspondence with seller
### Consumer notices
The portal must display or send the following notices:
1. **Acknowledgment notice** (immediate, automated): Confirms receipt of the dispute, states the dispute category, provides a case number, and states the expected response timeline.
2. **Reg E / FCBA rights notice** (for unauthorized-use and billing-error categories): Informs the consumer that they may also have rights under federal law to dispute the charge directly with their card issuer, provides a plain-language summary of those rights, and states that using this portal does not replace or delay those rights.
3. **No-waiver notice**: States that using the portal does not waive any consumer rights under federal or state law.
4. **Status-change notices** (automated): Sent when the dispute status changes (e.g., received, under review, seller responded, resolved, escalated).
5. **Resolution notice**: States the outcome, any refund amount and expected timing, and the consumer's right to escalate if unsatisfied.
6. **Language access**: All notices must be available in English and Spanish.
### Deadlines
| Event | Deadline | Basis |
|-------|----------|-------|
| Portal acknowledgment to buyer | Immediate (automated) | Best practice / state UDAP |
| Route dispute to marketplace/seller | Within 2 business days of intake | Internal SLA to preserve network response window |
| Marketplace/seller response to portal | Within 10 business days of routing | Provides buffer before network chargeback deadline |
| Escalation if no marketplace/seller response | Automatic at day 10 | See escalation paths below |
| Resolution notice to buyer | Within 2 business days of resolution | Best practice / state UDAP |
| Refund processing (if applicable) | Within 7 business days of resolution (credit card: one billing cycle) | FTC Mail Order Rule |
### Evidence rules
- **Legal requirements** (fixed): Evidence must be retained for at least the period required by network rules (typically 120 days from the chargeback date) and state record-retention laws. Evidence containing cardholder data must be handled per PCI DSS. Evidence shared with the marketplace/seller must be limited to what is necessary to resolve the dispute.
- **Policy choices** (flexible): File format and size limits, whether to allow video uploads, whether to allow anonymous evidence, and the specific evidence checklist per dispute category (beyond what network rules require).
### Status labels
Recommended status labels (mapped to consumer-friendly plain language):
1. **Received** — Dispute submitted and acknowledged
2. **Under review** — Dispute routed to marketplace/seller
3. **Seller responded** — Marketplace/seller has provided a response
4. **Resolved — refund issued** — Refund approved and processing
5. **Resolved — no refund** — Dispute denied, with explanation
6. **Escalated** — Marketplace/seller did not respond; dispute escalated
7. **Closed** — Dispute resolved or withdrawn
### Escalation paths
When the marketplace or seller does not respond within the 10-business-day window:
1. **Automatic escalation to marketplace management** (day 10): Automated notice to a designated marketplace escalation contact.
2. **Buyer notification** (day 10): Automated notice to the buyer that the marketplace has not responded and the dispute is being escalated.
3. **Default action** (day 15): If no response within 5 additional business days, the portal should default to recommending that the buyer contact their card issuer to initiate a chargeback. The portal should not auto-refund unless the marketplace has pre-authorized this in its terms.
4. **Audit trail**: All escalation events must be logged with timestamps.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If Mosaic Relay is the merchant of record**: The entire analysis shifts. Mosaic Relay would bear chargeback liability, need to comply with network rules directly, and potentially have direct Reg E/FCBA obligations. The portal would need to function as a chargeback-management system, not just a routing layer.
- **If the marketplace's seller terms do not allocate dispute responsibility**: The portal's escalation and liability-routing logic cannot be finalized. The marketplace may need to amend its terms before launch.
- **If the initial release must support networks beyond Visa/Mastercard** (e.g., Amex, Discover): Each network has different reason codes, deadlines, and evidence requirements. The portal's deadline engine and evidence checklists would need to be network-specific.
- **If buyers include business purchasers**: Reg E and FCBA apply only to consumer accounts. Business-card disputes are governed by network rules and contract, not federal consumer-protection law. The portal's notices and deadlines would need to differentiate.
- **If the marketplace operates in states with specific consumer-protection statutes** (e.g., California's Consumer Legal Remedies Act, New York's General Business Law § 349): Additional notice or disclosure requirements may apply.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — Merchant of record**: This analysis assumes the marketplace is the merchant of record and Mosaic Relay is a service provider. If Mosaic Relay is the merchant of record or a payment facilitator, the answer changes fundamentally: Mosaic Relay would bear direct chargeback liability, need network registration, and potentially have direct consumer-protection obligations. The six-week timeline would likely be unachievable.
- **Working assumption — U.S.-focused initial release**: The analysis assumes the portal is U.S.-focused. If the initial release includes European or Latin American markets, additional consumer-protection frameworks (e.g., EU Consumer Rights Directive, PSD2) would apply and the notice/deadline structure would need to be jurisdiction-specific.
- **Open fork — Marketplace non-response default**: If the marketplace pre-authorizes auto-refund on non-response, the portal becomes a decision-maker, not just a router. This increases Mosaic Relay's regulatory exposure and may require state money-transmitter analysis. If the default is to direct the buyer to their issuer, the portal remains a neutral platform but the consumer experience is worse.
- **Open fork — Evidence sharing scope**: If the marketplace requires access to full card numbers or bank account details in evidence, PCI DSS scope expands significantly and the portal's data-minimization obligations increase. If evidence is limited to order confirmations and photos, the compliance burden is lower.
- **Not examined — Marketplace's acquiring bank contract**: The marketplace's agreement with its acquiring bank may impose specific dispute-handling requirements (e.g., mandatory use of a particular chargeback-management platform, specific evidence formats, or shorter response deadlines). This document was not available and was not reviewed.
- **Not examined — State-specific consumer-protection statutes**: The analysis references state UDAP statutes generally but does not analyze any specific state's statute. If the marketplace has a concentration of buyers in a particular state (e.g., California, New York, Texas), a state-specific review may identify additional notice or disclosure requirements.
