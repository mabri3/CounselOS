# What are the current Visa and Mastercard chargeback timeframes and

## Question

What are the current Visa and Mastercard chargeback timeframes and evidence requirements for the categories non-receipt, unauthorized use, duplicate charges, and defective goods?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. (a fictional B2B payments-infrastructure provider) is being asked by a consumer-facing marketplace to build a dispute portal for card purchases. Buyers would report non-receipt, unauthorized use, duplicate charges, or defective goods; the portal would collect statements and documents, send automated messages, and route cases to the marketplace or seller. Counsel is involved because the portal must satisfy card-network chargeback rules and U.S. consumer-protection law while separating legal requirements from customer policy choices. Product wants an initial release in six weeks (target 2026-10-15).
## Decision question
Should Mosaic Relay launch the consumer dispute portal by 2026-10-15 with the proposed intake categories, evidence upload flow, and automated communications, given that merchant-of-record status, network-specific deadlines, and escalation paths when the marketplace does not respond are not yet determined?

- Which card networks and regions must the initial release support? (Not yet determined.)

- When the marketplace or seller does not respond within the network deadline, what should the portal do? (Not yet decided; recommendation needed.)
- What exact evidence rules and status labels should be treated as legal requirements versus customer policy choices? (Mixed — some fixed, some policy.)

The following is generated analysis based on general knowledge of Visa and Mastercard chargeback frameworks and U.S. consumer-protection law. It is not retrieved authority and should be verified against current network rules and issuer guides before launch.
### Visa chargeback timeframes and evidence (general framework)
- **Timeframes:** Visa dispute cycles generally allow the cardholder up to 120 calendar days from the transaction date (or from the date the cardholder became aware of the issue, depending on the reason code) to file a dispute. The merchant/acquirer typically has 30 calendar days to respond to a chargeback (representment). Pre-arbitration and arbitration windows add additional time.
- **Reason codes relevant to the portal categories:**
- **Non-receipt:** Visa reason code 13.1 (Merchandise/Services Not Received). Evidence: proof of delivery, tracking, cardholder acknowledgment, or evidence the cardholder used the merchandise/service.
- **Unauthorized use:** Visa reason code 10.4 (Other Fraud — Card-Absent Environment) or 10.5 (Visa Fraud Monitoring Program). Evidence: compelling evidence such as AVS/CVV match, device fingerprint, IP address, prior transaction history, or cardholder authentication (3-D Secure).
- **Duplicate charges:** Visa reason code 12.1 (Duplicate Processing). Evidence: two separate transaction receipts, proof the transactions were distinct, or evidence of a single valid transaction.
- **Defective goods:** Visa reason code 13.3 (Not as Described or Defective Merchandise). Evidence: cardholder’s description of the defect, merchant’s rebuttal, return/refund policy, proof of attempted resolution, or evidence the merchandise matched the description.
- **Compelling evidence:** Visa’s “Compelling Evidence 3.0” framework (for fraud disputes) allows merchants to submit data elements such as IP address, device ID, shipping address, and login history to shift liability.
### Mastercard chargeback timeframes and evidence (general framework)
- **Timeframes:** Mastercard generally allows the cardholder up to 120 calendar days from the transaction date (or from the date the cardholder became aware of the issue) to file a dispute. The merchant/acquirer typically has 45 calendar days to respond to a chargeback (second presentment). Pre-arbitration and arbitration windows add additional time.
- **Reason codes relevant to the portal categories:**
- **Non-receipt:** Mastercard reason code 4855 (Goods/Services Not Provided). Evidence: proof of delivery, tracking, cardholder acknowledgment, or evidence of use.
- **Unauthorized use:** Mastercard reason code 4837 (No Cardholder Authorization) or 4863 (Cardholder Does Not Recognize — Potential Fraud). Evidence: AVS/CVV match, 3-D Secure authentication, device fingerprint, IP address, or prior transaction history.
- **Duplicate charges:** Mastercard reason code 4834 (Duplicate Processing). Evidence: two separate transaction receipts, proof the transactions were distinct, or evidence of a single valid transaction.
- **Defective goods:** Mastercard reason code 4853 (Goods/Services Not as Described or Defective). Evidence: cardholder’s description of the defect, merchant’s rebuttal, return/refund policy, proof of attempted resolution, or evidence the merchandise matched the description.
- **Mastercard Dispute Resolution Initiative (DRI):** Mastercard has been consolidating reason codes and tightening evidence requirements; issuers are encouraged to request more information from cardholders before filing chargebacks.
### U.S. consumer-protection overlay
- **EFTA / Regulation E:** For unauthorized electronic fund transfers (including debit card transactions), consumers must report within 2 business days to limit liability to $50; within 60 days of statement transmittal to limit liability to $500. The portal must capture the date the consumer became aware of the unauthorized use and the date of the statement.
- **FTC Mail/Telephone Order Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 435):** For non-receipt claims, sellers must have a reasonable basis to expect shipment within the stated time (or 30 days if no time is stated). The portal should capture the promised delivery date and actual delivery date.
- **State consumer-protection laws:** Many states have unfair-and-deceptive-acts-and-practices (UDAP) statutes that may impose additional notice, refund, or evidence requirements.
- **TILA / Regulation Z:** For credit card transactions, the Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA) gives consumers 60 days from statement transmittal to dispute billing errors (including duplicate charges and defective goods). The portal must capture the statement date and the date the consumer first disputed the charge.
### Operational and product issues
- **Merchant-of-record status:** Determines who bears chargeback liability and who must respond to network inquiries. If Mosaic Relay is not the merchant of record, it is a service provider and the marketplace/seller bears the response obligation.
- **Evidence sharing:** Network rules and privacy law (GLBA, state privacy laws, GDPR if EU buyers are involved) limit what evidence can be shared with buyers, sellers, and support agents.
- **Language access:** English plus Spanish is required for the initial release; notices and status labels must be translated and culturally appropriate.
- **Accessibility:** WCAG 2.1 AA compliance is likely required for consumer-facing portals under the ADA and state accessibility laws.
- **Escalation when the marketplace does not respond:** The portal must have a default path (e.g., auto-escalate to Mosaic Relay support, auto-refund, or auto-close) to avoid missing network deadlines.

## Viable paths
1. **Launch with a minimum viable dispute portal (MVP) that hard-codes network deadlines and evidence checklists.**
- Build intake forms that map each dispute category to the relevant Visa/Mastercard reason code and required evidence fields.
- Set automated reminders at 7, 3, and 1 days before the network response deadline.
- Default escalation: if the marketplace/seller does not respond within 5 days of the deadline, auto-escalate to Mosaic Relay support with authority to issue a refund or close the case.
- **Pros:** Meets the six-week timeline; reduces deadline-miss risk; creates audit trail.
- **Cons:** Requires upfront legal review of evidence checklists; may need iteration after network feedback.
2. **Delay launch until merchant-of-record status and network coverage are confirmed.**
- Confirm whether Mosaic Relay, the marketplace, or the seller is the merchant of record.
- Confirm which card networks (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover) and regions (U.S. only, or also Latin America/Europe) are in scope.
- **Pros:** Reduces regulatory and network-compliance risk; ensures the portal routes cases to the correct responsible party.
- **Cons:** Likely misses the 2026-10-15 target; may lose the marketplace customer.
3. **Launch with a “service provider only” model and explicit contractual allocation.**
- Position Mosaic Relay as a technology service provider, not the merchant of record.
- Require the marketplace to contractually accept chargeback response obligations and indemnify Mosaic Relay for missed deadlines.
- Build the portal with configurable deadlines and evidence rules so the marketplace can adjust to its own network relationships.
- **Pros:** Shifts legal responsibility to the marketplace; allows faster launch.
- **Cons:** Requires marketplace agreement; may not be acceptable if the marketplace expects Mosaic Relay to manage disputes end-to-end.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Merchant-of-record status:** If Mosaic Relay is the merchant of record, it bears direct chargeback liability and must respond to network inquiries itself. If the marketplace or seller is the merchant of record, Mosaic Relay’s role is limited to routing and evidence collection.
- **Network and region scope:** If the initial release must support Amex, Discover, or non-U.S. networks, additional reason codes, timeframes, and evidence rules apply. If Latin American or European markets are in scope, local consumer-protection law (e.g., EU Consumer Rights Directive, Brazilian CDC) may impose additional notice and refund requirements.
- **Marketplace terms allocation:** If marketplace terms already allocate dispute responsibility to sellers, the portal must route cases to sellers, not the marketplace. If terms are silent, Mosaic Relay may need to default to the marketplace.
- **Escalation default:** If the business decides the portal should auto-refund when the marketplace does not respond, Mosaic Relay needs refund authority and funding. If the default is auto-close, the buyer may file a chargeback anyway, shifting costs to the merchant of record.
- **Buyer type:** The facts state buyers are consumers only. If business purchasers are later included, EFTA/Reg E and FCBA protections may not apply, changing notice and deadline requirements.
