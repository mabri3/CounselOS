# What are the data minimization, storage limitation, and individual-rights obliga

## Question

What are the data minimization, storage limitation, and individual-rights obligations under GDPR/CCPA for a seven-year retention of combined risk data?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. (a fictional B2B payments infrastructure provider) plans to combine onboarding data, transaction history, device signals, sanctions results, dispute records, and payout behavior into a unified risk-scoring model. The score would drive onboarding decisions, transaction review, payout controls, and account deactivation affecting business customers, individual payers, sellers, and contractors. Engineering proposes a shared data model with a ten-week launch timeline and seven-year retention of risk events. Counsel must determine what privacy controls, notices, rights workflows, and governance safeguards are required before legal can approve the launch.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay launch a combined risk-scoring system in ten weeks that retains risk events for seven years, and if so, what specific data minimization, storage limitation, individual rights, and automated-decision safeguards must be implemented to satisfy GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and other applicable privacy regimes given that jurisdictions are not yet scoped and the system will make or support decisions affecting individuals' access to payment services?
## Open questions
- **Jurisdiction scope**: Which specific countries/states will the system cover at launch? This determines whether GDPR Art. 22 (automated decisions), CCPA/CPRA, state ADM laws, or LATAM privacy regimes apply, and whether the seven-year retention is defensible.
- **Automated vs. human-reviewed decisions**: Will the risk score drive fully automated decisions (e.g., automatic account deactivation) or will humans review before action? This triggers different legal obligations under GDPR Art. 22 and emerging US state ADM laws.
- **Vendor contract permissions**: Do current contracts with data sources (identity vendors, sanctions providers, fraud vendors) permit combining their data into a shared risk model, and do they impose retention or use restrictions?
- **FCRA applicability**: Does the combined risk score constitute a "consumer report" under the Fair Credit Reporting Act when used for onboarding or account decisions, which would impose additional notice, consent, and dispute obligations?
- **Controller/processor roles**: When Mosaic Relay processes end-user data (payers, sellers, contractors) on behalf of business customers, who is the controller and who is the processor, and does this vary by use case or jurisdiction?

## Likely rules and issues
### Data minimization and storage limitation
**GDPR (if EU/UK data subjects are involved):**
- **Art. 5(1)(c)**: Personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the purposes. Combining six categories of data (onboarding, transactions, device signals, sanctions, disputes, payouts) into a single risk model requires a documented necessity and proportionality assessment for each data category and each use case (onboarding, transaction review, payout controls, deactivation).
- **Art. 5(1)(e)**: Personal data must be kept "no longer than is necessary." A blanket seven-year retention period for all risk events is likely excessive under GDPR unless justified by specific legal obligations (e.g., AML/CFT record-keeping) or demonstrated business necessity. The plan to delete some categories earlier (cleared sanctions, resolved disputes) is a start, but the default must be the shortest necessary period, not the longest permissible.
- **Art. 25**: Data protection by design and by default requires that the shared data model incorporate minimization, pseudonymization, and access controls from the outset, not as an afterthought.
**CCPA/CPRA (if California residents are involved):**
- **Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.100(c)**: Businesses must collect only what is "reasonably necessary and proportionate" to the disclosed purposes. The CPRA added data minimization as an express requirement.
- **Retention disclosure**: § 1798.100(a)(3) requires disclosure of retention periods or the criteria used to determine them. A seven-year blanket period must be justified and disclosed.
- **Storage limitation**: CPRA regulations (11 CCR § 7002) emphasize that retention must be "reasonably necessary" for the disclosed purpose.
**US state comprehensive privacy laws** (Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, etc.): Most include data minimization and storage limitation principles similar to GDPR, though enforcement and specific requirements vary.
### Individual rights obligations
**GDPR:**
- **Art. 15 (access)**: Individuals have the right to obtain confirmation of processing, access to their data, and information about logic involved in automated decision-making.
- **Art. 16 (rectification)**: Right to correct inaccurate data.
- **Art. 17 (erasure/"right to be forgotten")**: Right to deletion, subject to exceptions (legal obligations, public interest, legal claims). Seven-year retention must accommodate deletion requests unless an exception applies.
- **Art. 18 (restriction)**: Right to restrict processing in certain circumstances.
- **Art. 20 (portability)**: Right to receive data in a structured, machine-readable format.
- **Art. 21 (objection)**: Right to object to processing based on legitimate interests, including profiling.
- **Art. 22 (automated decision-making)**: If the risk score produces legal or similarly significant effects (e.g., account deactivation, denial of onboarding) without meaningful human involvement, individuals have the right not to be subject to purely automated decisions, except with explicit consent, contract necessity, or legal authorization. Safeguards include the right to human intervention, to express a point of view, and to contest the decision.
**CCPA/CPRA:**
- **Right to know/access** (§ 1798.110): Consumers can request categories and specific pieces of personal information collected.
- **Right to delete** (§ 1798.105): Consumers can request deletion, subject to exceptions (e.g., legal obligations, security, fraud prevention).
- **Right to correct** (§ 1798.106): Consumers can request correction of inaccurate information.
- **Right to opt-out of sale/sharing** (§ 1798.120): If risk data is shared with third parties in a way that constitutes a "sale" or "sharing" under CPRA, consumers must be able to opt out.
- **Right to limit use of sensitive personal information** (§ 1798.121): If device signals, precise geolocation, or other data qualify as "sensitive PI," consumers can limit use to what is necessary for the service.
- **Right to non-discrimination** (§ 1798.125): Cannot discriminate against consumers who exercise rights.
**FCRA (if applicable):**
- If the risk score is a "consumer report" used for "permissible purposes" (e.g., credit, employment, insurance, or "legitimate business need"), FCRA imposes notice, consent, accuracy, dispute, and adverse action notice requirements. Combining transaction history, dispute records, and payout behavior to assess risk for onboarding or account decisions could trigger FCRA if the data is used to assess creditworthiness, character, or general reputation.
### Automated decision-making and profiling
**GDPR Art. 22**: If the risk score drives decisions with legal or similarly significant effects (e.g., account deactivation, denial of service) without meaningful human review, Mosaic Relay must:
- Obtain explicit consent, or
- Show the decision is necessary for contract performance, or
- Rely on Union/Member State law authorization.
- Implement safeguards: right to human intervention, right to express a point of view, right to contest the decision.
- Provide meaningful information about the logic involved, the significance, and the envisaged consequences.
**US state ADM laws**: Colorado's Privacy Act (effective July 2023) and Connecticut's law (effective July 2023) regulate "profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects." California's CPRA regulations (still in draft as of early 2023) are expected to address automated decision-making. These laws typically require transparency, risk assessments, and opt-out rights.
### Data-source permissions and vendor contracts
Combining data from multiple sources (identity vendors, sanctions providers, fraud vendors, transaction processors) into a shared risk model requires:
- **Contractual permission**: Each vendor contract must permit the intended use (combining data, risk scoring, retention for seven years). Many vendor contracts restrict use to specific purposes or prohibit combining data across sources.
- **Data minimization**: Vendors should receive only the minimum fields necessary for their function.
- **Processor vs. controller roles**: Clarify whether vendors are processors (acting on Mosaic Relay's instructions) or independent controllers, and ensure data processing agreements (DPAs) are in place where required.
### Controller/processor relationships
When Mosaic Relay processes end-user data (payers, sellers, contractors) on behalf of business customers:
- **GDPR**: Mosaic Relay may be a processor (acting on customer instructions) or a joint controller (if it determines purposes and means). This affects who is responsible for responding to individual rights requests and who must provide notices.
- **CCPA**: Mosaic Relay may be a "service provider" or "contractor" (processing on behalf of a business) or a "third party" (determining purposes and means). Service providers have more limited obligations but must contractually agree to use data only for specified purposes.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Jurisdiction scope**: If the launch is US-only and does not involve California residents, GDPR and CPRA may not apply, reducing the compliance burden. If EU/UK data subjects are involved, GDPR applies and imposes stricter requirements (e.g., Art. 22 automated-decision safeguards, DPIA, data protection by design).
2. **Automated vs. human-reviewed decisions**: If the risk score is always reviewed by a human before action (e.g., before account deactivation), GDPR Art. 22 may not apply, reducing the need for explicit consent or contract-necessity justification. If the score drives fully automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, Art. 22 applies and imposes strict safeguards.
3. **Vendor contract permissions**: If vendor contracts prohibit combining data into a shared risk model or impose conflicting retention or use restrictions, Mosaic Relay may need to renegotiate contracts, obtain consent, or abandon the shared model.
4. **FCRA applicability**: If the risk score is a "consumer report" under FCRA, Mosaic Relay must comply with FCRA's notice, consent, accuracy, dispute, and adverse action requirements, which are more prescriptive than GDPR or CCPA.
5. **Controller/processor roles**: If Mosaic Relay is a processor (acting on customer instructions) rather than a controller, some obligations (e.g., responding to individual rights requests) may shift to the business customer. If Mosaic Relay is a joint controller, it shares responsibility with the customer.
6. **Retention justification**: If Mosaic Relay can demonstrate a specific legal obligation (e.g., AML/CFT record-keeping) or compelling business necessity for seven-year retention, the retention period may be defensible. If not, it must be reduced.
7. **Data sensitivity**: If device signals, precise geolocation, or other data qualify as "sensitive personal information" under CPRA or "special categories" under GDPR, additional protections (e.g., explicit consent, stricter access controls) may be required.
