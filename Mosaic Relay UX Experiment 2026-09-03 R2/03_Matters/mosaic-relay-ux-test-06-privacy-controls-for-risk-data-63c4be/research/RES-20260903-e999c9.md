# What are the FCRA consumer-report and adverse-action obligations for a

## Question

What are the FCRA consumer-report and adverse-action obligations for a combined fraud/risk scoring model used for onboarding, transaction review, payout controls, and account deactivation?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. (a fictional B2B payments infrastructure provider) plans to combine onboarding data, transaction history, device signals, sanctions results, dispute records, and payout behavior into a shared fraud/risk scoring model. The score would drive onboarding decisions, transaction review, payout controls, and account deactivation affecting individual payers, sellers, and contractors. Engineering proposes a ten-week launch timeline with a seven-year retention period for risk events. Counsel must determine whether this combined scoring model triggers FCRA consumer-report and adverse-action obligations before engineering freezes the data model.
## Decision question
Does Mosaic Relay's planned combination of onboarding, transaction, device, sanctions, dispute, and payout data into a shared risk score—used to make onboarding, transaction, payout, and deactivation decisions about individuals—constitute a "consumer report" under FCRA, and if so, what adverse-action notice, disclosure, and dispute obligations must be built into the product before the ten-week launch freeze?
## Open questions
- **Automated vs. human review**: Will the risk score drive fully automated decisions (e.g., automatic account deactivation) or will a human review each decision before action? This determines whether GDPR Art. 22 and state ADM laws apply, and affects FCRA adverse-action analysis.
- **Jurisdiction scope at launch**: The matter states "Global at launch" but exact jurisdictions are not yet scoped. FCRA applies only to U.S. consumers; EU/UK GDPR and LATAM laws impose different (and in some cases stricter) automated-decision and profiling rules.
- **Vendor contract permissions**: Do current contracts with sanctions-data providers, identity vendors, and fraud vendors permit combining their data into a shared scoring model? Many vendor contracts restrict secondary use or require separate consent for scoring.
- **Data-source permissions for device signals**: Are device signals collected under a privacy policy that permits use for risk scoring, or is this a secondary use requiring new notice or consent?
- **FCRA "consumer report" characterization**: Will the score be used to determine eligibility for credit, employment, insurance, or other FCRA-covered purposes, or is it strictly for fraud prevention and operational risk? Fraud-prevention use may fall outside FCRA, but the line is fact-specific.

## Likely rules and issues
### FCRA consumer-report analysis
**No external authority retrieved.**
The Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) regulates "consumer reports"—communications of information bearing on a consumer's creditworthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living that are used or expected to be used for credit, employment, insurance, or other permissible purposes.
**Key issue**: A fraud/risk score used for onboarding, transaction review, payout controls, and account deactivation may or may not be a "consumer report" depending on:
1. **Purpose**: If the score is used solely for fraud prevention and operational risk (e.g., detecting stolen identities, preventing account takeover), it likely falls outside FCRA. If it is used to assess creditworthiness, likelihood of default, or general "trustworthiness" for financial relationships, it may be a consumer report.
2. **Data sources**: Combining transaction history, dispute records, and payout behavior begins to resemble credit or financial-behavior data. If the score incorporates data traditionally used in credit decisions (payment history, financial disputes), FCRA risk increases.
3. **Decision impact**: Account deactivation and payout holds that effectively deny someone access to a financial platform could be characterized as adverse actions affecting the consumer's ability to obtain goods/services, which may trigger FCRA if the underlying data is a consumer report.
**Adverse-action obligations if FCRA applies**:
- Provide the consumer with notice of the adverse action (denial, deactivation, payout hold).
- Provide the name, address, and phone number of the consumer reporting agency (CRA) that supplied the report.
- Notify the consumer of their right to obtain a free copy of the report and to dispute inaccuracies.
- If Mosaic Relay is assembling the report itself (rather than obtaining it from a third-party CRA), it may be acting as a CRA and subject to additional obligations (accuracy, dispute resolution, file disclosure).
### GDPR Art. 22 and automated decision-making
If the score drives fully automated decisions (no human review) that produce legal or similarly significant effects (e.g., account deactivation, denial of payouts), GDPR Art. 22 restricts this unless:
- The individual has given explicit consent, or
- The processing is necessary for contract performance and safeguards are in place (right to human intervention, to express a point of view, to contest the decision).
The matter facts state the score is "fully explainable to individuals and business customers" and that individuals have "full access/correction/deletion workflows," which supports GDPR compliance, but the automated-decision question remains open.
### State ADM laws (CCPA/CPRA, Colorado, Connecticut, etc.)
Several U.S. states have enacted or proposed automated-decision-making laws that require:
- Notice when ADM is used.
- Opt-out rights for certain ADM uses.
- Impact assessments or audits.
The matter facts state "notice plus opt-out for certain uses," which aligns with these requirements, but the specific state scope is not yet determined.
### Data minimization and retention
The seven-year retention plan for risk events conflicts with GDPR and CCPA data-minimization principles unless justified by legal holds, regulatory requirements, or operational necessity. The matter facts note that "some categories deleted earlier (e.g., cleared sanctions, resolved disputes)," which is a good start, but the default seven-year period needs justification.
### Vendor contract and data-source permissions
Combining data from sanctions providers, identity vendors, and fraud vendors into a shared model requires contract review. Many vendor agreements:
- Restrict use to specific purposes (e.g., "identity verification only").
- Prohibit combining data with other sources.
- Require separate consent or notice for scoring or profiling.
The matter facts state vendor contracts are "not yet reviewed / unknown," which is a material gap.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Fraud-prevention-only design (FCRA-safe)
**Structure**: Limit the risk score to fraud prevention and operational risk (e.g., detecting stolen identities, account takeover, sanctions violations). Do not use it to assess creditworthiness, likelihood of default, or general financial trustworthiness.
**Controls**:
- Document that the score is not used for credit, employment, insurance, or housing decisions.
- Exclude data fields traditionally associated with credit decisions (e.g., payment history for credit obligations).
- Provide adverse-action notices as a best practice even if not legally required, to build trust and reduce litigation risk.
**Pros**: Avoids FCRA consumer-report obligations; simpler compliance posture.
**Cons**: May limit the business value of the score; requires careful scoping to avoid "mission creep" into credit-like decisions.
### Path 2: Full FCRA compliance
**Structure**: Treat the combined score as a consumer report and comply with FCRA adverse-action, disclosure, and dispute obligations.
**Controls**:
- Provide adverse-action notices when the score leads to denial, deactivation, or payout holds.
- If Mosaic Relay is assembling the report itself, comply with CRA obligations (accuracy, dispute resolution, file disclosure).
- Obtain consumer consent where required.
- Implement FCRA-compliant dispute workflows.
**Pros**: Allows broader use of the score, including credit-like decisions.
**Cons**: Significant compliance burden; may require registering as a CRA; increases litigation and regulatory risk.
### Path 3: Hybrid—fraud score + separate credit/eligibility score
**Structure**: Maintain two separate models:
1. A fraud-prevention score (FCRA-safe) for onboarding, transaction review, and sanctions screening.
2. A separate credit/eligibility score (FCRA-compliant) for payout controls and account deactivation if those decisions involve credit-like assessments.
**Controls**:
- Segregate data and models to prevent commingling.
- Apply FCRA controls only to the credit/eligibility score.
- Document the business purpose and legal basis for each score.
**Pros**: Balances business needs with compliance; limits FCRA scope.
**Cons**: Engineering complexity; requires clear internal governance to prevent scope creep.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Automated vs. human review**: If every decision is human-reviewed, GDPR Art. 22 and some state ADM laws may not apply, reducing compliance burden. If fully automated, additional safeguards are required.
2. **Jurisdiction scope**: If launch is U.S.-only, GDPR and LATAM laws drop out. If EU/UK is included, GDPR Art. 22 and data-minimization rules become controlling.
3. **Vendor contract permissions**: If vendor contracts prohibit combining data or using it for scoring, the entire shared-model approach may need to be redesigned or renegotiated.
4. **Data fields used**: If the score incorporates payment history, credit-like behavior, or financial disputes, FCRA risk increases. If it is limited to device signals, sanctions hits, and fraud indicators, FCRA risk decreases.
5. **Decision impact**: If account deactivation or payout holds effectively deny individuals access to essential financial services, regulators may characterize the score as a consumer report even if the intended use is fraud prevention.

8. **Model-governance documentation**: Document model explainability, bias testing, and audit trails. Ensure individuals and business customers can obtain explanations of adverse decisions.
