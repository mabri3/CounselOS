# What are the GDPR Art. 22 and CCPA/CPRA automated-decision-making and

## Question

What are the GDPR Art. 22 and CCPA/CPRA automated-decision-making and profiling obligations for a risk score used across these four use cases?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. (a fictional B2B payments infrastructure provider) plans to combine onboarding data, transaction history, device signals, sanctions results, dispute records, and payout behavior into a shared risk-scoring model. The score would drive onboarding, transaction review, payout controls, and account deactivation across a global footprint that is not yet jurisdiction-scoped. Engineering proposes a ten-week launch, and legal must deliver a go/no-go package before the shared model and launch plan freeze. The lawyer has already recorded a decision not to approve the launch as proposed and to approve only a gated, purpose-limited launch after specified controls are completed and verified.
## Decision question
Which automated-decision-making and profiling obligations under GDPR Art. 22 and CCPA/CPRA apply to each of the four risk-score use cases (onboarding, transaction review, payout controls, account deactivation), and what specific safeguards — human review, notice, opt-out, explanation, and access/correction rights — must Mosaic Relay implement before the ten-week launch to lawfully deploy the score in each jurisdiction branch?
## Open questions
- **Automated vs. human-reviewed decisions per use case** — The matter records this as "not yet determined." Whether each of the four use cases involves solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects is the single largest fork: it determines whether GDPR Art. 22's prohibition (and its exceptions) applies, and whether CCPA/CPRA's ADMT rules are triggered.
- **Jurisdiction scoping** — "Global at launch; exact jurisdictions not yet scoped." The answer changes materially depending on whether EU/UK data subjects are in scope at launch (GDPR Art. 22, Art. 13(2)(f), Art. 14(2)(g), Art. 15(1)(h), DPIA under Art. 35) versus US-only (CCPA/CPRA ADMT regulations, state ADM laws like Colorado's SB 24-205, and FCRA analysis).
- **Vendor contract permissions** — Whether current vendor contracts and data-source permissions permit combining data into a shared risk model is recorded as "not yet reviewed / unknown." If any vendor contract restricts secondary use or model training, the data flow for that source must be redesigned or the contract renegotiated before launch.
- **FCRA characterization** — Whether the combined risk score constitutes a "consumer report" under the Fair Credit Reporting Act when used for onboarding or account deactivation decisions. If it does, permissible-purpose, adverse-action notice, and dispute obligations apply that are entirely separate from GDPR/CCPA.
- **Controller/processor allocation** — Whether Mosaic Relay acts as a controller, joint controller, or processor with respect to business customers' end-user data (payers, sellers, contractors). This determines which entity bears Art. 22 and CPRA ADMT compliance obligations and what contractual terms are required.

## Likely rules and issues
### GDPR Art. 22 — Automated individual decision-making, including profiling
**Core rule:** A data subject has the right not to be subject to a decision based *solely* on automated processing, including profiling, which produces *legal effects* or *similarly significantly affects* them (Art. 22(1)).
**Application to the four use cases:**
| Use case | Likely "legal or similarly significant effect"? | Art. 22 analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Onboarding** (denying a merchant or platform access to payment infrastructure) | Likely yes — denial of access to a service essential for the applicant's business operations | If solely automated, prohibited unless an exception applies |
| **Transaction review** (blocking or flagging individual transactions) | Possibly — depends on whether blocks are frequent, consequential, or merely routing to manual review | If transactions are auto-declined without human review and the effect is significant (e.g., a contractor cannot receive payment), Art. 22 is engaged |
| **Payout controls** (holding, delaying, or limiting payouts) | Likely yes — withholding funds someone has earned is a significant financial effect | Same analysis as onboarding |
| **Account deactivation** (terminating a user's access) | Almost certainly yes — termination of a business relationship and loss of service access | Strongest case for Art. 22 applicability |
**Exceptions (Art. 22(2)):** Even if Art. 22 applies, the processing may proceed if it is:
- (a) Necessary for entering into or performing a contract — plausible for transaction review and payout controls where real-time fraud screening is arguably necessary, but the EDPB and WP29 guidance interpret "necessary" narrowly (the controller must show the decision cannot reasonably be made otherwise);
- (b) Authorized by Union or Member State law — some AML/fraud-prevention mandates may qualify, but this requires jurisdiction-specific analysis;
- (c) Based on explicit consent — possible but creates withdrawal and granularity obligations.
**Safeguards required when relying on (a) or (c) (Art. 22(3)):** At minimum, the right to obtain human intervention, to express one's point of view, and to contest the decision.
**Transparency obligations:** Arts. 13(2)(f) and 14(2)(g) require informing data subjects of the existence of automated decision-making, meaningful information about the logic involved, and the significance and envisaged consequences. Art. 15(1)(h) extends this to access requests.
**DPIA:** Art. 35(3)(a) mandates a data protection impact assessment for systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing, including profiling, on which decisions are based that produce legal or similarly significant effects. This program squarely triggers a DPIA.
**Meaningful human review:** If a human reviews before action, Art. 22 may not apply — but the review must be genuine. WP29/EDPB guidance states the reviewer must have the authority and competence to change the decision, must consider all relevant data, and must not merely rubber-stamp the automated output. A perfunctory "confirm" button does not remove the decision from Art. 22 scope.
### CCPA/CPRA — Automated decision-making technology (ADMT)
**Current state:** The CPRA amended the CCPA to authorize the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) to issue regulations governing automated decision-making technology. The CPPA proposed ADMT regulations (most recently revised in 2024–2025) that would:
- Grant consumers the right to **opt out** of ADMT used for decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects (financial, housing, employment, education, healthcare, etc.);
- Grant consumers the right to **access** information about the business's use of ADMT, including the logic, intended output, and how the output was used;
- Require **pre-use notice** describing the ADMT's purpose, the categories of personal information used, and the consumer's opt-out and access rights;
- Require businesses to conduct **risk assessments** for ADMT use.
**Application to Mosaic Relay:** If California consumers (payers, sellers, contractors) are in scope, and if the risk score drives decisions with significant effects (denying onboarding, holding payouts, deactivating accounts), the ADMT regulations would require opt-out mechanisms, pre-use notice, access rights, and risk assessments. The regulations are not yet final as of the matter date, but the trajectory is clear and the ten-week launch window means the final rules may be in effect or imminent.
**CCPA/CPRA profiling and sharing:** The combination of data from multiple sources into a shared risk model may constitute "sharing" under CPRA if any vendor receives personal information for cross-context behavioral advertising or if the arrangement does not fit a service-provider exception. This is unlikely for pure fraud prevention but must be confirmed per vendor.
### Other material frameworks
- **FCRA:** If the risk score is used to determine eligibility for payment services and is assembled from data sources that include third-party data, it may constitute a "consumer report." Adverse-action notices, permissible-purpose requirements, and dispute/investigation obligations would follow. This is a separate and potentially more operationally demanding regime than GDPR or CCPA.
- **State ADM laws:** Colorado's SB 24-205 (effective February 2026) imposes duty-of-care, impact-assessment, and disclosure obligations on developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems making consequential decisions. Other states are considering similar legislation. The "global at launch" scope means these must be tracked.
- **ECOA/Reg B:** If the risk score is used in any credit-adjacent decision (e.g., payout timing that functions as credit extension), adverse-action notice requirements may apply.
- **GLBA:** If any data source includes financial institution data subject to GLBA, reuse restrictions apply.

## Viable paths
### Path A — Human-in-the-loop for all four use cases (recommended starting position)
Structure every decision so that a trained human reviewer with authority to override makes the final call before any action is taken (onboarding denial, transaction block, payout hold, account deactivation). This removes the decision from GDPR Art. 22's prohibition (though not from all GDPR obligations — profiling, transparency, and DPIA requirements still apply). It also reduces CCPA/CPRA ADMT exposure, though the CPPA's proposed rules may still apply to ADMT that *substantially assists* human decisions.
**Trade-off:** Operationally expensive. Requires staffing, training, documentation of reviewer independence, and audit trails proving genuine review. May slow onboarding and transaction processing.
### Path B — Rely on Art. 22(2)(a) contractual necessity for transaction review and payout controls; human review for onboarding and deactivation
Argue that real-time automated fraud screening is necessary for contract performance (the payment service cannot function without it). Implement Art. 22(3) safeguards (human intervention on request, right to express point of view, right to contest). Apply full human review to onboarding denial and account deactivation, where the "necessity" argument is weakest and the impact is most severe.
**Trade-off:** The contractual-necessity exception is interpreted narrowly by EU regulators. The EDPB has stated that fraud prevention alone does not automatically make automated decisions "necessary" — the controller must show that less intrusive means are not reasonably available. This path carries enforcement risk in the EU/UK.
### Path C — Explicit consent for automated decisions
Obtain explicit consent from data subjects for automated risk-scoring decisions. This satisfies Art. 22(2)(c) but creates withdrawal obligations, granularity requirements (consent must be specific to each use case), and the practical problem that refusing consent cannot be a pretext for denying service unless the processing is genuinely optional.
**Trade-off:** Consent fatigue, withdrawal management, and the risk that consent is deemed invalid because it is a condition of service (GDPR Art. 7(4) — consent is not freely given if the contract is conditional on unnecessary processing).
### Path D — US-only launch with EU/UK deferred
Launch in the US only, defer EU/UK until jurisdiction-specific compliance is built. This avoids GDPR Art. 22 entirely at launch but still requires CCPA/CPRA ADMT compliance (if California consumers are in scope), FCRA analysis, and state ADM law tracking.
**Trade-off:** Contradicts the stated "global at launch" plan. Requires engineering to build jurisdiction-gating into the shared data model from day one, which is architecturally easier than retrofitting but still a scope change.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Whether any use case is genuinely human-reviewed today.** If engineering confirms that all four use cases already include meaningful human review, the GDPR Art. 22 prohibition does not apply (though transparency, DPIA, and profiling obligations remain). This would significantly reduce the compliance burden and could accelerate the launch timeline.
2. **Whether California consumers are in scope at launch.** If the initial user base is B2B-only with no California-resident individual payers, sellers, or contractors, CCPA/CPRA ADMT obligations may not apply at launch (though they would need to be built before consumer-facing expansion).
3. **Whether any data source is a consumer reporting agency or includes credit-header data.** If so, FCRA analysis becomes urgent and may impose the most operationally demanding obligations (permissible purpose, adverse action, dispute investigation, file disclosure).
4. **Whether the CPPA finalizes ADMT regulations before the ten-week launch.** If final rules are published and take effect during the launch window, the compliance package must include opt-out mechanisms, pre-use notice, and risk assessments that are not yet fully specified.
5. **Whether vendor contracts permit secondary use of data for model training.** If any vendor contract restricts use to "fraud screening for the specific transaction" and prohibits aggregation or model training, that data source must be excluded or the contract renegotiated — potentially delaying the launch or reducing model accuracy.

## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Confirm with engineering whether each of the four use cases involves solely automated decisions or human review.** Request a decision-flow diagram showing where human review occurs, what the reviewer sees, whether the reviewer can override, and how overrides are logged. This is the single highest-impact fact.
2. **Scope jurisdictions.** Obtain from the business a definitive list of launch jurisdictions. If EU/UK is in scope, commission a DPIA immediately. If California is in scope, begin ADMT compliance design.
3. **Review vendor contracts.** For each data source (identity vendors, sanctions-data providers, device-signal vendors, fraud vendors), confirm whether the contract permits combining data into a shared risk model, using data for model training, and retaining derived scores. Flag any contract that restricts secondary use.
4. **FCRA analysis.** Determine whether the risk score, when used for onboarding or deactivation, constitutes a consumer report. If any data source is a CRA or the score is used for eligibility determination, engage FCRA counsel.
5. **Monitor CPPA ADMT rulemaking.** Check the CPPA's website for the current status of proposed ADMT regulations. If final rules are published, map the compliance timeline against the ten-week launch.
6. **Draft the Art. 22(3) safeguard workflow.** Even if human review is the primary path, design the human-intervention, point-of-view, and contestation workflow now — it will be required regardless of which exception is relied upon.
