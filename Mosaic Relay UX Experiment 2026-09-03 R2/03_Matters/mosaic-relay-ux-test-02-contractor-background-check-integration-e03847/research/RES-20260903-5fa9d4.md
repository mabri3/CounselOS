# EEOC disparate-impact guidance on criminal-history screening

## Question

EEOC disparate-impact guidance on criminal-history screening

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A software platform wants to use Mosaic Relay to pay delivery contractors, with a background-check vendor screening contractors for identity, criminal history, sanctions, and work eligibility before the first payout. The platform would receive a “clear,” “review,” or “fail” result, while Mosaic Relay processes identity data and payout instructions. Counsel is involved because criminal-history screening of contractors creates disparate-impact discrimination exposure under Title VII, alongside FCRA, state fair-chance, privacy, and payments issues. Target launch is 2026-10-29, eight weeks out, and the contractors are independent contractors of the platform.
## Decision question
Before the 2026-10-29 launch, should legal permit the platform to use criminal-history screening results (clear/review/fail) to gate contractor payouts, and if so, what individualized-assessment, notice, and data-minimization controls must the platform and Mosaic Relay implement to avoid disparate-impact liability under EEOC guidance — given that the contractors are independent contractors, the final decision-maker is undecided, and Mosaic Relay would otherwise receive screening data it does not need?
## Open questions
- Who makes the final decision on a “review” or “fail” result — the platform, Mosaic Relay, or the vendor? This determines who bears Title VII disparate-impact exposure and who must run any individualized assessment.
- Are the background-check reports “consumer reports” under FCRA and is the vendor a consumer reporting agency? If yes, the full adverse-action apparatus applies; if the vendor is not a CRA, disparate-impact risk remains but the procedural overlay changes.
- Which states will screening and payouts cover at launch? State and local fair-chance laws (e.g., California, New York City) impose stricter limits on criminal-history use than federal guidance, and some apply explicitly to independent contractors.
- What criminal-history data does the vendor actually return (arrests vs. convictions, lookback period, offense categories)? EEOC guidance treats arrests and convictions differently, and blanket exclusions are the highest-risk pattern.
- Does the platform’s use of screening results create an employment relationship or joint-employer exposure with Mosaic Relay, given that Mosaic Relay processes identity data and payout instructions?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-only analysis based on established legal frameworks.
### 1. EEOC disparate-impact framework for criminal-history screening
The controlling framework is the EEOC’s 2012 Enforcement Guidance on Consideration of Arrest and Conviction Records in Employment Decisions Under Title VII. Although the guidance addresses “employment,” its principles extend to independent-contractor selection when the screening operates as a gateway to work opportunities, and several state and local laws explicitly cover contractors.
**Core principles:**
- **Disparate-impact liability** arises when a neutral criminal-history policy disproportionately screens out a protected group (race, national origin) and the policy is not job-related and consistent with business necessity.
- **Arrests vs. convictions:** An arrest alone does not establish criminal conduct and should not be the basis for exclusion. A conviction is more reliable but still requires analysis.
- **Blanket exclusions are disfavored.** A policy that excludes all persons with any criminal record, or broad categories, is the highest-risk pattern and difficult to defend.
- **Job-related and consistent with business necessity** can be shown in two ways:
1. **Validation study** — the employer validates the screen per the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (rarely practical for a platform launch).
2. **Targeted screen + individualized assessment** — the screen considers at least (a) the nature and gravity of the offense, (b) the time elapsed since the offense or completion of sentence, and (c) the nature of the job/contract work. For those screened out, the employer provides notice and an opportunity to demonstrate why the exclusion should not apply.
**Individualized assessment** is the practical safe harbor. It requires:
- Notice to the individual that they were screened out because of a criminal record.
- An opportunity to respond with evidence of rehabilitation, mitigating circumstances, or inaccuracy.
- Consideration of that response before a final decision.
### 2. Application to this matter
- **The platform is the likely “employer” equivalent.** It selects and onboards contractors, sets the screening criteria, and receives the clear/review/fail result. It bears the primary disparate-impact risk.
- **Mosaic Relay’s exposure is secondary but real.** If Mosaic Relay receives full criminal-history data or makes payout decisions based on screening results, it could be characterized as a joint decision-maker or as using consumer reports for an impermissible purpose. The current design — Mosaic Relay receives only status codes — is the right risk posture.
- **Delivery contractors:** The work involves access to customers’ homes, vehicles, and goods. This provides a stronger business-necessity argument for some screening than, say, remote software work, but it does not justify blanket exclusions. The screen must still be targeted to offenses that actually predict risk in delivery work (e.g., violent offenses, theft, DUI for driving roles) and must include individualized assessment.
- **“Review” and “fail” results:** If the platform treats “fail” as automatic exclusion without individualized assessment, that is the highest-risk pattern. If “review” triggers a human look but “fail” does not, the process is still vulnerable.
### 3. Interaction with FCRA and state law
- If the vendor is a CRA and the reports are consumer reports, FCRA adverse-action requirements (pre-adverse notice, copy of report, Summary of Rights, waiting period, final adverse-action notice) apply. These procedural steps overlap with but do not replace the EEOC individualized-assessment obligation.
- State and local fair-chance laws may impose stricter rules: some prohibit asking about criminal history until after a conditional offer, some limit lookback periods, some require specific notice language, and some explicitly cover independent contractors (e.g., New York City’s Fair Chance Act, California’s Fair Chance Act).

## Viable paths
### Path A — Platform owns screening and decision; Mosaic Relay receives only status codes (recommended)
- The platform contracts directly with the screening vendor, certifies permissible purpose, and owns the adverse-action workflow.
- The platform implements a targeted screen (nature/time/nature-of-work) and individualized assessment for all “review” and “fail” results.
- Mosaic Relay receives only the minimum status code needed to release or hold payout, with no access to underlying criminal-history data.
- **Pros:** Cleanest allocation of Title VII and FCRA risk; Mosaic Relay avoids becoming a user of consumer reports; aligns with data-minimization posture.
- **Cons:** Requires the platform to build or buy an individualized-assessment workflow; may slow contractor onboarding.
### Path B — Mosaic Relay receives full reports and participates in decision-making
- Mosaic Relay would need to certify permissible purpose, implement its own adverse-action workflow, and accept joint Title VII exposure.
- **Pros:** More control for Mosaic Relay over payout risk.
- **Cons:** Significant regulatory burden; Mosaic Relay becomes a user of consumer reports; high disparate-impact exposure if it applies its own screening criteria; inconsistent with the company’s stated risk posture.
### Path C — No criminal-history screening at launch; defer to post-launch
- Launch with identity verification and sanctions screening only; add criminal-history screening after the platform builds compliant workflows.
- **Pros:** Eliminates disparate-impact and FCRA risk for launch; fastest path to market.
- **Cons:** Platform may reject this as insufficient for delivery contractors; may create competitive or safety concerns.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If the vendor is not a CRA** (e.g., it provides only raw public-record data without assembling or evaluating), FCRA adverse-action requirements may not apply, but EEOC disparate-impact principles still do. The platform would still need individualized assessment.
- **If the platform operates in a state with a strict fair-chance law covering contractors** (e.g., New York City, California, Illinois), the permissible screening window, notice content, and waiting periods may be more restrictive than federal guidance.
- **If the delivery contractors are reclassified as employees** (or are found to be employees under state ABC tests), Title VII applies directly and the EEOC guidance is squarely on point. The current independent-contractor classification does not eliminate risk but changes the legal theory.
- **If the vendor’s “fail” result is based on arrest records only** (no conviction), the screen is very difficult to defend under EEOC guidance.
- **If Mosaic Relay’s payout hold based on a “fail” result is automatic and final** (no human review, no appeal), Mosaic Relay may be characterized as making an employment-like decision, increasing joint-liability exposure.
