# State ban-the-box and fair-chance hiring laws applicable to independent-contract

## Question

State ban-the-box and fair-chance hiring laws applicable to independent-contractor screening

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A software platform wants to use Mosaic Relay to pay delivery contractors and proposes adding a background-check vendor to screen contractors for identity, criminal history, sanctions, and work eligibility before the first payout. The platform would receive a “clear,” “review,” or “fail” result, while Mosaic Relay would process identity data and payout instructions. Counsel is involved because the screening workflow raises consumer-reporting, privacy, discrimination, and payments risks, and the target launch is eight weeks away on 2026-10-29. The key unresolved decision is who owns the final screening decision and the adverse-action workflow.
## Decision question
Before the 2026-10-29 launch, should Mosaic Relay permit the platform to use a background-check vendor to screen delivery contractors before first payout, and if so, what exact product behavior, consent language, notices, dispute rights, access controls, and customer contract terms must be required so the platform—not Mosaic Relay—can lawfully make and communicate the final decision?
## Open questions
- Which states will screening and payouts cover at launch?
- Are the vendor’s reports “consumer reports” under the FCRA, and is the vendor acting as a consumer reporting agency?
- Who makes the final decision on a “review” or “fail” result, and who owns pre-adverse-action, adverse-action, and dispute/reinvestigation steps?
- What exact reports, data sources, match logic, sanctions lists, work-eligibility source, and retention periods will the vendor use?
- Will Mosaic Relay receive only status codes and minimal decision metadata, or any full report content?
## Likely rules/issues
### 1) Ban-the-box and fair-chance laws may apply even though the workers are independent contractors
The biggest legal mistake would be assuming these laws only apply to W-2 hiring. Many state and local fair-chance laws are written around “employment,” but some extend to contractors, gig workers, or workers placed through platforms, and even where they do not, the same screening process can still create discrimination, consumer-reporting, and unfair-practice risk.
For this matter, the likely issue is not just whether a statute literally says “employee.” It is whether the platform’s contractor onboarding process is treated as a covered opportunity under the specific state or local law where the contractor works.
### 2) Timing rules can change the product flow
A common fair-chance requirement is that criminal-history inquiries must wait until after a conditional offer or until after an initial qualification decision. In this setup, the practical equivalent is: the platform should not screen for criminal history before it has otherwise decided the contractor is eligible to onboard and receive payouts, subject only to the background check.
That means the product flow likely needs to separate:
- identity / sanctions / work-eligibility checks, and
- criminal-history screening,
rather than running everything at once before first payout.
### 3) Individualized assessment may be required before a “fail” becomes final
Many fair-chance regimes require some form of individualized assessment before criminal history can be used adversely. The product should not allow a raw “review” or “fail” code to automatically block payout or onboarding without a human review step by the platform.
That review should consider, at minimum:
- the nature and gravity of the offense,
- the time elapsed,
- the nature of the contractor role, and
- any evidence of rehabilitation or mitigating context.
### 4) Notice and waiting-period rules are likely to control the workflow
Even apart from the FCRA, state and local fair-chance laws often require:
- a pre-adverse-action notice,
- a copy of the report or a summary of the disqualifying information,
- the specific legal or policy basis for concern,
- time to respond or dispute, and
- a final adverse-action notice.
Those obligations overlap with, but do not replace, FCRA obligations if the reports are consumer reports.
### 5) Some jurisdictions restrict what can be considered at all
The launch-state list matters because some jurisdictions limit or prohibit use of:
- arrests not leading to conviction,
- sealed or expunged records,
- older convictions,
- certain misdemeanors,
- non-conviction records, or
- records unrelated to the role.
If the vendor’s report includes categories a launch state does not permit to be used, the product must suppress or ignore them.
### 6) Pay-to-screen and cost-shifting rules may matter
Some jurisdictions restrict requiring workers to pay for their own background checks or otherwise shift screening costs to them. If the platform or Mosaic Relay flow passes screening costs to contractors, that needs a separate state-by-state review.
### 7) Discrimination risk remains even where a statute does not literally apply
Even if a particular state fair-chance law does not cover independent contractors, criminal-history screening can still create disparate-impact risk. For delivery contractors, that risk is material because broad exclusions can disproportionately affect protected groups. Legal should require a documented, role-related rationale for any criminal-history rule.
### 8) Payments and consumer-reporting issues are linked
If Mosaic Relay receives more than the minimum status code, or if Mosaic Relay effectively decides who gets paid based on the report, it could be pulled deeper into the consumer-reporting and adverse-action workflow. The safer structure is for Mosaic Relay to receive only the minimum decision metadata needed to operate payout controls.
## Viable paths
### Path A — Platform owns screening and adverse action end-to-end
This is the cleanest path.
- Platform is the user of the report.
- Platform obtains consent and makes the final decision.
- Platform sends pre-adverse and adverse-action notices.
- Platform handles contractor disputes and reinvestigation communications.
- Vendor reinvestigates and corrects errors.
- Mosaic Relay receives only a status code such as eligible / ineligible / pending, plus minimal metadata needed for payout operations.
This path best protects Mosaic Relay’s role as payments infrastructure.
### Path B — Shared workflow, with Mosaic Relay hosting notices or dispute intake
This is possible, but it increases Mosaic Relay’s operational and legal exposure.
If Mosaic Relay hosts any contractor-facing notices, dispute intake, or decision-support logic, legal should require:
- exact scripts and templates,
- state-specific timing controls,
- audit logs,
- a documented human-review step,
- a pause on adverse action during reinvestigation, and
- contract language making the platform the decision-maker unless a specific legal review says otherwise.
### Path C — Mosaic Relay receives full reports or makes the final call
This is the highest-risk path and should be treated as a launch blocker unless there is a specific, documented reason.
If Mosaic Relay receives full criminal, identity, or eligibility reports, or if Mosaic Relay decides who is cleared for payout, then Mosaic Relay may become entangled in:
- consumer-reporting obligations,
- state fair-chance compliance,
- discrimination analysis, and
- contractor dispute handling.
That is not consistent with the current facts or the company’s preferred role.
## Facts that could change the answer
- If the vendor is not a CRA and the reports are not FCRA consumer reports, the FCRA analysis changes, but state fair-chance and discrimination issues remain.
- If the launch states include jurisdictions with contractor-specific fair-chance rules, the product flow may need to delay criminal-history screening until after a conditional onboarding decision.
- If the platform wants to use a single combined report for identity, sanctions, work eligibility, and criminal history, the workflow may need to be split so criminal-history screening happens later.
- If contractors can dispute results directly to Mosaic Relay, then Mosaic Relay needs a formal intake, routing, and escalation process.
- If any automated score or rule affects payout, legal should require human review and a documented override path.
- If the platform wants contractors to pay for the screening, state pay-to-screen rules may block that design.
## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, legal should verify these items against the actual vendor documents and launch-state list:
1. **Vendor classification and report contents**
- Confirm whether the vendor is a CRA.
- Obtain the exact report types, data sources, match logic, sanctions lists, work-eligibility source, and retention periods.
2. **Launch-state matrix**
- Build a state-by-state matrix for:
- ban-the-box / fair-chance coverage,
- whether independent contractors are covered,
- timing rules,
- notice content,
- waiting periods,
- dispute/reinvestigation SLAs,
- permissible report contents, and
- pay-to-screen restrictions.
3. **Workflow ownership**
- Confirm in writing who:
- obtains consent,
- makes the final decision,
- sends pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notices,
- handles disputes,
- pauses payout during reinvestigation, and
- keeps the audit trail.
4. **Data minimization**
- Confirm Mosaic Relay receives only the minimum status code and decision metadata needed for payout operations.
- Confirm no full criminal, identity, or eligibility report is exposed to Mosaic Relay unless a documented legal purpose requires it.
5. **Contract terms**
- Require customer contract terms covering:
- FCRA allocation and certifications,
- permissible purpose,
- state-law compliance,
- adverse-action ownership,
- dispute handling,
- vendor flow-downs,
- confidentiality,
- security,
- deletion,
- audit rights,
- indemnity,
- insurance,
- regulatory cooperation, and
- no secondary use of screening data.
