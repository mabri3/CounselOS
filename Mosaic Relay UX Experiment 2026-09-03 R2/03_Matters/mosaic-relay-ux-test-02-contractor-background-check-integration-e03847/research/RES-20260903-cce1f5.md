# FCRA adverse-action notice requirements for consumer-report users

## Question

FCRA adverse-action notice requirements for consumer-report users

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A software platform wants to use Mosaic Relay to pay delivery contractors and proposes adding a background-check vendor to screen contractors for identity, criminal history, sanctions, and work eligibility before the first payout. The platform would receive a “clear,” “review,” or “fail” result, while Mosaic Relay would process identity data and payout instructions. Counsel is involved because the screening may trigger FCRA consumer-reporting duties, state background-check limits, discrimination risk, privacy/data-minimization obligations, and payments-regulatory boundary questions. Target launch is 2026-10-29, eight weeks out, and the matter is marked high priority and high risk.
## Decision question
Before the 2026-10-29 launch, should Mosaic Relay permit the platform to use background-check results to delay, block, or condition contractor payouts, and if so, what exact product behavior, consent language, FCRA/state notices, dispute rights, access controls, and customer contract terms must be required so the platform—not Mosaic Relay unless expressly agreed—owns the final decision and adverse-action workflow?
## Open questions
- Are the vendor reports “consumer reports” under the FCRA, and is the vendor acting as a consumer reporting agency for these searches?
- Who is the FCRA “user” of the report and who makes the final decision on a “review” or “fail” result—the platform, Mosaic Relay, or both?
- Which states are in launch scope, and which state ban-the-box, fair-chance, notice-timing, and pay-to-screen rules apply?
- What exact reports, data sources, match logic, sanctions lists, work-eligibility source, retention periods, and dispute/reinvestigation process does the vendor use?
- Will Mosaic Relay receive only status codes and minimal metadata, or any full report content that could increase its legal and security exposure?

### Generated analysis
The controlling legal issue is whether the vendor’s background checks are FCRA “consumer reports” used for a permissible purpose, and whether the platform is the “user” taking adverse action based in whole or in part on those reports. If yes, the platform likely must provide a clear standalone disclosure and obtain written authorization before procurement, then follow a two-step adverse-action process: pre-adverse-action notice with a copy of the report and the CFPB “Summary of Your Rights Under the FCRA,” followed by a final adverse-action notice after a reasonable opportunity to dispute. The final notice must include the CRA’s contact information, a statement that the CRA did not make the decision and cannot explain it, notice of the right to a free report within 60 days, and notice of the right to dispute accuracy or completeness.
A second major issue is role allocation. If Mosaic Relay only receives a status code and uses it operationally to hold or release payout, it may still become entangled in the decision chain if its product automatically treats “review” or “fail” as a payout denial. That creates risk that Mosaic Relay is viewed as participating in an adverse decision, or at least as operationalizing one without the required notices and dispute pause. The safer structure is that the platform is the sole decision-maker, Mosaic Relay acts only on the platform’s instruction or on a narrowly defined payout-hold rule, and any adverse-action workflow sits with the platform unless contractually assumed by Mosaic Relay with full compliance controls.
State law is a material overlay. Many states and localities restrict when criminal history can be requested, what can be considered, whether individualized assessment is required, what notices must be given, and how long a candidate must have to respond. Some jurisdictions also limit who may be charged for screening or impose special rules for independent contractors in delivery or gig work. Because launch states are unknown, the state matrix is a launch dependency, not a nice-to-have.
Privacy and security issues are also significant. Identity data, criminal-history data, sanctions data, and work-eligibility data are high-sensitivity categories in practice even when not all are treated the same under statute. The product should minimize collection, segregate report content from payout systems, restrict access, log access, encrypt data, define retention/deletion, and prohibit secondary use. Mosaic Relay should not ingest full reports unless there is a documented legal reason.
Discrimination risk is real even for independent contractors. Criminal-history screening can create disparate-impact exposure under federal and state civil-rights theories, and some state or local laws extend protections beyond traditional employees. If screening is used, the platform should use job-related criteria, individualized assessment where required, and avoid automatic disqualification based on broad categories of criminal history.
Payments risk is mostly boundary risk. Mosaic Relay should avoid becoming the party that decides eligibility to work or receive payment if that decision is really a consumer-report-based adverse action. It should also ensure payout holds are transparent, reversible, and tied to documented triggers, consistent with its risk posture.
## Viable paths
### Path 1 — Platform owns screening and adverse action; Mosaic Relay receives only status codes
This is the strongest path. The platform contracts with the vendor, certifies permissible purpose, obtains contractor consent, receives the report, makes the final decision, and owns all pre-adverse and adverse-action notices and disputes. Mosaic Relay receives only “clear,” “review,” or “fail” plus minimal metadata needed to hold or release payout.
**Pros:** lowest FCRA, privacy, discrimination, and security exposure for Mosaic Relay; cleanest role allocation.
**Cons:** requires disciplined API design and contract language so Mosaic Relay does not accidentally become a co-decision-maker.
### Path 2 — Platform owns decisions, but Mosaic Relay operationalizes a temporary payout hold during “review”
Mosaic Relay can hold payout when the platform sends a “review” state, but only under a documented, reversible rule: no final denial by Mosaic Relay, no report content, no automated adverse decision, and a release or escalation path after the platform completes its process.
**Pros:** operationally practical; preserves platform ownership.
**Cons:** still needs careful UX and contract drafting so a hold does not function as a hidden adverse action without notice.
### Path 3 — Mosaic Relay receives fuller report data or participates in decisioning
This is the weakest path and should be treated as exceptional. If Mosaic Relay receives report content, scores, or rules that materially affect payout eligibility, it may assume greater FCRA, privacy, security, and discrimination exposure, and may need deeper contractual and operational controls.
**Pros:** may support more integrated operations.
**Cons:** highest legal and operational burden; hardest to launch safely in eight weeks.
## Facts that could change the answer
- If the vendor is not a CRA and the reports are not FCRA consumer reports, the federal adverse-action burden may narrow, though state law and privacy obligations would remain.
- If Mosaic Relay receives only status codes and never report content, its exposure drops materially.
- If Mosaic Relay’s system automatically denies payout on “fail,” rather than merely reflecting the platform’s decision, Mosaic Relay’s role risk rises sharply.
- If launch includes states or cities with strict fair-chance or gig-worker screening rules, the product may need state-specific notice timing, individualized assessment, or delayed screening.
- If contractors are reclassified as employees in some jurisdictions, employment-law screening rules and discrimination exposure become more direct.
- If sanctions screening is separate from the consumer-report product and based on Mosaic Relay’s own compliance obligations, that piece may need a different workflow from criminal-history screening.
## Recommended requirements for launch
### Product behavior legal should require
- Mosaic Relay receives only the minimum status code and decision metadata needed for payout operations.
- No full criminal, identity, sanctions-detail, or work-eligibility report content flows to Mosaic Relay absent documented legal review.
- “Review” cannot auto-convert to denial. It must route to a human or platform-controlled workflow.
- Any payout hold based on screening must be reversible, time-bound, logged, and tied to a documented trigger.
- Product must block screening or payout in any launch state where required notices, waiting periods, or state configuration are missing.
- Product must include a kill switch to disable screening-linked payout holds by state, customer, or vendor report type.
- Contractor-facing UX must distinguish identity verification, sanctions screening, criminal-history screening, and work-eligibility screening rather than presenting one opaque “failed check.”
### Consent language legal should require
- A clear, standalone contractor disclosure that a consumer report may be obtained for engagement/contracting purposes.
- Separate written authorization before the report is obtained.
- The disclosure should identify the platform as the requester and decision-maker, name the screening vendor where required, and explain the categories of checks in plain language.
- Consent should not be buried in general terms of service.
- If state law requires additional language, notices, or separate consent for investigative consumer reports or criminal-history checks, state-specific text must be inserted.
### Notices legal should require
- Pre-adverse-action notice before any final denial, suspension, or material payout restriction based on the report.
- The pre-adverse package must include the report and the FCRA rights summary.
- A reasonable waiting period before final action, with state-specific timing where stricter.
- Final adverse-action notice with CRA contact details, the statement that the CRA did not make the decision, the right to a free report within 60 days, and the right to dispute.
- Contractor-facing notice explaining whether payout is delayed, released, or blocked, and whom to contact.
### Dispute rights legal should require
- A visible contractor dispute path in the product.
- Pause on final adverse action while a timely dispute is under reinvestigation.
- Vendor obligation to reinvestigate and correct errors within defined SLAs.
- Escalation path for mixed files, identity mismatches, expunged/sealed records, and incorrect sanctions matches.
- Final written outcome to the contractor after reinvestigation.
### Access controls legal should require
- Least-privilege access to screening data.
- Field-level separation between report content and payout operations.
- No Mosaic Relay access to full reports by default.
- Audit logs for every access, status change, hold, release, and override.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Tenant isolation between platform customers.
- Defined retention and deletion schedule for identity artifacts and screening metadata.
- Incident-response playbook for vendor data exposure or incorrect report transmission.
### Customer contract terms legal should require
- Platform is the sole decision-maker for contractor eligibility and any adverse action.
- Platform certifies permissible purpose and FCRA compliance.
- Platform owns disclosure, authorization, pre-adverse and adverse-action notices, and disputes unless expressly shifted in writing.
- Vendor flow-downs requiring CRA compliance, reinvestigation duties, security controls, deletion, audit rights, and incident notice.
- No secondary use of contractor data by Mosaic Relay or the vendor.
- Indemnity and insurance allocation for screening errors, unlawful use, and data breaches.
- Regulatory cooperation and audit rights.
- Clear suspension/termination rights if the platform or vendor fails compliance obligations.
