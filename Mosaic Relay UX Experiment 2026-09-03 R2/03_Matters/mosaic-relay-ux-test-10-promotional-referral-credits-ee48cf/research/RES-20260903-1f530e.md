# Platform and fraud risk controls for a referral program: self-referral

## Question

Platform and fraud risk controls for a referral program: self-referral and synthetic-referral abuse, duplicate referrals, clawback/holdback mechanisms, and campaign record-keeping requirements for a payments infrastructure provider.

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. (a fictional B2B payments infrastructure provider) wants to launch a three‑month referral test in the United States and Canada. Existing business customers would earn a $100 platform credit when a referred merchant completes onboarding and settles its first eligible payment. Marketing plans to promote the offer through email, social media, and partner channels, with a requested launch date of October 3, 2026. Counsel is involved because the program implicates advertising‑law compliance, email‑marketing consent (CAN‑SPAM/CASL), tax characterization of the credit, and platform‑risk controls against referral fraud.
## Decision question
Should legal approve the referral program’s specific eligibility rules, disclosure language, email‑consent basis, partner compensation terms, reward restrictions, 90‑day expiration, fraud‑response protocols, and campaign record‑keeping requirements in time for the October 3, 2026 launch, given that tax treatment and email‑consent status remain unconfirmed?
## Open questions
- **Tax treatment:** Is the $100 credit taxable income to the referrer (1099‑MISC/1099‑NEC threshold analysis) and deductible for Mosaic Relay, and does it create GST/HST or provincial tax obligations in Canada?
- **Email‑consent basis:** Does Marketing hold documented opt‑in consent for promotional email to US and Canadian recipients, or only transactional/service consent? CASL requires express or implied consent for commercial electronic messages to Canadian recipients.
- **Partner‑channel compliance:** Are marketing partners’ flat‑fee‑per‑referral arrangements structured to avoid money‑transmission or unlicensed‑activity characterization, and do partner agreements include required FTC‑style material‑connection disclosures?
- **Fraud‑control sufficiency:** Do the existing draft fraud controls adequately address self‑referrals, synthetic referrals, duplicate accounts, and clawback/holdback mechanics before credit issuance?
- **Regional exclusions:** Are any US states or Canadian provinces excluded from the test, and do local rules (e.g., Quebec’s French‑language requirements or provincial privacy laws) affect the campaign?
---
## Likely rules and issues
**Advertising and promotion law**
- **FTC Act § 5** (US): Disclosures of material terms (eligibility, expiration, caps, non‑cash nature) must be clear, conspicuous, and adjacent to the call to action. The proposed short‑form disclosure adjacent to every CTA and full terms linked from each placement aligns with FTC guidance on deceptive pricing and promotional offers.
- **Competition Act** (Canada): Similar deceptive‑marketing‑practices rules apply; performance claims and conditions must be adequately disclosed.
- **FTC Endorsement Guides**: Marketing partners receiving flat fees per successful referral have a material connection that must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously in their posts—not buried in a footer.
**Email marketing**
- **CAN‑SPAM Act** (US): Requires accurate header information, non‑deceptive subject lines, identification as an ad, a valid physical postal address, and a functioning opt‑out mechanism honored within 10 business days. Consent is not strictly required for B2B email, but Mosaic Relay’s risk posture favors documented opt‑in.
- **CASL** (Canada): Requires express or implied consent before sending commercial electronic messages. Implied consent may exist for existing business relationships, but the referral offer is promotional, not transactional. Express opt‑in is the safer basis. Messages must identify the sender, provide contact information, and include an unsubscribe mechanism.
**Tax**
- **US federal income tax**: A $100 credit is likely taxable income to the referrer if the referrer is a business (potential 1099‑MISC reporting if aggregate payments exceed $600 in a calendar year). For the referred merchant, the credit may be a reduction in platform fees rather than income. Deductibility for Mosaic Relay depends on characterization as a marketing expense.
- **Canada**: Similar income‑inclusion principles apply; GST/HST implications depend on whether the credit is treated as a discount or consideration.
**Platform risk and fraud**
- **Self‑referral and synthetic‑referral abuse**: Controls must verify that the referred merchant is genuinely new to Mosaic Relay, not a duplicate account or an employee/sales‑team referral.
- **Duplicate referrals**: One credit per referred merchant, with deduplication logic at onboarding.
- **Clawback/holdback**: Credits should be issued only after the first eligible payment settles, with a holdback period to detect chargebacks or fraud. Terms must reserve the right to claw back credits obtained through abuse.
- **Record‑keeping**: Campaign records must capture consent proof, disclosure versions, partner agreements, credit issuance logs, and fraud‑review outcomes for audit and regulatory examination.
**Money‑transmission and licensing perimeter**
- The $100 credit is non‑cash, non‑transferable, and redeemable only against future platform fees. This structure minimizes money‑transmission risk, but counsel should confirm that the credit is not characterized as stored value or a monetary instrument under state money‑transmitter statutes or Canadian provincial equivalents.

## Viable paths
**Path A — Launch with conditions (recommended for speed)**
Approve the program subject to: (1) documented email‑consent basis before any promotional send; (2) tax counsel sign‑off on 1099/GST treatment; (3) final fraud‑control review; and (4) partner agreements executed with required disclosures. This preserves the October 3 launch date while gating the highest‑risk elements.
**Path B — Delay launch pending full clearance**
Hold the launch until tax and consent questions are fully resolved. This reduces legal risk but likely misses the three‑month test window and Growth’s timing.
**Path C — Pilot with transactional‑only email**
Launch the program but restrict email promotion to transactional/service messages (e.g., in‑product notifications, receipts) until opt‑in consent is confirmed. Social and partner channels could proceed with proper disclosures. This narrows reach but avoids CASL/CAN‑SPAM exposure.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Consent records**: If Marketing produces documented express opt‑in for US and Canadian recipients, Path A becomes cleaner. If only transactional consent exists, Path C or a consent‑gathering campaign is necessary.
- **Tax counsel opinion**: If the credit is characterized as a rebate or discount rather than income, 1099 reporting may not apply, simplifying compliance.
- **State/provincial exclusions**: If Growth excludes Quebec or states with strict promotional‑contest rules (e.g., Florida, New York registration requirements for certain promotions), the compliance burden changes.
- **Partner compensation structure**: If partners are compensated per lead rather than per verified successful referral, the risk of deceptive‑practices and money‑transmission characterization increases.
- **Credit expiration**: A 90‑day expiration is aggressive; some states have gift‑card or stored‑value laws that could apply if the credit is deemed monetary value. Confirming the credit is a promotional discount, not stored value, is critical.
---
## Recommended last‑mile verification
1. **Email consent**: Obtain from Marketing a list of US and Canadian recipients with documented opt‑in timestamps and consent language. If unavailable, restrict email to transactional messages or run a pre‑launch consent campaign.
2. **Tax opinion**: Engage tax counsel to confirm 1099‑MISC/NEC reporting thresholds, deductibility, and GST/HST treatment before launch.
3. **Fraud‑control walkthrough**: Review the draft fraud controls with Risk and Payments Operations to confirm deduplication logic, holdback periods, and clawback triggers are operational before credit issuance.
4. **Partner agreement template**: Verify that partner agreements include FTC‑compliant material‑connection disclosures, approved‑copy requirements, and clawback/holdback provisions.
5. **Terms finalization**: Confirm that the full terms state the 90‑day expiration, non‑cash/non‑transferable nature, 10‑credit cap, and eligibility restrictions clearly at sign‑up and at issuance.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption**: The $100 credit is a promotional discount, not stored value or a monetary instrument. If state or provincial regulators characterize it as stored value, money‑transmitter licensing or gift‑card laws (including expiration‑date restrictions) could apply, potentially invalidating the 90‑day expiration.
- **Open fork**: Email‑consent basis. If Marketing cannot produce opt‑in records, the program must either delay, restrict email to transactional messages, or run a consent‑gathering campaign—each path materially affects reach and timeline.
- **Not examined**: The draft terms and fraud controls referenced in the facts were not read. Specific language on clawback triggers, holdback periods, and dispute resolution could change the fraud‑control recommendation.
- **Not examined**: State‑by‑state and provincial promotional‑contest and gift‑card laws were not surveyed. If the credit is deemed a prize or stored value, registration, bonding, or expiration‑date rules in Florida, New York, Quebec, or other jurisdictions could apply.
- **Working assumption**: The referred merchant’s first eligible payment is a genuine commercial transaction, not a test or micro‑transaction designed to trigger the credit. If “eligible payment” is not defined to exclude nominal or self‑dealing transactions, synthetic‑referral fraud risk increases significantly.
