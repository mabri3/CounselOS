# Partner/affiliate disclosure obligations for marketing partners promoting a refe

## Question

Partner/affiliate disclosure obligations for marketing partners promoting a referral offer in the US (FTC endorsement/endorsement guides, material connection) and Canada (Competition Act, false/misleading representations), including disclosure placement requirements.

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. wants to launch a three-month referral test in the United States and Canada on 2026-10-03. Existing business customers would receive a $100 platform credit when a referred merchant completes onboarding and settles its first eligible payment. Marketing plans to promote the offer through email, social media, and partner channels, with marketing partners compensated at a flat $25 per verified successful referral. Counsel is involved because the partner compensation structure creates material-connection disclosure obligations under US and Canadian advertising law, and the email-consent basis for promotional outreach remains unconfirmed.
## Decision question
Should legal approve the referral program’s partner-channel promotion structure—specifically, the flat $25 partner fee, the requirement that partners disclose their material connection adjacent to referral claims, and the planned email/social/partner creative—given that email-consent records are not yet confirmed and tax treatment of the $100 credit is unresolved, or should launch be conditioned on resolving those gaps first?
## Open questions
- What is the documented email-consent basis for promotional outreach to US and Canadian recipients (CAN-SPAM compliance vs. CASL opt-in), and can Marketing produce opt-in records before 2026-10-03?
- Will tax counsel confirm whether the $100 platform credit is taxable income to the referrer, a rebate, or a business expense reduction, and does that treatment require disclosure in program terms?
- Are there any state or provincial licensing or registration requirements triggered by compensating partners for merchant referrals (e.g., money-transmission or loan-broker rules if credits are deemed cash-equivalent)?
- Does the draft partner agreement include audit rights, clawback mechanics, and indemnification for partner violations of disclosure or consent rules?

## Likely rules and issues
### United States: FTC Endorsement Guides and material-connection disclosure
The FTC’s Endorsement Guides (16 C.F.R. Part 255) treat a marketing partner’s promotion of the referral offer as an endorsement when the partner receives compensation (here, $25 per verified referral). The Guides require clear and conspicuous disclosure of the material connection—i.e., the fact that the partner is paid for successful referrals—when that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience. Disclosure must be unavoidable, in close proximity to the endorsement claim, and in the same medium (e.g., text disclosure in a social post, verbal disclosure in a video). A footer-only or hyperlink-only disclosure is generally insufficient.
The FTC has signaled that “#ad” or “Sponsored” tags may suffice in social media if unambiguous and visible without clicking “more,” but the disclosure must appear before the consumer engages with the referral link or call to action. For email, the subject line and body must not be deceptive, and the material connection should be disclosed in the body near the referral pitch.
### Canada: Competition Act and false/misleading representations
Sections 74.01 and 74.02 of the Competition Act prohibit false or misleading representations and deceptive marketing practices. The Competition Bureau’s guidance on influencer marketing (which applies by analogy to paid referral partners) requires disclosure of material connections in a way that is clear, prominent, and proximate to the representation. The Bureau has taken enforcement action where disclosures were buried in footers, hashtags, or hyperlinks.
CASL (S.C. 2010, c. 23) applies to commercial electronic messages sent to Canadian recipients. Promotional email about the referral program requires either express opt-in consent or an implied consent basis (e.g., existing business relationship within the last two years). The message must identify the sender, include contact information, and provide a functioning unsubscribe mechanism. The current fact record states that consent status is “not yet confirmed” and assumes “transactional/service consent only,” which is likely insufficient for promotional referral offers under CASL unless the message is strictly informational about an existing service.
### Disclosure placement and format
The matter facts propose a short-form disclosure adjacent to every call to action and a link to full terms. That structure aligns with FTC and Competition Bureau guidance, provided the short-form disclosure is:
- In the same language and medium as the claim
- Visible without scrolling or clicking (above the fold in email, in the first lines of a social post)
- Unambiguous (e.g., “We’re paid when you sign up” or “Sponsored referral link”)
- Repeated when the credit is issued and when the referred merchant completes onboarding
Partner posts must show the material-connection disclosure next to the claim, not only in a footer or bio. Mosaic Relay should require partners to use approved disclosure language and should monitor compliance.
### Tax treatment of the $100 credit
The $100 platform credit may be taxable income to the referrer under IRC § 61 (US) or a business expense reduction under Canadian tax rules, depending on whether it is characterized as a rebate, discount, or payment for services. If the credit is income, Mosaic Relay may have information-reporting obligations (e.g., Form 1099-MISC if the referrer receives $600+ in a year, though the 10-credit cap suggests most referrers will stay below that threshold). The terms should disclose that the credit may be taxable and that referrers are responsible for their own tax reporting. Tax counsel review is flagged as pending.
### Platform-risk and fraud controls
The eligibility rules (existing business customers only, new referred merchants, no self-referrals, no employee/sales referrals) and the 10-credit cap are designed to limit abuse. The partner agreement should include clawback and holdback provisions for fraudulent or non-compliant referrals, and Mosaic Relay should monitor for patterns (e.g., high-volume referrals from a single partner, referrals that fail to settle, duplicate accounts).

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Approve with conditions (recommended if consent and tax gaps close before launch)
Approve the program structure, partner terms, and disclosure framework now, but condition launch on:
1. Marketing producing documented opt-in consent records for Canadian recipients (or confirming an implied-consent basis under CASL) and CAN-SPAM compliance for US recipients
2. Tax counsel confirming the credit’s tax treatment and any reporting obligations
3. Final partner agreement including audit rights, clawback, indemnification, and approved disclosure language
4. Creative review confirming that short-form disclosures appear adjacent to every call to action in email, social, and partner placements
This path allows the 2026-10-03 launch if the gaps close in time and preserves the three-month test window.
### Path 2: Delay launch until consent and tax are resolved
Hold launch until Marketing confirms CASL-compliant consent for Canadian recipients and tax counsel issues an opinion. This eliminates regulatory risk but may miss the 2026-10-03 target and compress the test window.
### Path 3: Launch in the US only, defer Canada
Launch the test in the US under CAN-SPAM (which permits promotional email to business contacts with an opt-out mechanism) and defer the Canadian launch until CASL consent is confirmed. This preserves the US test timeline but reduces the data set and may require separate creative and terms for Canada later.

## Facts that could change the answer
- If Marketing cannot produce CASL-compliant consent records for Canadian recipients, the Canadian launch must be delayed or restructured (e.g., limited to non-email channels or existing customers with implied consent).
- If tax counsel determines the $100 credit is taxable income and Mosaic Relay has reporting obligations, the program terms must disclose that, and the 10-credit cap may need adjustment to stay below reporting thresholds.
- If any partner plans to promote the offer through paid search or display ads (not just organic social or email), additional disclosure and platform-policy requirements may apply (e.g., Google Ads disclosure rules).
- If the referred merchant’s “first eligible payment” includes ACH or local-payment methods that settle slowly, the timing of credit issuance and the definition of “settled” may need clarification to avoid disputes.

## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Email consent**: Marketing and Compliance should produce a list of Canadian recipients with documented opt-in consent or a legal basis for implied consent under CASL § 10(9). For US recipients, confirm CAN-SPAM compliance (accurate header information, non-deceptive subject line, functioning opt-out, physical postal address).
2. **Tax opinion**: Tax counsel should confirm whether the $100 credit is income to the referrer, whether Mosaic Relay has withholding or reporting obligations, and whether the credit is deductible as a business expense for Mosaic Relay.
3. **Partner agreement**: Confirm the final agreement includes (a) approved disclosure language and placement requirements, (b) audit and monitoring rights, (c) clawback and holdback for fraud or non-compliance, (d) indemnification for partner violations, and (e) a requirement that partners comply with FTC Guides, Competition Act, CASL, and CAN-SPAM.
4. **Creative review**: Review final email, social, and partner creative to confirm short-form disclosures appear adjacent to every call to action, are visible without scrolling or clicking, and link to full terms.
5. **Monitoring plan**: Confirm the fraud-monitoring plan includes triggers for high-volume referrals, duplicate accounts, and referrals that fail to settle, and that Compliance reviews partner activity weekly during the test.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption**: The analysis assumes the $25 partner fee is a flat, one-time payment per verified referral, not a recurring or volume-based commission. If partners receive recurring payments or a percentage of the referred merchant’s processing volume, the arrangement may trigger money-transmission or loan-broker licensing requirements in some states or provinces, and the FTC/Competition Bureau may scrutinize the disclosure more closely as a material conflict of interest.
- **Open fork**: If Marketing cannot produce CASL-compliant consent records for Canadian recipients before 2026-10-03, counsel must choose between (a) delaying the Canadian launch until consent is documented, (b) limiting Canadian promotion to non-email channels (social, partner websites) where CASL does not apply, or (c) relying on implied consent under CASL § 10(9) if an existing business relationship can be documented. Each path has different timing, scope, and risk profiles.
- **Not examined**: The vault contains a draft research packet (`referral-credit-program-research-packet-022913.md`) that was not read for this answer. It may contain additional analysis, draft terms, or monitoring-plan details that could refine the disclosure language, fraud controls, or partner-agreement provisions recommended here.
- **Working assumption**: The analysis assumes the referred merchant’s “first eligible payment” will settle within a commercially reasonable time (e.g., 2–5 business days for card payments, longer for ACH). If settlement timing varies significantly by payment method or jurisdiction, the terms should define “settled” and specify when the credit is issued to avoid disputes.
- **Open fork**: If tax counsel determines the $100 credit is taxable income to the referrer and Mosaic Relay has information-reporting obligations, the program terms must disclose that, and Mosaic Relay may need to collect W-9s (US) or equivalent tax information from referrers. If the credit is a rebate or discount, no reporting is required, but the terms should still disclose that referrers are responsible for their own tax treatment. The choice affects the onboarding flow, terms language, and administrative burden.
