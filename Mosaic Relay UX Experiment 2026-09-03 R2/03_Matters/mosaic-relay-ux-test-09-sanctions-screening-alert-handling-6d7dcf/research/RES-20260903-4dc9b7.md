# What are common regulatory expectations for evidence standards and record

## Question

What are common regulatory expectations for evidence standards and record retention for sanctions screening and review determinations?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc., a B2B payments infrastructure provider, is replacing manual sanctions screening with a vendor solution that returns potential matches and confidence signals. Product proposes automatically blocking exact matches, allowing low-confidence matches to proceed, and sending review cases to an offshore operations team, with a weekend migration planned in two months. Counsel is involved because the design implicates OFAC and global sanctions compliance, cross-border data transfers, evidence standards for true/false match determinations, and recordkeeping obligations. The decision is time-sensitive: the migration timeline is aggressive given unresolved controls around list coverage, escalation authority, and data transfer mechanisms.
## Decision question
What specific screening-event triggers, matching thresholds, manual-review steps, access restrictions, customer notices, record-retention periods, and fallback procedures must Mosaic Relay implement before legal can approve the vendor-based sanctions screening program and authorize the weekend cutover in two months?
## Open questions
- What is the vendor's sanctions list coverage by jurisdiction, and how is daily refresh independently verified to support global/multi-jurisdiction coverage?
- What evidence standard and documentation will the two-person (maker/checker) review team use to distinguish true matches from false positives, and what happens when reviewers disagree or are unavailable?
- What cross-border data transfer mechanism (adequacy decision, SCCs, derogation) will govern the offshore operations team's access to sanctions review data, and has the vendor's DPA and sub-processor terms been reviewed?
- What customer communications will be sent when payouts are held or blocked, and what appeal or escalation path will be offered?
- What record retention schedule applies to screening logs, review determinations, and escalation records, and does it satisfy OFAC's five-year requirement and any longer partner or jurisdictional requirements?

## Likely rules and issues
**Sanctions compliance framework.** Mosaic Relay's obligations arise from OFAC regulations (31 C.F.R. Parts 500–599), which prohibit dealings with blocked persons and require U.S. persons to block or reject transactions involving sanctioned parties. As a payments infrastructure provider coordinating acquiring banks and processors, Mosaic Relay is expected to maintain a risk-based sanctions compliance program that includes screening at onboarding and before payouts, with documented procedures for handling potential matches.
**Evidence standards for match determinations.** OFAC does not prescribe a specific evidence standard, but enforcement actions and guidance (e.g., OFAC's Framework for Sanctions Compliance Commitments, 2019) emphasize that determinations must be based on a documented review of relevant identifiers (name, address, date of birth, government ID, etc.) and a reasoned conclusion. A two-person (maker/checker) review is a common control, but the evidence standard must be defined: what identifiers are required to clear a match, what level of confidence is needed, and how disagreements are resolved. The absence of a documented standard is a material gap.
**Record retention.** OFAC's recordkeeping requirements (31 C.F.R. § 501.601) mandate that records of blocked property and rejected transactions be retained for five years. Best practice and partner bank expectations often extend this to five years for all screening and review records, including logs of potential matches, evidence reviewed, determinations made, and escalations. The retention schedule must also account for longer requirements under state money transmitter laws, partner contracts, or non-U.S. jurisdictions (e.g., EU AMLD recordkeeping requirements).
**Cross-border data transfers.** Sending sanctions review data to an offshore operations team in a different region implicates data protection laws (e.g., GDPR if EU data subjects are involved, or other local privacy laws). Sanctions review data includes personal data (names, identifiers, transaction details), and cross-border transfers require a lawful mechanism (adequacy decision, standard contractual clauses, or a derogation). The vendor's data processing agreement and sub-processor terms must be reviewed to ensure they permit the offshore transfer and include appropriate safeguards.
**Matching thresholds and auto-blocking.** Automatically blocking exact matches is defensible if "exact match" is narrowly defined (e.g., exact name + exact date of birth + exact government ID). Allowing low-confidence matches to proceed without human review is high-risk: OFAC expects potential matches to be reviewed before funds are released, and auto-releasing low-confidence matches could result in processing transactions for sanctioned parties. The intake response indicating that low-confidence matches will be held until maker/checker review clears is the correct approach, but this must be codified in the program design.
**Fallback procedures.** The vendor's unavailability during onboarding or before a payout must trigger a fallback to manual screening. This fallback must be documented, tested, and staffed, and the decision to fall back must be logged. A weekend migration without a tested fallback is a material risk.
**Customer communications.** When a payout is held or blocked, Mosaic Relay must communicate with the customer in a way that complies with OFAC's restrictions on tipping off sanctioned parties while providing enough information to allow the customer to respond or appeal. The communication plan must be defined, including timing, content, and escalation paths.
## Viable paths
**Path 1: Conditional approval with hard gates (recommended).** Approve the sanctions screening design conditionally, but do not authorize the weekend cutover until all migration-readiness gates are complete. This path allows Product to continue implementation while ensuring that material gaps (evidence standards, data transfer mechanism, list coverage verification, customer communications, record retention) are resolved before go-live. The recorded durable decision reflects this path.
**Path 2: Delay the migration.** Do not approve the design or the cutover until all gaps are resolved. This path is lower risk but may not be feasible given the two-month timeline and business pressure.
**Path 3: Phased rollout.** Approve the design for a limited pilot (e.g., a subset of customers or a single jurisdiction) with full manual review of all potential matches, then expand to full automation after the pilot validates the controls. This path reduces risk but extends the timeline.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Vendor list coverage.** If the vendor's list coverage does not include all jurisdictions where Mosaic Relay operates or where its customers' payees are located, the program will not satisfy global/multi-jurisdiction coverage, and additional vendors or manual screening may be required.
- **Offshore team location.** If the offshore operations team is in a jurisdiction without an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards, the cross-border data transfer may violate GDPR or other privacy laws, requiring a different transfer mechanism or onshore review.
- **Partner bank requirements.** If Mosaic Relay's acquiring banks or processors impose stricter evidence standards, longer record retention, or specific customer communication requirements, the program must be adjusted to meet those contractual obligations.
- **Low-confidence match volume.** If the volume of low-confidence matches is high, holding all of them for maker/checker review may create operational bottlenecks, requiring additional staffing or a risk-based triage process.
- **Weekend migration feasibility.** If the vendor's API response times or the fallback manual screening process cannot handle the migration volume over a weekend, the cutover timeline must be extended or phased.
## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Confirm vendor list coverage and refresh verification.** Obtain written confirmation from the vendor of which sanctions lists are covered (OFAC SDN, EU consolidated list, UN, UK OFSI, etc.), how often lists are refreshed, and how Mosaic Relay can independently verify refresh (e.g., daily hash checks, API health checks, or audit reports).
2. **Document the evidence standard for match determinations.** Draft a written standard that specifies which identifiers must match to clear a potential match, what level of confidence is required, and how maker/checker disagreements are resolved. Include examples of true and false matches.
3. **Finalize the cross-border data transfer mechanism.** Confirm the offshore team's location, identify the applicable data protection law, and execute the necessary transfer mechanism (SCCs, adequacy decision, or derogation). Review the vendor's DPA and sub-processor terms to ensure they permit the transfer.
4. **Define customer communications.** Draft template communications for held and blocked payouts that comply with OFAC's tipping-off restrictions while providing customers with enough information to respond or appeal. Define the escalation path and timeline.
5. **Establish the record retention schedule.** Confirm that the retention schedule meets OFAC's five-year requirement and any longer partner or jurisdictional requirements. Ensure that screening logs, review determinations, and escalation records are retained in an auditable format.
6. **Test the fallback procedure.** Conduct a tabletop exercise or live test of the fallback to manual screening, including staffing, decision logging, and customer communications. Confirm that the fallback can handle the expected volume during the weekend migration.
7. **Review partner bank contracts.** Confirm that the program design satisfies any contractual requirements imposed by acquiring banks or processors, including evidence standards, record retention, and customer communications.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption:** The analysis assumes that Mosaic Relay's sanctions obligations are driven primarily by OFAC and partner bank requirements, not by direct licensing as a money transmitter in all jurisdictions. If Mosaic Relay is licensed as a money transmitter in states with stricter recordkeeping or evidence standards (e.g., New York), the program must be adjusted to meet those requirements.
- **Open fork:** If the vendor's list coverage does not support global/multi-jurisdiction screening, Mosaic Relay must either add vendors, restrict its geographic footprint, or maintain manual screening for uncovered jurisdictions. Each path has materially different cost, timeline, and risk implications.
- **Not examined:** The vendor's API documentation, data processing agreement, and sub-processor terms were not reviewed. These documents may contain limitations on data transfers, liability caps, or service level commitments that affect the program design.
- **Not examined:** Partner bank contracts were not reviewed. These contracts may impose stricter evidence standards, longer record retention, or specific customer communication requirements that must be incorporated into the program.
- **Working assumption:** The analysis assumes that the offshore operations team is in a jurisdiction that permits cross-border data transfers under GDPR or other applicable privacy laws. If the team is in a jurisdiction without an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards, the transfer mechanism must be redesigned or the team must be relocated onshore.
