# What are OFAC and other major-jurisdiction expectations for sanctions screening

## Question

What are OFAC and other major-jurisdiction expectations for sanctions screening list-update frequency and independent verification?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. is replacing manual sanctions screening at onboarding and before selected payouts with a vendor that returns potential matches and confidence signals. Product proposes automatically blocking exact matches, allowing low-confidence matches to proceed, and routing review cases to an offshore operations team in a different region, with a weekend migration planned in two months. Counsel is involved because the change touches OFAC and multi-jurisdiction sanctions compliance, cross-border data transfers, funds-flow controls, and recordkeeping obligations. The migration deadline creates urgency to define exact screening events, matching rules, manual-review steps, access restrictions, notices, record retention, and fallback procedures before launch.
## Decision question
What specific screening events, matching rules, manual-review steps, access restrictions, customer notices, record retention periods, and fallback procedures should legal approve for Mosaic Relay's automated sanctions screening program before the weekend migration in two months, given that the vendor provides daily list updates, the offshore review team operates in a different region, and the program must cover global/multi-jurisdiction sanctions lists?
## Open questions
- What is the vendor's actual list-update frequency and how is that refresh independently verified (beyond "daily refresh, vendor-managed")?
- Which specific sanctions lists and jurisdictions are in scope, and how will the program handle conflicting or divergent list requirements across jurisdictions?
- What are the data transfer locations and mechanisms for the offshore operations team, and do they comply with applicable data protection and sanctions-related data localization rules?
- What evidence standards and documented procedures will govern true vs false match determinations, and who has escalation authority when the two-person review (maker/checker) disagrees?
- What customer communications are required when payouts are held or blocked, and what record retention schedule applies to screening and review records?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on model knowledge and the matter context, the following sanctions compliance frameworks are likely relevant:
### OFAC (U.S. Office of Foreign Assets Control)
OFAC expects risk-based sanctions compliance programs under its Framework for OFAC Compliance Commitments (May 2019). Key expectations relevant here:
- **Screening frequency**: OFAC does not mandate a specific list-update frequency but expects screening to occur against current lists. Daily updates are common industry practice; intraday updates may be expected for high-risk or high-volume operations.
- **Independent verification**: OFAC expects companies to test and audit their screening programs, including validating that list updates are applied correctly and that matching logic works as intended. Reliance on vendor assurances alone is insufficient.
- **Automated blocking**: OFAC permits automated blocking of true matches but expects human review for potential matches to avoid false positives that could constitute unfair blocking or discrimination.
- **Recordkeeping**: OFAC's regulations (31 C.F.R. § 501.601) require retention of records related to blocked property and rejected transactions for at least five years.
- **Reporting**: Blocked property must be reported to OFAC within 10 business days (31 C.F.R. § 501.603).
### EU sanctions (Council of the European Union)
- EU sanctions regulations require screening against EU consolidated lists. Member state competent authorities enforce compliance.
- The EU expects risk-based screening and has issued guidance on internal compliance programs (e.g., European Commission guidance on internal compliance programs for dual-use trade controls, which is analogous).
- Data protection under GDPR applies to sanctions screening data, including cross-border transfers to offshore teams.
### UK sanctions (OFSI)
- The UK Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation expects firms to have adequate screening systems and to screen against UK sanctions lists.
- OFSI guidance emphasizes the importance of list-update frequency and testing.
### UN sanctions
- UN Security Council sanctions lists are incorporated into member state law. Screening against UN lists is a baseline expectation.
### Other jurisdictions
- Latin American and European markets may have local sanctions lists or data localization requirements that affect the offshore review team's access to screening data.
### Key issues for this matter
1. **List-update frequency and verification**: Daily vendor-managed refresh is a starting point, but legal should require independent verification (e.g., periodic audits, checksums, or third-party validation) to ensure lists are current and correctly applied.
2. **Matching rules and confidence thresholds**: Automatically blocking exact matches is defensible if "exact" is narrowly defined (e.g., exact name + date of birth + address). Allowing low-confidence matches to proceed without review creates risk of processing transactions for sanctioned parties. The decision to hold funds pending human review for low-confidence matches (per the internal record) is prudent and should be formalized.
3. **Manual review steps**: Two-person review (maker/checker) is a strong control. Legal should define escalation authority for disagreements, evidence standards for true/false match determinations, and documentation requirements.
4. **Cross-border data transfers**: Sending review cases to an offshore team in a different region implicates GDPR (if EU data is involved), data localization laws, and potentially sanctions-related restrictions on sharing screening data with certain jurisdictions. Legal should require a data transfer impact assessment and appropriate safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules).
5. **Customer communications**: When payouts are held or blocked, customers and payees may need notice. Legal should define what can be disclosed (e.g., "your transaction is under review for compliance purposes") without tipping off sanctioned parties or violating confidentiality obligations.
6. **Record retention**: Screening and review records should be retained for at least five years (OFAC standard) and potentially longer under other jurisdictions' requirements. Legal should define a retention schedule that covers screening logs, review notes, escalation records, and customer communications.
7. **Fallback procedures**: Falling back to manual screening when the vendor is unavailable is prudent. Legal should define the trigger for fallback, the manual screening procedure, and how to handle the backlog once the vendor is restored.
8. **Migration readiness**: A weekend migration in two months is aggressive given unresolved controls. Legal should require a phased rollout, parallel running (manual + automated) for a period, and a rollback plan.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Approve with conditions (recommended)
Approve the automated screening program subject to the following conditions:
- **List-update verification**: Require independent verification of daily list updates (e.g., weekly audits, automated checksums, or third-party validation).
- **Matching rules**: Define "exact match" narrowly (e.g., exact name + at least one additional identifier). Require human review for all non-exact matches, including low-confidence matches.
- **Manual review**: Formalize two-person review with documented evidence standards, escalation authority, and a maximum review time (e.g., 24 hours for onboarding, 4 hours for payouts).
- **Cross-border data transfers**: Complete a data transfer impact assessment and implement appropriate safeguards before routing review cases offshore.
- **Customer communications**: Approve template notices for held or blocked payouts that balance transparency with confidentiality.
- **Record retention**: Adopt a five-year retention schedule for all screening and review records.
- **Fallback procedures**: Document the manual screening fallback procedure and test it before migration.
- **Migration**: Require a phased rollout with parallel running for at least two weeks and a rollback plan.
### Path 2: Delay migration
Delay the weekend migration until all conditions in Path 1 are met. This is the lower-risk option but may not align with business timing.
### Path 3: Pilot program
Run a pilot with a subset of customers or transactions before full migration. This allows testing of controls and identification of issues before full-scale launch.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Vendor's actual list-update frequency and verification process**: If the vendor updates lists less frequently than daily or cannot support independent verification, the program may not meet regulatory expectations.
- **Specific jurisdictions in scope**: If the program covers jurisdictions with conflicting sanctions lists (e.g., U.S. vs. EU on certain entities), the matching rules and review procedures may need to be jurisdiction-specific.
- **Offshore team's location and data access**: If the offshore team is in a jurisdiction with data localization laws or sanctions-related restrictions, the cross-border data transfer may not be permissible.
- **Volume and velocity of transactions**: High-volume or real-time payout requirements may make manual review of all non-exact matches impractical, requiring a risk-based approach.
- **Partner requirements**: Acquiring banks or payment processors may impose additional screening or recordkeeping requirements that affect the program design.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before legal approves the program, verify the following:
1. **Vendor documentation**: Obtain and review the vendor's list-update frequency, sources, and verification process. Confirm that the vendor screens against all required lists (OFAC, EU, UK, UN, and any local lists).
2. **Data transfer assessment**: Complete a data transfer impact assessment for the offshore review team, including the legal basis for transfer, safeguards, and any data localization requirements.
3. **Matching logic testing**: Test the vendor's matching logic against a sample of known true and false matches to validate confidence thresholds and false positive rates.
4. **Manual review procedures**: Document the two-person review procedure, including evidence standards, escalation authority, and maximum review times.
5. **Customer communication templates**: Draft and approve template notices for held or blocked payouts.
6. **Record retention schedule**: Adopt a written record retention schedule that covers all screening and review records.
7. **Fallback procedure testing**: Test the manual screening fallback procedure to ensure it can handle the expected volume.
8. **Migration plan**: Review and approve the migration plan, including parallel running, rollback procedures, and success criteria.

If the offshore team is in a jurisdiction with adequate data protection (e.g., EU, UK, Switzerland), the transfer may be permissible with appropriate safeguards.
