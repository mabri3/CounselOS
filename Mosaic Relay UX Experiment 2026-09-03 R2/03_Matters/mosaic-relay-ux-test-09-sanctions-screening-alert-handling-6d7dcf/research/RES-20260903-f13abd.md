# What data-transfer and privacy obligations apply to sending sanctions review

## Question

What data-transfer and privacy obligations apply to sending sanctions review data to an offshore operations team in a different region?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc., a fictional B2B payments infrastructure provider, is replacing manual sanctions screening with an automated vendor solution. The proposed system will screen customers at onboarding and before selected payouts, automatically block exact matches, allow low-confidence matches to proceed, and route review cases to an offshore operations team located in a different region. Counsel is involved because this cross-border transfer of sanctions review data triggers material privacy and data-transfer obligations that must be resolved before the planned weekend migration in two months.
## Decision question
Should legal approve the transfer of sanctions screening data—including potential matches, confidence signals, and personal data of customers, sellers, and beneficial owners—to an offshore operations team in a different region, given that the specific data transfer locations, jurisdictional scope, and escalation authority remain undefined, and the migration is scheduled to occur over a weekend in two months?
## Open questions
- **Where exactly is the offshore operations team located?** The specific country or region determines which data-transfer mechanisms (e.g., adequacy decisions, standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules) are required and whether local law imposes additional restrictions on sanctions-related data.
- **What categories of personal data will be included in the screening records transferred offshore?** Sanctions screening typically involves names, dates of birth, nationalities, identification numbers, and potentially sensitive personal data; the exact data elements affect the legal basis for transfer and the required safeguards.
- **Does Mosaic Relay act as a data controller or data processor with respect to the screening data?** The answer determines which privacy obligations apply directly to Mosaic Relay versus its customers, and whether additional contractual protections are needed.
- **What is the legal basis for processing and transferring sanctions screening data under applicable privacy laws?** Depending on the jurisdiction, this may rely on legal obligation, legitimate interests, or consent; the basis affects transparency requirements and data subject rights.
- **Are there any local law restrictions in the offshore location that could conflict with sanctions screening obligations?** Some jurisdictions have blocking statutes or data localization requirements that could impede compliance with U.S. or EU sanctions.

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on the matter facts and general privacy and sanctions compliance principles, the following rules and issues are likely implicated:
### Data transfer and privacy obligations
1. **Cross-border data transfer restrictions**: Transferring personal data from the United States, Latin America, or Europe to an offshore operations team in a different region triggers data-transfer restrictions under applicable privacy laws. For European data subjects, the GDPR requires an adequacy decision, appropriate safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules), or a derogation. For U.S. data subjects, state privacy laws (e.g., CCPA/CPRA) and sectoral laws may impose notice and contractual requirements. Latin American jurisdictions (e.g., Brazil's LGPD, Mexico's LFPDPPP) have similar transfer restrictions.
2. **Data minimization and purpose limitation**: Sanctions screening data should be limited to what is necessary for compliance purposes. The transfer should not include excessive personal data beyond what is required for the offshore team to perform the review.
3. **Transparency and notice obligations**: Customers, sellers, and beneficial owners must be informed that their personal data may be transferred to an offshore location for sanctions screening purposes. This typically requires updating privacy notices and, in some cases, obtaining consent or providing opt-out mechanisms.
4. **Data subject rights**: The offshore transfer must not impede data subjects' ability to exercise their rights (e.g., access, correction, deletion, objection). Mosaic Relay must ensure that the offshore team can facilitate these rights or that mechanisms are in place to route requests appropriately.
5. **Security and confidentiality obligations**: The offshore operations team must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to protect the screening data. This includes access controls, encryption, audit trails, and incident response procedures.
6. **Vendor and subprocessor management**: If the offshore operations team is a third-party vendor or subprocessor, Mosaic Relay must conduct due diligence, execute data processing agreements, and ensure ongoing oversight.
### Sanctions-specific considerations
7. **Conflicting legal obligations**: Some jurisdictions have blocking statutes or anti-boycott laws that may conflict with U.S. or EU sanctions requirements. The offshore team's location must be evaluated for such conflicts.
8. **Recordkeeping and audit trails**: Sanctions screening records, including the basis for true/false match determinations, must be retained and accessible for regulatory examinations. Cross-border transfers must not compromise the integrity or availability of these records.
9. **Escalation and decision-making authority**: The offshore team's authority to make final true/false match determinations and release held payouts must be clearly defined and consistent with Mosaic Relay's risk appetite and regulatory obligations.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Approve with conditions (recommended)
Legal approves the offshore transfer subject to the following conditions:
- **Confirm the offshore location** and verify that an adequate data-transfer mechanism is in place (e.g., SCCs for EU data, adequacy for UK/Swiss data, or local equivalents for Latin American data).
- **Execute a data processing agreement** with the offshore operations team that includes standard contractual clauses, security requirements, audit rights, and incident notification obligations.
- **Update privacy notices** to disclose the offshore transfer and the categories of data involved.
- **Implement access controls** to ensure the offshore team only accesses data necessary for their review function.
- **Define escalation authority** such that final true/false match determinations and payout releases require approval from a designated compliance officer (maker/checker model, as indicated in the facts).
- **Establish record retention procedures** that ensure screening records are preserved and accessible regardless of where the review occurs.
- **Conduct a transfer impact assessment** to evaluate local law risks in the offshore location, including any blocking statutes or data localization requirements.
**Pros**: Allows the migration to proceed on schedule while addressing material privacy and sanctions risks.
**Cons**: Requires significant legal and compliance work in a compressed timeframe; may require renegotiation with the vendor or offshore team.
### Path 2: Delay migration until privacy framework is complete
Legal recommends delaying the weekend migration until the data-transfer framework is fully documented and implemented.
**Pros**: Ensures full compliance with privacy and sanctions obligations; reduces risk of regulatory enforcement or data subject complaints.
**Cons**: Delays the compliance automation initiative; may frustrate business stakeholders; manual screening continues in the interim.
### Path 3: Limit offshore team's role to preliminary review only
Legal approves the offshore transfer but restricts the offshore team to preliminary review and escalation only. Final true/false match determinations and payout releases remain with onshore compliance personnel.
**Pros**: Reduces the risk of erroneous sanctions decisions; maintains tighter control over sensitive determinations; may simplify privacy compliance if the offshore team has limited data access.
**Cons**: May reduce the efficiency gains from automation; requires onshore compliance resources to remain available; may not fully achieve the business objective.
### Path 4: Use a regional or onshore operations team instead
Legal recommends using an operations team located in the same region as the data subjects or in a jurisdiction with an adequacy decision or equivalent protection.
**Pros**: Simplifies data-transfer compliance; reduces risk of conflicting local laws; may improve data subject trust.
**Cons**: May increase costs; may not align with the business's operational model; may not be feasible given existing vendor relationships.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Specific offshore location**: If the offshore team is located in a jurisdiction with an EU adequacy decision (e.g., UK, Switzerland, Japan), the transfer analysis is simpler. If located in a jurisdiction without adequacy (e.g., India, Philippines), additional safeguards and a transfer impact assessment are required.
- **Categories of data transferred**: If the screening data includes sensitive personal data (e.g., government-issued ID numbers, biometric data), additional protections and possibly explicit consent are required.
- **Mosaic Relay's role**: If Mosaic Relay is a data processor acting on behalf of its customers (who are the controllers), the customers' instructions and the data processing agreement govern the transfer. If Mosaic Relay is a controller, it bears direct responsibility for compliance.
- **Existence of blocking statutes**: If the offshore location has a blocking statute or anti-boycott law that conflicts with U.S. or EU sanctions, the transfer may need to be restructured or the offshore team's role limited.
- **Vendor's data handling practices**: If the screening vendor (not just the offshore ops team) also processes or stores data in a different region, additional transfer mechanisms and due diligence are required.
- **Customer contractual commitments**: If Mosaic Relay's customer agreements include data localization or transfer restrictions, those commitments must be honored or renegotiated.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before legal approves the offshore transfer, the following verification steps should be completed:
1. **Confirm the exact offshore location(s)** and obtain a list of all countries where the operations team and any subprocessors will access or store screening data.
2. **Obtain and review the vendor's data processing agreement** and any subprocessor agreements to ensure they include appropriate data-transfer mechanisms and security safeguards.
3. **Conduct or review a transfer impact assessment** for the offshore location, focusing on local law conflicts, government access risks, and data localization requirements.
4. **Verify that privacy notices** are updated to disclose the offshore transfer and that any required consents or opt-outs are obtained.
5. **Confirm the escalation and decision-making authority** for true/false match determinations and payout releases, and ensure this is documented in written procedures.
6. **Review the record retention schedule** for screening and review records to ensure compliance with sanctions and privacy requirements.
7. **Test the fallback procedure** (manual screening) to ensure it is operational and can be activated if the vendor or offshore team is unavailable.
8. **Validate the vendor's list-update frequency** and verification procedures to ensure the screening data is current and accurate.
