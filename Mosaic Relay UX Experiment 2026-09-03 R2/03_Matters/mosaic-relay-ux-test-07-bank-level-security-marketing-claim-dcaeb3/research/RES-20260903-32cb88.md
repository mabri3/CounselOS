# Financial-promotion and advertising rules for security claims in EU/UK and

## Question

Financial-promotion and advertising rules for security claims in EU/UK and LatAm markets

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc., a payments-infrastructure provider that is not a bank and does not hold deposits, wants to launch a marketing campaign claiming "bank-level security" and "institution-grade protection" across its website, sales materials, partner presentations, and paid search ads. The campaign is targeted for launch on 2026-10-03. Counsel is involved because the claims raise substantiation, consumer-perception, and regulatory-classification risks — particularly the risk that the language implies bank status or fund protection that Mosaic Relay does not provide. The geographic scope is unresolved; the company operates in the US plus selected Latin American and European markets, and the intake answer on geographic scope was "Continue with assumptions."
## Decision question
Should legal approve the phrases "bank-level security" and "institution-grade protection" for use in marketing across the US and potentially EU/UK and Latin American markets by 2026-10-03, and if so, what specific qualifiers, substantiation evidence, prohibited language, and review controls must accompany them — or should the campaign instead use only specific, verifiable descriptive security claims to avoid implying bank status or fund protection?
## Open questions
- **Which specific countries in Latin America and Europe will the campaign reach?** The intake answer deferred this ("Continue with assumptions"). Financial-promotion and misleading-advertising rules differ materially by jurisdiction, and several EU member states plus the UK impose specific restrictions on claims that could imply regulated-entity status.
- **What certifications does Mosaic Relay actually hold?** The intake response listed "documented encryption standards" and "documented monitoring and incident-response program" but did not confirm any specific certification (e.g., PCI DSS, SOC 2, ISO 27001). The substantiation file cannot be completed without this.
- **Will the campaign use customer logos or name competitors?** This was flagged as a missing fact in the original request and remains unresolved. Logo use raises endorsement/consent issues; competitor comparisons trigger comparative-advertising rules that vary by jurisdiction.
- **What are the specific limitations of coverage?** The request asks what "bank-level" does and does not cover, but no one has yet defined the boundaries of the security claim — what threats, what layers of the stack, what exclusions.
- **Do Mosaic Relay's acquiring-bank and processor contracts restrict or require pre-approval of marketing claims that reference banking or security?** Partner contracts in payments commonly include marketing-review clauses, and a claim referencing "bank-level" anything may trigger those provisions.

## Likely rules and issues
### United States
**FTC Act § 5 (15 U.S.C. § 45) — deceptive advertising.** The core federal rule. An advertisement is deceptive if it contains a representation or omission likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, and that representation is material. "Bank-level security" is an objective claim that requires a reasonable basis — competent and reliable evidence — before dissemination. The FTC's substantiation doctrine applies to both express and implied claims. The express claim is "our security is at the level banks use." The implied claims may include: (a) we are a bank or bank-affiliated, (b) customer funds are protected the way bank deposits are, and (c) a regulator oversees our security the way banking regulators oversee banks. Each implied claim independently requires substantiation or must be effectively disclaimed.
**State UDAP statutes.** Every state has an unfair-and-deceptive-acts-and-practices statute (e.g., California's Unfair Competition Law and False Advertising Law, New York's General Business Law §§ 349–350, Texas DTPA). These generally track FTC standards but may have lower intent requirements, private rights of action, and statutory damages. A claim that is arguably permissible under FTC guidance may still draw a state AG inquiry or private demand letter.
**FFIEC / bank-regulator perimeter risk.** Mosaic Relay is not a bank. Using "bank-level" in proximity to payment and fund-flow language could create a perception that Mosaic Relay is engaged in banking activity or holds deposits. This is primarily a regulatory-classification risk (money transmission, unlicensed banking activity) rather than an advertising-law risk, but it compounds the deception analysis: if the language implies deposit-taking, it may also imply a regulated activity Mosaic Relay is not licensed for.
### United Kingdom
**Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) § 21 — financial-promotion restriction.** A person must not communicate an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity unless authorized, exempt, or the communication is approved by an authorized person. If "bank-level security" or "institution-grade protection" is used in materials that also promote payment services involving safeguarding or e-money, the FCA could view the communication as a financial promotion requiring approval. Even if Mosaic Relay's UK activity is structured to fall outside FSMA's perimeter, the language itself — implying institutional status — increases the risk of being pulled in.
**Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (CPRs) / Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCC).** Misleading actions and omissions are prohibited. The ASA (Advertising Standards Authority) enforces the CAP Code, which requires that marketing communications be materially accurate, not misleading, and substantiated. The ASA has specifically acted against fintech companies for implying bank status or deposit protection they did not have. "Bank-level security" from a non-bank would likely draw ASA scrutiny if the ad reaches UK consumers.
**FCA Principle 7 (clear, fair, not misleading).** If Mosaic Relay is or becomes FCA-authorized (e.g., as a payment institution or e-money institution), all communications must be clear, fair, and not misleading. A claim implying bank status would likely fail this standard.
### European Union
**Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC).** Prohibits misleading actions (Article 6) and misleading omissions (Article 7). A claim is misleading if it contains false information or if it deceives or is likely to deceive the average consumer regarding, among other things, the nature of the trader or the extent of the trader's commitments. "Bank-level security" from a non-bank could be misleading as to the nature of the trader. Member-state implementations vary in enforcement intensity and private-right availability.
**Misleading and Comparative Advertising Directive (2006/114/EC).** If the campaign compares Mosaic Relay's security to named competitors, comparative-advertising rules apply: the comparison must be objective, verifiable, and not misleading. Several member states (e.g., Germany, France) have historically been stricter about comparative claims than the US.
**Payment Services Directive 2 (2015/2366/EU) and national implementations.** If Mosaic Relay or its partners operate as payment institutions in the EU, marketing materials must not misrepresent the nature of the service or the level of protection. National competent authorities (e.g., BaFin in Germany, ACPR in France) have taken enforcement action against firms whose marketing implied deposit protection or banking status they did not hold.
**Digital Services Act (2022/2065).** For paid search ads served in the EU, the DSA imposes transparency requirements on online advertising. While not directly a substantiation rule, it adds a compliance layer for the paid-search surface.
### Latin America
The specific countries are unknown. The most likely markets for a payments-infrastructure company are Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, and Chile. Each has consumer-protection and advertising law that prohibits misleading claims:
- **Brazil:** Consumer Defense Code (CDC, Law 8.078/90) Articles 6, 30, 35, 37 — prohibits misleading advertising and requires that advertising claims be substantiated. CONAR (National Council for Advertising Self-Regulation) actively reviews and acts on complaints. The Central Bank of Brazil regulates payment institutions and has specific rules on how non-bank payment providers may describe themselves.
- **Mexico:** Federal Consumer Protection Law (LFPC) Article 32 — prohibits misleading advertising. PROFECO enforces. The Fintech Law (Ley para Regular las Instituciones de Tecnología Financiera) regulates how fintech companies describe their services and prohibits implying banking status without a license.
- **Colombia:** Consumer Statute (Law 1480/2011) — prohibits misleading advertising. The Superintendence of Industry and Commerce enforces.
- **Argentina:** Consumer Protection Law 24.240 — prohibits misleading advertising. The Central Bank (BCRA) regulates payment service providers.
- **Chile:** Consumer Protection Law 19.496 — prohibits misleading advertising. SERNAC enforces.
In each of these markets, a claim implying bank status or deposit protection from a non-bank entity would likely violate consumer-protection law and could also trigger financial-regulator attention if the company is perceived as engaging in unlicensed financial activity.

## Viable paths
**Path A — Approve "bank-level security" with mandatory qualifiers and substantiation (medium risk).**
Require: (1) a completed substantiation file showing Mosaic Relay's controls meet or exceed a defined bank security baseline; (2) a mandatory qualifier in every instance — "Mosaic Relay is not a bank and does not hold deposits or customer funds"; (3) a specific description of what the claim covers (encryption standard, monitoring scope, incident-response capability); (4) a limitations-of-coverage statement; (5) geographic-scope confirmation with local review for EU/UK and LatAm markets; (6) acquiring-bank/processor contract review for marketing-approval clauses. Prohibit "institution-grade protection" entirely.
**Path B — Replace both phrases with specific, verifiable descriptive claims (low risk; recommended default).**
Instead of "bank-level security," use: "AES-256 encryption at rest and TLS 1.2+ in transit, 24/7 security monitoring, documented incident-response program, [specific certification if held]." This eliminates the implied-claim problem entirely, requires no comparative substantiation against banks, and works in every jurisdiction without local-law variation. The tradeoff is less punchy marketing language.
**Path C — Delay launch pending substantiation audit and geographic-scope confirmation (low legal risk, high business cost).**
Hold the campaign until the substantiation file is complete, the geographic audience is confirmed, and local counsel in each target market has reviewed the claims. This is the safest path but likely misses the 2026-10-03 target.
**Path D — Approve for US only, defer international (medium-low risk).**
Approve qualified "bank-level security" for US-facing materials only, with the mandatory qualifier. Defer EU/UK and LatAm use until local review is complete. This allows the campaign to launch on time for the primary market while containing international risk.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If Mosaic Relay holds a specific certification (PCI DSS Level 1, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001):** This would materially strengthen the substantiation file and could support a more specific version of the claim (e.g., "PCI DSS Level 1 certified — the same standard required by major card networks"). It would not, however, substantiate "bank-level" as a comparison to banks unless the certification is also held by banks and the comparison is documented.
- **If the campaign will reach EU/UK or LatAm audiences:** This triggers the financial-promotion, misleading-advertising, and consumer-protection rules described above. The answer changes from a primarily FTC/UDAP analysis to a multi-jurisdictional compliance review. Path D (US-only launch) becomes more attractive.
- **If any acquiring-bank or processor contract requires pre-approval of marketing materials referencing banking or security:** This could add a contractual gate that delays launch regardless of the legal analysis.
- **If the campaign will use customer logos:** This requires written consent from each customer whose logo appears and raises endorsement-substantiation issues under FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) and equivalent rules in other jurisdictions.
- **If the campaign will name competitors:** This triggers comparative-advertising rules in the EU (Directive 2006/114/EC) and raises Lanham Act § 43(a) exposure in the US if the comparison is challenged as false or misleading.
- **If Mosaic Relay's security posture changes before launch (e.g., a certification lapses, an incident occurs):** The substantiation file must be current at the time of each publication. A re-verification trigger is essential.

## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Complete the substantiation evidence file.** Obtain from Security: the specific encryption standards in use (algorithm, key length, at-rest and in-transit), the monitoring program description (scope, coverage hours, alerting thresholds), the incident-response plan (last tested date, tabletop exercise results), and all current certifications with expiration dates. Verify each document is current and covers the claim period.
2. **Confirm geographic scope with Marketing.** Get a definitive list of countries where the campaign will appear, including where paid search ads will be served. This determines whether EU/UK and LatAm review is needed before launch or can be deferred.
3. **Review acquiring-bank and processor contracts.** Check for marketing-approval clauses, co-branding restrictions, and any prohibition on referencing banking or security in customer-facing materials.
4. **Run an audience-perception check.** Have someone outside the immediate team (or a small panel) read the proposed copy and answer: "Does this company hold your money? Is this company a bank?" If the answer to either is yes or unclear, the qualifier is not working.
5. **Confirm no customer logos or competitor names are in the current creative.** If they are, flag for separate review.
6. **Record the approved wording.** Once the lawyer selects a path, record the exact approved language, the qualifier text, the prohibited-language list, and the re-verification trigger in the matter workspace. Distribute to Marketing, Sales, and Product with a clear instruction that any deviation requires re-approval.
