# What substantiation, qualifiers, review controls, and prohibited language should

## Question

What substantiation, qualifiers, review controls, and prohibited language should legal require before approving "bank-level security" and "institution-grade protection" claims for a non-bank payments-infrastructure provider across website, sales materials, partner presentations, and paid search ads, under FTC Act s.5, state UDAP, and relevant EU/UK advertising rules?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Marketing at Mosaic Relay Holdings, Inc. — a fictional payments-infrastructure provider that is not a bank and does not hold deposits — wants to advertise "bank-level security" and "institution-grade protection" across the website, sales materials, partner presentations, and paid search ads. Counsel is involved because the phrases risk implying bank status or fund protection the company cannot support, and because comparative security claims require substantiation under FTC Act §5 and state UDAP law. The campaign targets launch on 2026-10-03, leaving roughly one month to complete a substantiation file and approve final wording.
## Decision question
Should legal approve "bank-level security" and "institution-grade protection" for the 2026-10-03 launch across website, sales materials, partner presentations, and paid search — and if so, with what mandatory qualifiers, substantiation evidence, review controls, and prohibited language — given that Mosaic Relay is not a bank and does not hold customer funds?
## Open questions
- What specific substantiating evidence does Mosaic Relay currently hold (encryption standards, monitoring scope, incident-response metrics, certifications), and does it meet or exceed the security baseline of actual banks?
- What is the primary geographic audience — US only, or does the campaign reach EU/UK/LatAm markets where local advertising rules differ?
- Will the campaign use customer logos or name competitors, and if so, is there documented consent and substantiation for any comparative claim?
- What limitations of coverage exist (what "bank-level" does and does not protect) that would need disclosure to avoid a misleading omission?
- Do acquiring-bank or processor contracts restrict or prescribe security-related marketing language?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The analysis below is model-generated analysis based on general legal principles, not on retrieved citations.
**FTC Act §5 (deceptive practices).** An objective claim like "bank-level security" requires a reasonable basis — competent and reliable evidence — before dissemination. For a non-bank, the claim is deceptive if it conveys (1) that the company is a bank or regulated like one, or (2) a level of security the company cannot document. "Institution-grade protection" compounds the problem by adding a fund-protection implication.
**State UDAP statutes.** Every state has a consumer-protection statute (e.g., California UCL/FAL, New York GBL §349, Texas DTPA) that mirrors or exceeds FTC §5. Several states allow private plaintiffs and statutory damages, raising litigation exposure beyond FTC enforcement.
**Comparative advertising.** "Bank-level" is an implicit comparison to banks. Under FTC policy and the Lanham Act §43(a), comparative claims must be truthful, non-deceptive, and substantiated. The comparison must be against a defined, verifiable baseline — not an abstract notion of what a bank's security is.
**EU/UK advertising rules (if reached).** The EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) and the UK CAP Code (Section 3) prohibit misleading actions and omissions. Comparative claims must be verifiable. Several EU member states impose pre-clearance or stricter substantiation duties for financial-services-adjacent claims.
**Bank-status implication.** Even if the intent is a security-strength comparison, the phrases could cause a reasonable consumer to believe Mosaic Relay is a bank, holds deposits, or provides deposit insurance. That misimpression is independently deceptive and could trigger money-transmission or banking-regulator scrutiny.
**Endorsement and logo use.** If customer logos appear alongside the security claim, FTC endorsement guides (16 CFR Part 255) require that the endorsement reflect the endorser's honest experience and that any material connection be disclosed.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Substantiation file contents.** If Mosaic Relay holds certifications (SOC 2, PCI DSS, ISO 27001) and documented encryption/monitoring standards that meet or exceed common bank baselines, Path A becomes more defensible. If the evidence is thin or outdated, Path A fails.
- **Geographic scope.** If the campaign reaches EU/UK audiences, local counsel review and possibly pre-clearance are required, which could delay launch or require different wording.
- **Customer logos or competitor comparisons.** If either is planned, additional consent documentation and comparative substantiation are required, adding time and narrowing the approved wording.
- **Acquiring-bank or processor contract terms.** If contracts restrict security-related marketing or require partner pre-approval, the launch timeline and approved wording change.
- **Prior legal approvals.** If any version of these phrases was previously approved, the analysis shifts to whether the prior approval covers the current surfaces and audience.

3. **Review acquiring-bank and processor contracts** — check for marketing restrictions, pre-approval requirements, or prescribed security language.
4. **Run an audience-perception check** — test whether the qualified "bank-level security" language still conveys bank status or fund protection to the target audience.
5. **Record approved wording** — document the exact approved phrases, qualifiers, and prohibited language in the matter workspace and distribute to Marketing, Sales, and Product.
6. **Set a re-verification trigger** — require re-approval if any security control changes or annually, whichever comes first.
