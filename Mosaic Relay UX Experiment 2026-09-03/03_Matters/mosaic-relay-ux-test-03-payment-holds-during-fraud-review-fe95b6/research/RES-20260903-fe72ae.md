# Lawful authority to hold merchant proceeds pending fraud review, card

## Question

Lawful authority to hold merchant proceeds pending fraud review, card and ACH timing limits, treatment of held funds, merchant notice and disclosure language, appeal or evidence rights, card-network and processor requirements, and money-transmission implications.

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payments technology company, plans to implement a fraud-review control that temporarily holds merchant card and ACH proceeds when transaction-risk signals indicate possible fraud. The proposed UX displays a banner to the merchant, blocks payout for the affected amount, and allows the merchant to submit invoices or fulfillment evidence; Operations may then release the funds, extend the review, reverse the payment, or deactivate processing. Counsel is involved because the company needs to confirm lawful authority to hold funds, determine timing limits, establish disclosure and appeal requirements, and ensure compliance with card-network rules and money-transmission laws before the Product team launches an initial version in one quarter.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay lawfully implement a one-quarter-launch fraud-review hold on merchant card and ACH proceeds that blocks payout pending investigation, and if so, what maximum hold periods, disclosure language, appeal rights, and funds-treatment rules must the design include to comply with contract law, card-network rules, ACH regulations, and money-transmission requirements?
## Open questions
- What does the current merchant agreement permit regarding holds, and does it require amendment to authorize the proposed fraud-review hold?
- What maximum hold period will Mosaic Relay set for card versus ACH transactions, given that network rules and NACHA requirements may differ?
- May held funds be used to cover chargebacks, returns, or fees during the review period, or must they remain segregated and untouched?
- What specific appeal process and evidence-submission rights must be provided to merchants to satisfy due-process and card-network requirements?
- Does holding merchant proceeds pending review trigger any state money-transmission licensing or safeguarding requirements beyond Mosaic Relay's current compliance posture?

## Likely rules and issues
### Contractual authority and property rights
**Core issue:** Mosaic Relay's authority to hold merchant proceeds derives primarily from the merchant agreement. Without clear contractual authorization, holding funds may constitute conversion, breach of contract, or unjust enrichment.
**Likely rules:**
- **Contract law:** The merchant agreement must expressly authorize Mosaic Relay to delay payout, hold proceeds pending fraud review, and specify the conditions, duration, and merchant rights during the hold. Silence or vague language creates litigation risk.
- **Property law:** Once a transaction settles, the merchant has a property interest in the proceeds (less fees and reserves). Mosaic Relay holds those funds as agent or bailee, not owner. Unauthorized retention may be conversion.
- **UCC Article 4A (ACH):** For ACH transactions, funds are typically credited to the merchant's account (or Mosaic Relay's settlement account for the merchant's benefit). Holding funds after final settlement may require specific contractual authority and compliance with NACHA rules.
**Practical consequence:** If the current merchant agreement does not clearly authorize fraud-review holds, Mosaic Relay must amend the agreement and provide notice to existing merchants before launching the feature. Unilateral holds without contractual basis expose the company to breach-of-contract and conversion claims.
### Card-network rules (Visa, Mastercard, etc.)
**Core issue:** Card networks impose strict rules on merchant holds, reserves, and delayed payouts, especially when the hold is not a standard reserve but a fraud-review mechanism.
**Likely rules:**
- **Visa and Mastercard rules:** Both networks allow acquirers and payment facilitators to hold funds or establish reserves to manage risk, but they require clear contractual terms, reasonable hold periods, and merchant notice. Indefinite or punitive holds may violate network rules.
- **Chargeback and dispute rights:** Networks require that merchants be given an opportunity to respond to fraud claims and provide evidence. A hold that prevents the merchant from accessing funds without a clear process to contest the hold may conflict with network dispute-resolution rules.
- **Timing limits:** Networks generally expect fraud reviews to be completed promptly. Holds exceeding 30–90 days without resolution may trigger network scrutiny or require escalation to the acquirer.
**Practical consequence:** Mosaic Relay must confirm with its acquiring bank and processor that the proposed hold structure complies with network rules, including maximum hold periods, notice requirements, and merchant appeal rights. The acquirer may impose additional conditions or require Mosaic Relay to follow specific fraud-review protocols.
### ACH rules (NACHA)
**Core issue:** ACH transactions are governed by NACHA rules, which impose strict timing and finality requirements. Holding ACH proceeds after settlement may conflict with NACHA's prompt-crediting and availability rules.
**Likely rules:**
- **NACHA Operating Rules:** Once an ACH credit is received, funds must be made available to the recipient (or the recipient's agent) promptly, typically by the next business day. Delaying availability for fraud review may require specific contractual authorization and compliance with NACHA's risk-management provisions.
- **Return and reversal rights:** NACHA allows returns for unauthorized transactions, but the return window is short (typically 2 banking days for most returns, up to 60 days for unauthorized consumer debits). Holding funds beyond the return window may prevent Mosaic Relay from reversing fraudulent transactions.
- **ODFI/RDFI obligations:** Mosaic Relay's acquiring bank (ODFI) and the merchant's bank (RDFI) have specific obligations under NACHA. Mosaic Relay must coordinate with its ODFI to ensure the hold does not violate NACHA timing or settlement rules.
**Practical consequence:** ACH holds must be shorter than card holds, and Mosaic Relay must ensure that the hold period does not exceed the NACHA return window if it intends to reverse fraudulent transactions. The company should consult with its ODFI to confirm that the proposed hold structure is permissible.
### Money transmission and safeguarding
**Core issue:** Holding merchant proceeds may trigger state money-transmission licensing requirements or require Mosaic Relay to safeguard funds in a specific manner (e.g., segregation, permissible investments).
**Likely rules:**
- **State money-transmission laws:** Most states regulate the receipt and transmission of money. If Mosaic Relay holds merchant funds for more than a short settlement period, it may be deemed to be engaged in money transmission, requiring licenses in each state where merchants are located.
- **Safeguarding requirements:** Licensed money transmitters must typically segregate customer funds, maintain permissible investments (e.g., cash, government securities), and undergo regular audits. Holding funds for extended fraud reviews may trigger these requirements.
- **Agent-of-payee exemption:** Some states exempt payment processors from money-transmission licensing if they act as agent of the payee (merchant) under a written agreement and funds are held for the merchant's benefit. The exemption typically requires prompt remittance and may not cover extended holds.
**Practical consequence:** Mosaic Relay must confirm whether its current money-transmission licenses (if any) cover the proposed hold structure, or whether the agent-of-payee exemption applies. If the hold period exceeds typical settlement timelines, the company may need to obtain additional licenses or restructure the hold to avoid triggering safeguarding requirements.
### Consumer protection and unfair or deceptive acts or practices (UDAP)
**Core issue:** Holding merchant funds without clear disclosure, reasonable timing, or an appeal process may be deemed an unfair or deceptive practice under state UDAP laws or Section 5 of the FTC Act.
**Likely rules:**
- **FTC Act Section 5:** Unfair practices are those that cause substantial injury to consumers (or businesses) that is not reasonably avoidable and not outweighed by countervailing benefits. Holding funds indefinitely without notice or appeal rights may be unfair.
- **State UDAP laws:** Many states prohibit deceptive practices, including failing to disclose material terms (e.g., hold policies) or misrepresenting the reasons for a hold.
- **Plain-language disclosure:** Consumer-protection principles require clear, conspicuous disclosure of hold policies, timing, and appeal rights before the merchant agrees to the terms.
**Practical consequence:** Mosaic Relay must provide clear, plain-language disclosure of the hold policy in the merchant agreement and at the point of the hold (e.g., the banner). The disclosure must explain the reason for the hold, the expected duration, the merchant's rights, and the appeal process.
### Appeal and due-process rights
**Core issue:** Merchants must have a meaningful opportunity to contest the hold and provide evidence. A hold without an appeal process may violate contract law, network rules, and UDAP principles.
**Likely rules:**
- **Contractual due process:** The merchant agreement should specify the evidence merchants can submit, the timeline for review, and the process for escalating disputes.
- **Network dispute rights:** Card networks require that merchants be given an opportunity to respond to fraud claims and provide evidence. A hold that prevents the merchant from accessing funds without a clear process to contest the hold may conflict with network rules.
- **UDAP principles:** An appeal process that is illusory or unreasonably burdensome may be deemed unfair or deceptive.
**Practical consequence:** The proposed UX (banner, evidence submission, operations review) is a good start, but Mosaic Relay must ensure the process is documented in the merchant agreement, provides clear timelines, and allows merchants to escalate disputes to a human reviewer.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Amend merchant agreement and launch with conservative hold limits
**Description:** Amend the merchant agreement to expressly authorize fraud-review holds, specify maximum hold periods (e.g., 30 days for card, 5 business days for ACH), disclose the appeal process, and confirm that held funds will not be used for chargebacks or fees unless the merchant consents or the agreement permits it.
**Pros:**
- Provides clear contractual authority and reduces litigation risk.
- Aligns with card-network and NACHA timing expectations.
- Demonstrates good-faith compliance with UDAP and consumer-protection principles.
**Cons:**
- Requires notice to existing merchants and may require consent (depending on the amendment clause in the current agreement).
- May delay launch if the amendment process is complex.
**Next steps:**
1. Review the current merchant agreement to determine whether it permits fraud-review holds and whether it can be amended unilaterally.
2. Draft an amendment that authorizes holds, specifies timing limits, discloses the appeal process, and addresses funds treatment.
3. Provide notice to existing merchants and obtain consent if required.
4. Confirm with acquiring bank and processor that the hold structure complies with network rules.
5. Launch the feature with the amended agreement in place.
### Path 2: Launch as pilot with limited scope and manual review
**Description:** Launch the fraud-review hold as a pilot for a small group of high-risk merchants, with manual review by operations and a short maximum hold period (e.g., 10 business days). Use the pilot to gather data and refine the process before a broader launch.
**Pros:**
- Reduces risk by limiting the scope and allowing for manual oversight.
- Provides real-world data to refine hold periods, appeal processes, and disclosure language.
- May not require immediate amendment of the merchant agreement if the pilot is framed as a voluntary program.
**Cons:**
- May not scale to the full merchant base without additional legal and operational work.
- Manual review is resource-intensive and may not be sustainable.
**Next steps:**
1. Identify a small group of high-risk merchants for the pilot.
2. Obtain explicit consent from pilot participants to the hold terms.
3. Implement manual review and a short maximum hold period.
4. Monitor outcomes and gather feedback.
5. Use pilot data to refine the broader launch plan.
### Path 3: Restructure as a reserve or rolling hold
**Description:** Instead of holding specific transaction proceeds, establish a rolling reserve (e.g., 5–10% of daily settlements) that is held for a fixed period (e.g., 90 days) to cover chargebacks, fraud losses, and fees. This is a common industry practice and may be easier to justify under network rules and money-transmission laws.
**Pros:**
- Aligns with industry practice and network expectations for risk management.
- Provides a clear, predictable framework for merchants.
- May reduce the need for case-by-case fraud-review holds.
**Cons:**
- Ties up merchant funds for a longer period, which may be unpopular.
- May not address the specific fraud-review use case as effectively as a targeted hold.
**Next steps:**
1. Evaluate whether a rolling reserve would achieve the same risk-management goals as a fraud-review hold.
2. Confirm with acquiring bank and processor that the reserve structure complies with network rules.
3. Amend the merchant agreement to authorize the reserve and disclose the terms.
4. Launch the reserve program alongside or instead of the fraud-review hold.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Current merchant agreement terms:** If the agreement already authorizes fraud-review holds, the analysis shifts to whether the proposed UX and timing comply with the agreement and network rules. If the agreement is silent or prohibits holds, Mosaic Relay must amend it before launch.
- **Acquiring bank and processor requirements:** If the acquiring bank or processor imposes stricter hold limits, notice requirements, or appeal processes than the networks, Mosaic Relay must comply with those requirements.
- **State money-transmission licenses:** If Mosaic Relay holds licenses in states with strict safeguarding or timing requirements, the hold structure must comply with those laws. If the company relies on the agent-of-payee exemption, the hold period must be short enough to avoid triggering licensing requirements.
- **Merchant base and risk profile:** If the merchant base includes high-risk industries (e.g., travel, ticketing, adult), networks and regulators may expect longer hold periods and more robust appeal processes.
- **Fraud-loss history:** If Mosaic Relay has a history of significant fraud losses, networks and regulators may be more tolerant of aggressive hold policies. If losses are low, indefinite or punitive holds may be deemed unfair.
