# EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and national advertising-law requiremen

## Question

EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and national advertising-law requirements for comparative claims and substantiation

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay’s Product team wants to launch a LATAM and Europe marketing campaign in seven weeks promoting new local payment methods using the claims “accept local payments everywhere,” “lower-cost payments,” and “fewer failed transactions.” The campaign would run through country-specific landing pages and sales emails, with fee, refund-support, and approval-rate comparisons shown to merchants. Counsel is involved because the substantiation file is thin: no validated cost study or benchmark exists, approval-rate data comes only from a small internal pilot, and permitted wording per jurisdiction is unconfirmed. The consequence of proceeding as drafted is exposure under EU and national advertising and consumer-protection regimes in every campaign market.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay launch the LATAM/EU local-payment-methods campaign in seven weeks using the three proposed comparative claims, or must the campaign be re-scoped to narrower, country-specific factual claims — and which claims, if any, can be substantiated and legally approved before launch given that no validated cost study, benchmark, or jurisdiction-specific wording review currently exists?
## Open questions
- Which specific countries in LATAM and Europe are in scope, and which payment methods are confirmed live in each? (The exclusion list is not confirmed; this determines which national advertising regimes apply and what the factual availability claim can say.)
- Do the fee figures Product intends to publish include processor and foreign-exchange charges, or only Mosaic Relay’s own fees? (Unknown — this determines whether any cost comparison can be non-misleading.)
- Can a validated comparative cost study and a defined benchmark (baseline, time period, countries, methods, like-for-like measures) be completed within the seven-week window, or only for a subset of claims?
- Which local processors, acquiring banks, and networks require pre-launch review or approval of marketing that names their methods, and what are their timelines?
- What local disclosure or wording restrictions attach to specific methods (e.g., installment/credit-adjacent methods, cash-voucher methods) in each launch country?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-generated analysis of the applicable frameworks, not retrieved or verified authority. It should be confirmed against current texts and local counsel before reliance.
**EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC, as amended by the Omnibus Directive (EU) 2019/2161).** The UCPD prohibits misleading actions (Art. 6) and misleading omissions (Art. 7) in business-to-consumer commercial practices, and Member State implementations extend misleading-advertising rules to B2B comparative advertising via the Misleading and Comparative Advertising Directive (2006/114/EC). Three pressure points for this campaign:
1. **“Accept local payments everywhere”** is an absolute availability claim. Under Art. 6 UCPD and national equivalents, an unqualified geographic/availability claim that is false for any material part of the advertised market is misleading. Because support differs by country and method, the claim as written is not defensible anywhere; even a qualified version must name the actual footprint.
2. **“Lower-cost payments”** is a comparative price claim. Comparative advertising is lawful only if it compares like-for-like, is based on material, relevant, verifiable, and representative features (price is expressly named in 2006/114/EC Art. 4), and is not misleading. With no validated cost study, no defined benchmark, and unknown FX/processor-fee treatment, this claim currently fails every element. If fees exclude FX or processor charges, the comparison is also a misleading omission under UCPD Art. 7.
3. **“Fewer failed transactions”** is an objective performance claim requiring substantiation with evidence available *before* the claim is made. A small internal pilot, without a defined baseline, period, and representative sample, is not adequate substantiation under the substantiation expectations applied by national regulators (e.g., Germany’s Wettbewerbszentrale enforcement practice, France’s DGCCRF, and the EU average-consumer standard from CJEU case law such as *Gut Springenheide*). Approval-rate comparisons shown to merchants in dashboards or emails are commercial communications subject to the same rules.
**National advertising laws.** Each launch country transposes these directives with local variations: Germany (UWG — aggressive enforcement by competitors and standing associations; comparative claims invite cease-and-desist letters), France (Code de la consommation; DGCCRF scrutiny of price claims), Spain, Italy, Netherlands, Poland, and the Nordics each have self-regulatory bodies (e.g., Deutscher Werberat, ARPP, AUTOCONTROL) whose codes require substantiation on demand. In LATAM: Brazil (CDC arts. 6, 30, 36–37 — objective claims in advertising bind the supplier; CONAR self-regulation; consumer-protection agencies can fine), Mexico (PROFECO under the LFPC — advertising must be truthful and verifiable; comparative claims must be provable), Colombia (SIC — Decreto 1366/Reglamento de Publicidad requires prior substantiation), Chile (SERNAC, Ley 19.496), Argentina (Ley 24.240 and fair-trading law). Several LATAM regimes treat advertised claims as binding offers and impose reversal-of-burden or prior-substantiation duties — the advertiser must hold the evidence before publishing.
**Pricing transparency.** If published fee comparisons omit processor charges or FX markups, the comparison is misleading both as an action (false impression of total cost) and as an omission (material information withheld). EU rules on price indication and the Omnibus Directive’s transparency amendments reinforce total-price disclosure expectations; Brazil and Mexico similarly require total-price clarity.
**Consumer-protection and method-specific disclosures.** Some local methods carry statutory disclosure or restriction regimes (e.g., cash-based voucher methods, installment methods that may be credit-regulated, methods with no refund/chargeback rights). Marketing that presents such methods as equivalent to cards without disclosing refund and protection differences creates Art. 6/7 UCPD and national-law exposure, and may violate processor/network rules.
**Partner review.** Local processors, acquiring banks, and networks commonly contractually require pre-approval of marketing that names their methods or displays their marks, plus compliance with their brand and claims guidelines. This is a gating operational item, not just a legal nicety.
## Viable paths
**Path A — Re-scoped factual campaign within seven weeks (recommended for the deadline).** Launch with claims limited to what Operations confirms is live: named countries, named methods, eligibility, and factual feature descriptions (settlement timing, refund availability) stated as facts per method, not comparisons. Drop “everywhere,” “lower-cost,” and “fewer failed transactions” entirely. Publish the fee comparison only if it shows total cost including processor and FX charges, per method, per country, with the basis stated. This path is achievable in the window and keeps the sales campaign on schedule.
**Path B — Hold comparative claims pending substantiation.** Keep the launch date for the factual campaign; run the validated cost study and define the approval-rate benchmark (baseline, period, countries, methods, like-for-like measures) in parallel; release comparative claims in a later wave once substantiation exists and local counsel clears wording per country. Product has already indicated claims can be qualified with conditions/exclusions, which supports this sequencing.
**Path C — Launch as drafted.** Not viable. No comparative claim is substantiated today; “everywhere” is false on the known facts; proceeding invites competitor complaints (especially Germany), regulator action (DGCCRF, PROFECO, SIC, SERNAC), self-regulatory challenges, and partner contract breaches.
## Facts that could change the answer
- If the fee figures turn out to include processor and FX charges and a defensible like-for-like dataset exists internally, a narrow, qualified cost claim (e.g., “lower total fees than [defined baseline] for [named methods] in [named countries], based on [period] data”) might clear in some jurisdictions — but still needs per-country wording review.
- If the pilot data covers a representative volume and period for specific named methods/countries, a qualified approval-rate statement limited to that scope might be supportable with the methodology disclosed.
- If the in-scope country list shrinks to a few markets, local review becomes fast and cheap; if it spans most of the EU plus five LATAM countries, Path A is the only realistic seven-week option.
- If partner contracts impose long pre-approval windows, even the factual campaign’s timeline compresses.

2. Have Finance confirm whether fee figures include processor and FX charges; if not, rebuild the comparison on a total-cost basis or drop it.
3. Commission the cost study and benchmark definition now (Product + Finance own this; it is the standing work item).
4. Instruct local counsel in each launch country to clear final wording — prioritizing Germany, France, Brazil, and Mexico as the highest-enforcement-risk markets.
5. Obtain written partner/processor marketing approvals before any asset names a method or displays a mark.
6. Build the substantiation file (studies, data pulls, methodology, approvals) before publication, since several regimes require evidence to exist prior to the claim.

## What would change this
1. **Working assumption — enforcement posture of the specific launch countries.** The analysis assumes a mixed EU/LATAM footprint including high-enforcement markets (Germany, France, Brazil, Mexico). If the actual scope is, say, only Spain, Portugal, and Chile, the competitor-complaint risk drops materially and some qualified claims might clear faster. The country list is unconfirmed and the vault could not supply it.
2. **Working assumption — no substantiation exists beyond the pilot.** The answer treats the small internal pilot as the only evidence. If Product or Finance holds unpublished transaction data covering a representative period, the “fewer failed transactions” claim could move from “drop” to “qualify and disclose methodology” for named methods — the recommendation on Path B timing would change.
3. **Open fork — fee figures include or exclude processor/FX charges.** If inclusive, a qualified total-cost comparison becomes possible in some markets; if exclusive (or unknown at launch), any cost comparison is a misleading-omission risk and must be dropped or rebuilt. This single fact determines whether the fee-comparison feature ships at all.
4. **Open fork — partner pre-approval timelines.** If local processors or acquirers require multi-week marketing review, even the re-scoped factual campaign may miss the seven-week date in some countries, forcing a staggered launch; if approvals are fast or unnecessary for unbranded mentions, the full footprint can launch together.
