# First-pass analysis: Consortium data sharing with fraud/identity vendors
**Bottom line up front:** The merchant opt-in design, as described, is **not sufficient on its own** to authorize the sharing, because the data subjects are **consumers**, not merchants. The merchant's opt-in is a necessary contractual/authorization step, but it does not substitute for the consumer-facing notice and rights that U.S. privacy law (and, if applicable, state biometric law) requires. The design can be made workable, but it needs consumer notice, a defensible de-identification standard, and contractual controls on vendors — several of which are currently undecided.
This is a first pass built on assumptions. Every section below flags what is assumed, what is missing, and what is unverified.
---
## 1. Permissible data sharing
**What's permissible now.** Sharing transaction, device, identity, and dispute signals with fraud/identity vendors to detect fraud is a legitimate, recognized purpose under U.S. law. It is not categorically prohibited. The analysis turns on *which* fields, *with whom*, *for what*, and *under what notice/consent regime*.
**The controlling question is Mosaic Relay's role** (controller vs. processor), which is currently undecided. That role determines who must provide notice and obtain consent:
- If **Mosaic Relay is the controller** (determines purposes/means), it bears primary responsibility for consumer notice, lawful basis, and responding to consumer rights.
- If **Mosaic Relay is a processor** acting on merchants' instructions, the merchants are the controllers and bear primary responsibility — but Mosaic Relay still must ensure its vendor contracts and data flows comply, and it cannot independently repurpose the data.
**Key risk — the "sale"/"share" question.** Under CCPA/CPRA, sharing personal information with a vendor that uses it for its *own* purposes (e.g., combining with its own datasets, training models, or building a consortium-wide risk database) can constitute a **"sale" or "share"** of personal information, triggering consumer **opt-out** rights regardless of what the merchant agreed to. If vendors merely process on Mosaic Relay's instructions as service providers, this risk is lower. This is a central fork and is currently undecided.
**Assumption:** U.S.-primary jurisdiction; CCPA/CPRA and analogous state laws apply to California consumers at minimum. **Missing:** which states' consumers are in scope, and whether any non-U.S. consumers are involved (which would change the analysis materially).

## 2. Notices and consent
**The merchant opt-in is not the operative consent.** The data subjects are consumers. A merchant's opt-in authorizes *Mosaic Relay's* sharing of data the merchant controls, but it does not satisfy consumer-facing obligations.
**What is needed:**
- **Consumer notice** at or before the point of collection, disclosing the categories of personal information collected, the purposes (fraud detection), and the categories of third parties (vendors) with whom it is shared. Under CCPA/CPRA this is a baseline requirement.
- **Consumer opt-out** if the sharing constitutes a "sale" or "share" (see §1). If it does, consumers must get a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" mechanism.
- **Merchant opt-in** remains useful as a contractual authorization and as a control on which merchants' data enters the consortium — but it is *in addition to*, not *instead of*, consumer-facing notice.
**Assumption:** The "general explanation" in the dashboard is a merchant-facing description, not consumer notice. **Missing:** whether consumers receive any notice at all (currently undecided).

## 3. De-identification standards
**Pseudonymization is not de-identification.** The request says consumer data would be "pseudonymized where possible." Pseudonymization (replacing identifiers with tokens) is *not* the same as de-identification under U.S. law, and it does **not** remove the data from privacy-law coverage if the data can be re-linked.
**The hard fields.** Device identifiers, IP addresses, and payment tokens are the problem:
- **IP addresses** are generally treated as personal information and are often re-identifiable.
- **Device identifiers** (e.g., IDFA, device fingerprints) are frequently personal information and can be re-linked.
- **Payment tokens** are sensitive and, if re-linkable to the underlying account, carry high risk.
**What is achievable:** True de-identification (removing or irreversibly transforming identifiers so the data cannot reasonably be re-identified) is achievable for *some* fields but not for the fields that make the consortium valuable — a fraud consortium needs the ability to link a device or IP across merchants, which is precisely what makes the data re-identifiable. **This is a genuine tension:** the more useful the shared signal is for cross-merchant fraud detection, the harder it is to de-identify.
**Recommendation direction:** Do not promise "pseudonymized where possible" as a compliance shield. Instead, apply data minimization (share only the fields needed), use tokenization where feasible, and treat the data as personal information subject to the full notice/rights regime rather than relying on de-identification to avoid it.
**Assumption:** No specific de-identification standard (e.g., HIPAA Safe Harbor, FTC guidance) has been selected. **Missing:** the actual fields to be shared and whether any are truly de-identifiable.

- **Set retention limits** and require **deletion with certification**.

- **Correct** inaccurate information.
If identity-verification results are shared, state biometric laws add strict consent and destruction duties. Mosaic Relay needs a mechanism to honor access and deletion requests across the consortium — which is complicated because the data is shared with vendors who may hold it independently.

## 8. Whether the merchant opt-in design is sufficient
**No — not as described.** The merchant opt-in:
- Is a **necessary** contractual authorization from the merchant (the party that controls the data).
- Is **not sufficient** to satisfy consumer-facing obligations, because consumers are the data subjects.
**What would make it sufficient:**
1. **Consumer notice** at collection (and opt-out if the sharing is a sale/share).
2. **Merchant opt-in** retained as a contractual control.
3. **Vendor contractual constraints** (no combining/training, retention limits, deletion).
4. **A defensible de-identification/minimization approach** that does not over-rely on "pseudonymization."
5. **A consumer rights mechanism** (access/deletion) across the consortium.
**Special flag — identity-verification results.** If the shared data includes identity-verification results derived from biometrics, state biometric statutes (Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, Washington) may apply, with written-consent, retention, and private-right-of-action exposure. This is a material risk that the current design does not address. (This mirrors the analysis in the related identity-verification matter.)
