    # First-pass analysis: Consortium data sharing with fraud/identity vendors

    **Bottom line up front:** The merchant opt-in design, as described, is **not sufficient
    on its own** to authorize the sharing, because the data subjects are **consumers**,
    not merchants. The merchant''s opt-in is a necessary contractual/authorization
    step, but it does not substitute for the consumer-facing notice and rights that
    U.S. privacy law (and, if applicable, state biometric law) requires. The design
    can be made workable, but it needs consumer notice, a defensible de-identification
    standard, and contractual controls on vendors — several of which are currently
    undecided.

    This is a first pass built on assumptions. Every section below flags what is assumed,
    what is missing, and what is unverified.

    ---

    ## 1. Permissible data sharing

    **What''s permissible now.** Sharing transaction, device, identity, and dispute
    signals with fraud/identity vendors to detect fraud is a legitimate, recognized
    purpose under U.S. law. It is not categorically prohibited. The analysis turns
    on *which* fields, *with whom*, *for what*, and *under what notice/consent regime*.

    **The controlling question is Mosaic Relay''s role** (controller vs. processor),
    which is currently undecided. That role determines who must provide notice and
    obtain consent:

    - If **Mosaic Relay is the controller** (determines purposes/means), it bears
    primary responsibility for consumer notice, lawful basis, and responding to consumer
    rights.

    - If **Mosaic Relay is a processor** acting on merchants'' instructions, the merchants
    are the controllers and bear primary responsibility — but Mosaic Relay still must
    ensure its vendor contracts and data flows comply, and it cannot independently
    repurpose the data.

    **Key risk — the "sale"/"share" question.** Under CCPA/CPRA, sharing personal
    information with a vendor that uses it for its *own* purposes (e.g., combining
    with its own datasets, training models, or building a consortium-wide risk database)
    can constitute a **"sale" or "share"** of personal information, triggering consumer
    **opt-out** rights regardless of what the merchant agreed to. If vendors merely
    process on Mosaic Relay''s instructions as service providers, this risk is lower.
    This is a central fork and is currently undecided.

    **Assumption:** U.S.-primary jurisdiction; CCPA/CPRA and analogous state laws
    apply to California consumers at minimum. **Missing:** which states'' consumers
    are in scope, and whether any non-U.S. consumers are involved (which would change
    the analysis materially).

    ---

    ## 2. Notices and consent

    **The merchant opt-in is not the operative consent.** The data subjects are consumers.
    A merchant''s opt-in authorizes *Mosaic Relay''s* sharing of data the merchant
    controls, but it does not satisfy consumer-facing obligations.

    **What is needed:**

    - **Consumer notice** at or before the point of collection, disclosing the categories
    of personal information collected, the purposes (fraud detection), and the categories
    of third parties (vendors) with whom it is shared. Under CCPA/CPRA this is a baseline
    requirement.

    - **Consumer opt-out** if the sharing constitutes a "sale" or "share" (see §1).
    If it does, consumers must get a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information"
    mechanism.

    - **Merchant opt-in** remains useful as a contractual authorization and as a control
    on which merchants'' data enters the consortium — but it is *in addition to*,
    not *instead of*, consumer-facing notice.

    **Assumption:** The "general explanation" in the dashboard is a merchant-facing
    description, not consumer notice. **Missing:** whether consumers receive any notice
    at all (currently undecided).

    ---

    ## 3. De-identification standards

    **Pseudonymization is not de-identification.** The request says consumer data
    would be "pseudonymized where possible." Pseudonymization (replacing identifiers
    with tokens) is *not* the same as de-identification under U.S. law, and it does
    **not** remove the data from privacy-law coverage if the data can be re-linked.

    **The hard fields.** Device identifiers, IP addresses, and payment tokens are
    the problem:

    - **IP addresses** are generally treated as personal information and are often
    re-identifiable.

    - **Device identifiers** (e.g., IDFA, device fingerprints) are frequently personal
    information and can be re-linked.

    - **Payment tokens** are sensitive and, if re-linkable to the underlying account,
    carry high risk.

    **What is achievable:** True de-identification (removing or irreversibly transforming
    identifiers so the data cannot reasonably be re-identified) is achievable for
    *some* fields but not for the fields that make the consortium valuable — a fraud
    consortium needs the ability to link a device or IP across merchants, which is
    precisely what makes the data re-identifiable. **This is a genuine tension:**
    the more useful the shared signal is for cross-merchant fraud detection, the harder
    it is to de-identify.

    **Recommendation direction:** Do not promise "pseudonymized where possible" as
    a compliance shield. Instead, apply data minimization (share only the fields needed),
    use tokenization where feasible, and treat the data as personal information subject
    to the full notice/rights regime rather than relying on de-identification to avoid
    it.

    **Assumption:** No specific de-identification standard (e.g., HIPAA Safe Harbor,
    FTC guidance) has been selected. **Missing:** the actual fields to be shared and
    whether any are truly de-identifiable.

    ---

    ## 4. Contractual controls

    **Vendor contracts are the primary control** and are currently undecided. The
    critical terms:

    - **Prohibit combining** shared data with vendors'' own datasets and **prohibit
    model training** unless explicitly approved — otherwise the sharing likely becomes
    a "sale"/"share" and the vendor becomes a third party rather than a service provider.

    - **Restrict use** to returning risk indicators for the fraud purpose only.

    - **Set retention limits** and require **deletion with certification**.

    - **Include CCPA-compliant service-provider terms** (if Mosaic Relay is the controller)
    or processor terms (if GDPR applies).

    - **Breach notification** and security obligations.

    **Assumption:** Vendors are currently unconstrained. **Missing:** the actual vendor
    agreements and whether vendors already hold their own datasets (which makes "combining"
    risk real).

    ---

    ## 5. Cross-border transfers

    **Currently undecided where data is hosted/transferred.** If all processing stays
    in the U.S., the analysis is U.S.-state-law driven. If any data moves outside
    the U.S. (or involves non-U.S. consumers), GDPR or other regimes may apply, requiring
    transfer safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses) and potentially a DPIA.

    **Assumption:** U.S.-only hosting unless told otherwise. **Missing:** hosting
    location and whether any non-U.S. consumers are in scope.

    ---

    ## 6. Retention

    **Currently undecided.** Retention should be the shortest period reasonably necessary
    for the fraud-detection purpose, documented with a risk-based rationale, with
    a defined deletion workflow and vendor deletion certification. If identity-verification
    results are shared, state biometric laws (BIPA, CUBI, Washington) impose strict
    retention limits (1–3 years) and destruction obligations — see §8.

    ---

    ## 7. Consumer rights

    **Currently undecided whether consumers get notice or choice.** If the sharing
    is a "sale"/"share" under CCPA/CPRA, consumers have the right to:

    - **Know** what is collected and shared.

    - **Opt out** of sale/share.

    - **Delete** their personal information.

    - **Correct** inaccurate information.

    If identity-verification results are shared, state biometric laws add strict consent
    and destruction duties. Mosaic Relay needs a mechanism to honor access and deletion
    requests across the consortium — which is complicated because the data is shared
    with vendors who may hold it independently.

    ---

    ## 8. Whether the merchant opt-in design is sufficient

    **No — not as described.** The merchant opt-in:

    - Is a **necessary** contractual authorization from the merchant (the party that
    controls the data).

    - Is **not sufficient** to satisfy consumer-facing obligations, because consumers
    are the data subjects.

    **What would make it sufficient:**

    1. **Consumer notice** at collection (and opt-out if the sharing is a sale/share).

    2. **Merchant opt-in** retained as a contractual control.

    3. **Vendor contractual constraints** (no combining/training, retention limits,
    deletion).

    4. **A defensible de-identification/minimization approach** that does not over-rely
    on "pseudonymization."

    5. **A consumer rights mechanism** (access/deletion) across the consortium.

    **Special flag — identity-verification results.** If the shared data includes
    identity-verification results derived from biometrics, state biometric statutes
    (Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, Washington) may apply, with written-consent, retention,
    and private-right-of-action exposure. This is a material risk that the current
    design does not address. (This mirrors the analysis in the related identity-verification
    matter.)

    ---

    ## Recommendation (separate from any recorded decision)

    Proceed with the consortium **only after** (a) resolving Mosaic Relay''s controller/processor
    role, (b) adding consumer notice and a sale/share opt-out mechanism, (c) contractually
    constraining vendors (no combining/training, retention limits, deletion), and
    (d) confirming whether identity-verification results trigger state biometric law.
    The merchant opt-in alone is insufficient. No decision has been recorded; this
    is a recommendation for counsel''s consideration.

    ---

    ## What would change this

    - **Working assumption — controller/processor role.** The analysis assumes the
    role is undecided and treats Mosaic Relay as bearing primary responsibility. If
    Mosaic Relay is a pure processor acting on merchants'' instructions, consumer
    notice/consent shifts to merchants, but Mosaic Relay still must ensure vendor
    contracts and data flows comply. If it is a joint controller, obligations are
    shared.

    - **Working assumption — U.S.-only.** The analysis assumes U.S.-primary jurisdiction.
    If any data is hosted or consumers are located outside the U.S., GDPR or other
    regimes apply and materially change the cross-border, DPIA, and consent analysis.

    - **Open fork — "sale"/"share."** If vendors combine shared data with their own
    datasets or train models, the sharing is likely a "sale"/"share" under CCPA/CPRA,
    triggering consumer opt-out rights. If vendors act purely as service providers,
    this risk is lower. This is undecided and is the single most decision-changing
    item after the role question.

    - **Open fork — identity-verification results.** If shared data includes biometric-derived
    identity-verification results, BIPA/CUBI/Washington may apply with written consent,
    retention, and private-right-of-action exposure. If no biometric data is shared,
    this risk drops out.

    - **Not examined — vendor agreements.** The actual vendor contracts, vendors''
    existing datasets, and vendors'' processing locations were not reviewed. These
    determine whether "combining" is a real risk and whether cross-border obligations
    apply.

    - **Not examined — state scope.** The specific U.S. states whose consumers are
    in scope were not identified; CCPA/CPRA is assumed as the baseline. Other state
    laws (e.g., Colorado, Virginia, Connecticut) may add or vary obligations.

  # First-pass analysis: Consortium data sharing with fraud/identity vendors

  **Bottom line up front:** The merchant opt-in design, as described, is **not sufficient
  on its own** to authorize the sharing, because the data subjects are **consumers**,
  not merchants. The merchant''s opt-in is a necessary contractual/authorization step,
  but it does not substitute for the consumer-facing notice and rights that U.S. privacy
  law (and, if applicable, state biometric law) requires. The design can be made workable,
  but it needs consumer notice, a defensible de-identification standard, and contractual
  controls on vendors — several of which are currently undecided.

  This is a first pass built on assumptions. Every section below flags what is assumed,
  what is missing, and what is unverified.

  ---

  ## 1. Permissible data sharing

  **What''s permissible now.** Sharing transaction, device, identity, and dispute
  signals with fraud/identity vendors to detect fraud is a legitimate, recognized
  purpose under U.S. law. It is not categorically prohibited. The analysis turns on
  *which* fields, *with whom*, *for what*, and *under what notice/consent regime*.

  **The controlling question is Mosaic Relay''s role** (controller vs. processor),
  which is currently undecided. That role determines who must provide notice and obtain
  consent:

  - If **Mosaic Relay is the controller** (determines purposes/means), it bears primary
  responsibility for consumer notice, lawful basis, and responding to consumer rights.

  - If **Mosaic Relay is a processor** acting on merchants'' instructions, the merchants
  are the controllers and bear primary responsibility — but Mosaic Relay still must
  ensure its vendor contracts and data flows comply, and it cannot independently repurpose
  the data.

  **Key risk — the "sale"/"share" question.** Under CCPA/CPRA, sharing personal information
  with a vendor that uses it for its *own* purposes (e.g., combining with its own
  datasets, training models, or building a consortium-wide risk database) can constitute
  a **"sale" or "share"** of personal information, triggering consumer **opt-out**
  rights regardless of what the merchant agreed to. If vendors merely process on Mosaic
  Relay''s instructions as service providers, this risk is lower. This is a central
  fork and is currently undecided.

  **Assumption:** U.S.-primary jurisdiction; CCPA/CPRA and analogous state laws apply
  to California consumers at minimum. **Missing:** which states'' consumers are in
  scope, and whether any non-U.S. consumers are involved (which would change the analysis
  materially).

  ---

  ## 2. Notices and consent

  **The merchant opt-in is not the operative consent.** The data subjects are consumers.
  A merchant''s opt-in authorizes *Mosaic Relay''s* sharing of data the merchant controls,
  but it does not satisfy consumer-facing obligations.

  **What is needed:**

  - **Consumer notice** at or before the point of collection, disclosing the categories
  of personal information collected, the purposes (fraud detection), and the categories
  of third parties (vendors) with whom it is shared. Under CCPA/CPRA this is a baseline
  requirement.

  - **Consumer opt-out** if the sharing constitutes a "sale" or "share" (see §1).
  If it does, consumers must get a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information"
  mechanism.

  - **Merchant opt-in** remains useful as a contractual authorization and as a control
  on which merchants'' data enters the consortium — but it is *in addition to*, not
  *instead of*, consumer-facing notice.

  **Assumption:** The "general explanation" in the dashboard is a merchant-facing
  description, not consumer notice. **Missing:** whether consumers receive any notice
  at all (currently undecided).

  ## 3. De-identification standards

  **Pseudonymization is not de-identification.** The request says consumer data would
  be "pseudonymized where possible." Pseudonymization (replacing identifiers with
  tokens) is *not* the same as de-identification under U.S. law, and it does **not**
  remove the data from privacy-law coverage if the data can be re-linked.

  **The hard fields.** Device identifiers, IP addresses, and payment tokens are the
  problem:

  - **IP addresses** are generally treated as personal information and are often re-identifiable.

  - **Device identifiers** (e.g., IDFA, device fingerprints) are frequently personal
  information and can be re-linked.

  - **Payment tokens** are sensitive and, if re-linkable to the underlying account,
  carry high risk.

  **What is achievable:** True de-identification (removing or irreversibly transforming
  identifiers so the data cannot reasonably be re-identified) is achievable for *some*
  fields but not for the fields that make the consortium valuable — a fraud consortium
  needs the ability to link a device or IP across merchants, which is precisely what
  makes the data re-identifiable. **This is a genuine tension:** the more useful the
  shared signal is for cross-merchant fraud detection, the harder it is to de-identify.

  **Recommendation direction:** Do not promise "pseudonymized where possible" as a
  compliance shield. Instead, apply data minimization (share only the fields needed),
  use tokenization where feasible, and treat the data as personal information subject
  to the full notice/rights regime rather than relying on de-identification to avoid
  it.

  **Assumption:** No specific de-identification standard (e.g., HIPAA Safe Harbor,
  FTC guidance) has been selected. **Missing:** the actual fields to be shared and
  whether any are truly de-identifiable.

  ## 7. Consumer rights

  **Currently undecided whether consumers get notice or choice.** If the sharing is

  - **Opt out** of sale/share.

  - **Delete** their personal information.

  - **Correct** inaccurate information.

  ## 8. Whether the merchant opt-in design is sufficient

  **No — not as described.** The merchant opt-in:

  - Is a **necessary** contractual authorization from the merchant (the party that
  controls the data).

  - Is **not sufficient** to satisfy consumer-facing obligations, because consumers
  are the data subjects.

  **What would make it sufficient:**

  1. **Consumer notice** at collection (and opt-out if the sharing is a sale/share).

  2. **Merchant opt-in** retained as a contractual control.

  3. **Vendor contractual constraints** (no combining/training, retention limits,
  deletion).

  4. **A defensible de-identification/minimization approach** that does not over-rely
  on "pseudonymization."

  5. **A consumer rights mechanism** (access/deletion) across the consortium.

  **Special flag — identity-verification results.** If the shared data includes identity-verification

  CUBI, Washington) may apply, with written-consent, retention, and private-right-of-action
  exposure. This is a material risk that the current design does not address. (This
  mirrors the analysis in the related identity-verification matter.)
