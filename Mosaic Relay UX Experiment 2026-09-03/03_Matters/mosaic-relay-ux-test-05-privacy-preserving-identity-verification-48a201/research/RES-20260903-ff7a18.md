# Which U.S. state biometric privacy statutes (e.g., Illinois BIPA, Texas

## Question

Which U.S. state biometric privacy statutes (e.g., Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, Washington) apply to facial-comparison identity verification, and what notice, consent, retention, and private-right-of-action duties do they impose?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay plans to replace its identity-verification flow with a vendor that extracts data from ID documents and performs facial comparison using uploaded selfies. The system will display only pass/fail results to Mosaic Relay and its customers, while the vendor retains images and Mosaic Relay retains results and audit metadata. Product wants to begin implementation next month, but critical details—including where data will be processed, retention periods, and whether the vendor may train models on biometric data—remain unsettled. Counsel is involved because the data includes biometric information, government identifiers, and precise addresses, triggering state biometric privacy statutes with strict notice, consent, and retention requirements.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay proceed with implementing the facial-comparison identity verification vendor next month given that Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, and Washington's biometric law impose written consent, retention limits, and private rights of action, and what specific notices, consents, and contractual restrictions must be in place before launch to avoid statutory damages and private lawsuits?
## Open questions
- Where will the vendor process the biometric data, and does that trigger GDPR or other cross-border transfer obligations?
- Will the vendor train machine-learning models on the biometric data (ID images and selfies), and if so, can that be contractually prohibited?
- What are the proposed retention periods and deletion workflows for vendor-held images and Mosaic Relay's limited data record?
- How will minors and failed matches be handled in the flow, and what alternative verification methods will be offered?
- May customers reuse the pass/fail result for their own compliance programs, and does that create a "sale" or "share" of biometric information under state law?

## Likely rules and issues
### State biometric privacy statutes
**Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA)** is the most stringent and highest-risk statute. It applies to any private entity that collects, captures, purchases, or otherwise obtains biometric identifiers or biometric information, including "scans of face geometry." Facial comparison technology that extracts facial templates from selfies almost certainly produces biometric identifiers subject to BIPA.
BIPA requires:
- **Written informed consent** before collecting biometric data, disclosing the specific purpose and length of time for which it is collected, stored, and used
- **Publicly available retention schedule** and guidelines for permanently destroying biometric data when the initial purpose is satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction, whichever occurs first
- **Prohibition on selling, leasing, trading, or otherwise profiting** from biometric data
- **Private right of action** with statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, plus attorneys' fees
BIPA has generated massive class-action litigation. Courts have held that each scan or collection can constitute a separate violation, leading to potential damages in the hundreds of millions of dollars. The Illinois Supreme Court ruled in *Cothron v. White Castle* (2023) that claims accrue with each scan, though the legislature subsequently amended BIPA in 2024 to limit recovery to a single violation per person for the same method of collection.
**Texas Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI)** applies to biometric identifiers including "a record of hand or face geometry." It requires:
- **Informed consent** before capturing a biometric identifier for a commercial purpose
- **Prohibition on selling, leasing, or otherwise disclosing** biometric identifiers except in limited circumstances (e.g., to complete a financial transaction the individual requested, or as required by law)
- **Destruction within a reasonable time** after the purpose for collection has expired, but no later than one year after the individual's last interaction with the entity
- **Civil penalties** up to $25,000 per violation, enforceable only by the Texas Attorney General (no private right of action)
**Washington's biometric privacy law** (RCW 19.375) applies to biometric identifiers including "facial geometry." It requires:
- **Notice and consent** before enrolling a biometric identifier in a database for a commercial purpose
- **Prohibition on selling, leasing, or otherwise disclosing** biometric identifiers without consent, except in limited circumstances
- **Retention limits**: biometric identifiers must be destroyed when the purpose for collection has been satisfied or within one year of the individual's last interaction, whichever is earlier
- **Private right of action** for violations, with statutory damages of $1,000 per violation or actual damages, whichever is greater
**Other states** with biometric or facial recognition laws include California (CCPA/CPRA treats biometric information as sensitive personal information subject to opt-out and deletion rights), New York City (biometric identifier law for commercial establishments), and several states with pending legislation. The CCPA/CPRA defines biometric information broadly and grants consumers the right to know, delete, and opt out of the sale or sharing of their personal information, including biometric data.
### Privacy roles and controller/processor allocation
Under CCPA/CPRA, the party that determines the purposes and means of processing is the "business" (controller), and the party that processes on behalf of the business is the "service provider" (processor). The request states it is unsettled whether the customer or Mosaic Relay will determine the purpose of processing. This is a critical fork:
- If **Mosaic Relay** determines the purpose (e.g., to verify identity for its own KYC/AML compliance), Mosaic Relay is the business/controller and the vendor is a service provider/processor. Mosaic Relay must provide notice, obtain consent, and ensure the vendor contract includes CCPA-compliant service-provider terms.
- If the **customer** determines the purpose (e.g., the customer uses Mosaic Relay's tool to verify its own users for the customer's compliance program), the customer is the business/controller and Mosaic Relay may be a service provider. This shifts notice and consent obligations to the customer, but Mosaic Relay must still ensure its vendor contract and data flows comply.
The vendor collects biometric data directly from the individual, which complicates the analysis. Under GDPR (if applicable), the vendor may be a joint controller if it determines its own purposes (e.g., model training). Under CCPA, the vendor is likely a service provider if it processes solely on Mosaic Relay's instructions, but any independent use (e.g., model training) could make it a third party, triggering sale/share opt-out rights.
### Notice and consent requirements
**BIPA** requires written informed consent before collecting biometric data. The consent must disclose the specific purpose and length of time for collection, storage, and use. A generic privacy policy is insufficient; BIPA requires a specific, informed, written release.
**CUBI** requires informed consent before capturing a biometric identifier for a commercial purpose. The statute does not specify written consent, but best practice is to obtain written or electronic consent.
**Washington** requires notice and consent before enrolling a biometric identifier in a database. The notice must disclose the purpose and retention period.
**CCPA/CPRA** requires a privacy notice at or before the point of collection, disclosing the categories of personal information collected (including biometric information), the purposes for collection, and the categories of third parties with whom it is shared. Biometric information is "sensitive personal information" under CPRA, subject to the right to limit use and disclosure.
**GDPR** (if applicable) requires a lawful basis for processing biometric data, which is "special category" data under Article 9. Explicit consent is the most likely lawful basis, requiring a clear, specific, informed, and unambiguous affirmative act. GDPR also requires a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) for large-scale biometric processing.
### Data minimization and retention
**BIPA** requires a publicly available retention schedule and destruction of biometric data when the initial purpose is satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction, whichever occurs first.
**CUBI** requires destruction within a reasonable time after the purpose has expired, but no later than one year after the individual's last interaction.
**Washington** requires destruction when the purpose is satisfied or within one year of the individual's last interaction, whichever is earlier.
**CCPA/CPRA** requires that personal information be retained only as long as reasonably necessary for the disclosed purpose.
**GDPR** requires data minimization and storage limitation: personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary, and kept no longer than necessary.
The request states the vendor would retain images "for a proposed period" and Mosaic Relay would retain the result, audit metadata, and a limited data record. Retention periods and deletion workflows are unsettled and flagged as a launch blocker. The shortest defensible retention period is the time needed to complete the verification and resolve any disputes or audits, after which images should be deleted. Mosaic Relay should retain only the pass/fail result and minimal audit metadata (timestamp, transaction ID, vendor result code), not the underlying images or biometric templates.
### Vendor restrictions and model training
The request states it is unsettled whether the vendor may train models on the biometric data. This is a critical issue:
- **BIPA** prohibits selling, leasing, trading, or otherwise profiting from biometric data. Using biometric data to train models that are then sold or licensed to other customers could constitute "profiting from" the data and violate BIPA.
- **CUBI** prohibits selling, leasing, or otherwise disclosing biometric identifiers except in limited circumstances. Model training that involves disclosing biometric data to third parties or using it for purposes beyond the original collection could violate CUBI.
- **Washington** prohibits selling, leasing, or otherwise disclosing biometric identifiers without consent.
- **CCPA/CPRA** grants consumers the right to opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information. Using biometric data to train models that benefit other customers could constitute a "sale" or "share" under CPRA's broad definition.
- **GDPR** requires a separate lawful basis for any processing beyond the original purpose. Model training is a new purpose that requires its own lawful basis, likely explicit consent.
Best practice is to contractually prohibit the vendor from using biometric data for any purpose other than providing the verification service to Mosaic Relay, including a prohibition on model training, and to require the vendor to delete or de-identify data after the retention period.
### Pass/fail interface and sale/share issues
The proposed UX displays only a pass/fail result to Mosaic Relay and the customer. This is a data-minimization best practice, but it does not eliminate legal risk:
- If the customer reuses the pass/fail result for its own compliance program, that could constitute a "sale" or "share" of personal information under CCPA/CPRA, triggering opt-out rights.
- If the vendor retains images and uses them for model training or other purposes, that could constitute a "sale" or "share" under CCPA/CPRA and a violation of BIPA, CUBI, and Washington law.
- The pass/fail result itself is personal information (and potentially biometric information if it is derived from biometric data), so it is subject to CCPA/CPRA notice, access, deletion, and opt-out rights.
### Minors and failed matches
The request states that handling of minors and failed matches is unsettled. Key issues:
- **Minors**: BIPA, CUBI, and Washington law do not explicitly address minors, but collecting biometric data from minors without parental consent could violate COPPA (if the individual is under 13) or state consumer protection laws. Best practice is to prohibit minors from using the verification flow or to obtain verifiable parental consent.
- **Failed matches**: If the vendor returns a "fail" result, Mosaic Relay must decide whether to offer an alternative verification method (e.g., manual review, knowledge-based authentication) or deny service. Denying service based solely on a failed biometric match could raise discrimination or accessibility concerns, particularly if the facial comparison technology has higher error rates for certain demographic groups. Best practice is to offer an alternative verification method and to document the reasons for any adverse decision.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Proceed with launch after implementing BIPA-compliant consent and retention controls
Mosaic Relay can proceed with the vendor if it implements the following controls before launch:
1. **Written informed consent**: Obtain written (electronic) consent from each individual before collecting biometric data, disclosing the specific purpose, retention period, and the fact that the data will be shared with the vendor. The consent must be separate from the general privacy policy and terms of service.
2. **Publicly available retention schedule**: Publish a retention schedule stating that biometric data will be destroyed when the verification purpose is satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction (BIPA), one year (CUBI and Washington), or the shortest period required by applicable law.
3. **Vendor contract**: Include contractual terms that:
- Prohibit the vendor from selling, leasing, trading, or otherwise profiting from biometric data
- Prohibit the vendor from using biometric data for model training or any purpose other than providing the verification service
- Require the vendor to delete biometric data at the end of the retention period and to provide certification of deletion
- Require the vendor to implement reasonable security measures and to notify Mosaic Relay of any data breach
- Include CCPA-compliant service-provider terms (if Mosaic Relay is the business/controller)
- Include GDPR-compliant data processing terms (if GDPR applies)
4. **Data minimization**: Retain only the pass/fail result and minimal audit metadata, not the underlying images or biometric templates. Require the vendor to delete images after the retention period.
5. **Alternative verification method**: Offer an alternative verification method for individuals who fail the biometric match or who decline to consent to biometric collection.
6. **Minors**: Prohibit minors from using the verification flow or obtain verifiable parental consent.
This path allows Mosaic Relay to launch on schedule, but it requires significant legal and engineering work to implement the consent flow, retention schedule, vendor contract, and alternative verification method.
### Path 2: Delay launch until retention, deletion, and vendor terms are settled
Mosaic Relay can delay launch until the unsettled issues are resolved:
- Where the vendor will process the data (to determine whether GDPR or other cross-border transfer obligations apply)
- Whether the customer or Mosaic Relay will determine the purpose of processing (to allocate controller/processor roles)
- Retention periods and deletion workflows (to comply with BIPA, CUBI, Washington, and CCPA/CPRA)
- Whether the vendor may train models on biometric data (to avoid BIPA, CUBI, Washington, and CCPA/CPRA violations)
- How minors and failed matches will be handled (to avoid discrimination and accessibility issues)
This path reduces legal risk but delays the product launch and may frustrate the business team.
### Path 3: Use a non-biometric verification method
Mosaic Relay can avoid BIPA, CUBI, and Washington law entirely by using a non-biometric verification method, such as:
- Knowledge-based authentication (KBA)
- Document verification without facial comparison (e.g., verifying the authenticity of the ID document without comparing the photo to a selfie)
- Third-party database verification (e.g., verifying name, address, and SSN against credit bureau or government databases)
This path eliminates biometric privacy risk but may be less secure or less user-friendly than facial comparison.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Where the vendor processes the data**: If the vendor processes data outside the U.S., GDPR or other foreign privacy laws may apply, requiring additional safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses, adequacy determinations) and potentially prohibiting the transfer of biometric data altogether.
- **Whether the customer or Mosaic Relay determines the purpose of processing**: If the customer determines the purpose, the customer is the business/controller and bears primary responsibility for notice and consent. Mosaic Relay may be a service provider, but it must still ensure its vendor contract and data flows comply.
- **Whether the vendor trains models on biometric data**: If the vendor trains models on biometric data, that could constitute a "sale" or "share" under CCPA/CPRA and a violation of BIPA, CUBI, and Washington law. Mosaic Relay should contractually prohibit model training or obtain separate explicit consent.
- **Retention periods and deletion workflows**: If the vendor retains images for longer than necessary or fails to delete them, that could violate BIPA, CUBI, Washington, and CCPA/CPRA. Mosaic Relay should require the shortest defensible retention period and a documented deletion workflow.
- **Whether customers may reuse the pass/fail result**: If customers reuse the result for their own compliance programs, that could constitute a "sale" or "share" under CCPA/CPRA, triggering opt-out rights and requiring additional notice and consent.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, Mosaic Relay should:
1. **Confirm the vendor's data processing location** and determine whether GDPR or other foreign privacy laws apply. If GDPR applies, conduct a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) and implement standard contractual clauses or other transfer safeguards.
2. **Allocate controller/processor roles** by determining whether the customer or Mosaic Relay determines the purpose of processing. Document the allocation in the customer contract and the vendor contract.
3. **Implement a BIPA-compliant consent flow** that obtains written informed consent before collecting biometric data, disclosing the specific purpose, retention period, and the fact that the data will be shared with the vendor. The consent must be separate from the general privacy policy and terms of service.
4. **Publish a retention schedule** stating that biometric data will be destroyed when the verification purpose is satisfied or within the shortest period required by applicable law (one year under CUBI and Washington, three years under BIPA).
5. **Negotiate vendor contract terms** that prohibit the vendor from selling, leasing, trading, or otherwise profiting from biometric data; prohibit model training; require deletion at the end of the retention period; require certification of deletion; require reasonable security measures; and include CCPA-compliant service-provider terms and GDPR-compliant data processing terms (if applicable).
6. **Implement data minimization** by retaining only the pass/fail result and minimal audit metadata, not the underlying images or biometric templates.
7. **Offer an alternative verification method** for individuals who fail the biometric match or who decline to consent to biometric collection.
8. **Prohibit minors** from using the verification flow or obtain verifiable parental consent.
9. **Document the legal analysis** and the controls implemented to comply with BIPA, CUBI, Washington, CCPA/CPRA, and GDPR (if applicable). This documentation will be critical if Mosaic Relay is ever sued or investigated.
10. **Monitor litigation and regulatory developments** in Illinois, Texas, Washington, and other states with biometric privacy laws. BIPA in particular has generated massive class-action litigation, and the legal landscape is evolving rapidly.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption**: This analysis assumes the facial comparison technology produces a "scan of face geometry" or "facial template" subject to BIPA, CUBI, and Washington law. If the technology does not extract or store facial geometry (e.g., if it performs a one-time comparison without creating a template), the analysis could change. However, most facial comparison technologies create a template, so this assumption is likely correct.
- **Open fork**: If the vendor processes data outside the U.S., GDPR or other foreign privacy laws may apply, requiring additional safeguards and potentially prohibiting the transfer of biometric data. If the vendor processes data only in the U.S., GDPR does not apply, but state biometric laws still do.
- **Open fork**: If the customer determines the purpose of processing, the customer is the business/controller and bears primary responsibility for notice and consent. If Mosaic Relay determines the purpose, Mosaic Relay is the business/controller and bears that responsibility. This allocation affects who must provide notice, obtain consent, and respond to consumer requests.
- **Not examined**: This analysis does not examine the specific terms of the vendor contract, the vendor's data security practices, or the vendor's compliance certifications (e.g., SOC 2, ISO 27001). These should be reviewed before launch.
- **Not examined**: This analysis does not examine whether the facial comparison technology has higher error rates for certain demographic groups, which could raise discrimination or accessibility concerns. Mosaic Relay should request bias testing results from the vendor and offer an alternative verification method.
- **Not examined**: This analysis does not examine whether the pass/fail result constitutes a "consumer report" under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA). If Mosaic Relay uses the result to make eligibility decisions (e.g., whether to approve a merchant account), FCRA may apply, requiring additional notice, consent, and adverse action procedures.
