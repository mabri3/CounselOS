# What cross-border data-transfer obligations apply when a U.S. verification vendo

## Question

What cross-border data-transfer obligations apply when a U.S. verification vendor processes biometric and ID-document data, including any GDPR or other transfer requirements?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a U.S. payments infrastructure company, plans to replace its identity-verification flow with a vendor that extracts data from government ID documents and performs facial comparison via live selfie. The individuals affected are merchant applicants, beneficial owners, and payout recipients. Product wants to begin implementation next month, but the vendor's processing location is undecided, which determines whether GDPR or other cross-border transfer obligations apply. Counsel is involved because the data includes biometric information, government identifiers, and precise addresses — categories that trigger heightened transfer and processing requirements in multiple jurisdictions.
## Decision question
Before implementation begins next month, can Mosaic Relay lawfully structure its identity-verification vendor arrangement so that biometric and ID-document data collected from individuals — potentially including EU/UK data subjects — is transferred to and processed by a U.S.-based (or other non-EEA) vendor, and what transfer mechanisms, contractual safeguards, and notices must be in place before launch?
## Open questions
- Where will the vendor actually process and store the data (which countries, which cloud regions)? This is the single most consequential unresolved fact — it determines whether GDPR Chapter V, UK GDPR, or other transfer regimes apply at all.
- Will Mosaic Relay or its platform customers onboard individuals who are located in the EEA, UK, or Switzerland? If the customer base is exclusively U.S.-domestic, GDPR transfer obligations likely do not attach, but this has not been confirmed.
- Does the vendor hold any current transfer mechanism certifications (e.g., EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework certification, standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules)?
- Will the vendor use sub-processors or cloud-storage providers in additional jurisdictions beyond its own processing location?
- Are any of the individuals whose data is collected located in jurisdictions with their own cross-border transfer restrictions (e.g., Brazil LGPD, Canada PIPEDA/Quebec Law 25, or U.S. state laws with data-transfer provisions)?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
The analysis below is model-generated based on established legal frameworks. It is not drawn from retrieved sources and should be verified against current authority before reliance.
### 1. GDPR Chapter V — Transfers to third countries
If any individual whose data is collected is located in the EEA (or if Mosaic Relay or its customers are established in the EEA and the vendor is not), the transfer of biometric and ID-document data to a U.S.-based vendor constitutes a transfer to a third country under GDPR Chapter V (Articles 44–49). Key obligations:
- **Adequacy or appropriate safeguard required.** The transfer is lawful only if the European Commission has issued an adequacy decision for the destination (the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework provides one for certified U.S. companies), or if appropriate safeguards are in place — most commonly Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) adopted by the Commission in June 2021, or Binding Corporate Rules for intra-group transfers.
- **Transfer impact assessment (TIA).** Post-*Schrems II* (CJEU, Case C-311/18), the data exporter must assess whether the law of the destination country (here, the U.S.) ensures an essentially equivalent level of protection, particularly regarding government access to data. For biometric data — a special category under Article 9 — this assessment carries heightened weight.
- **Article 9 conditions.** Biometric data used for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person is special-category data. Processing requires both a lawful basis under Article 6 and a separate condition under Article 9(2) — most likely explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)) or substantial public interest (Art. 9(2)(g)) if a member-state law provides one. The transfer mechanism does not substitute for the Article 9 condition; both must be satisfied.
- **Sub-processor chains.** If the vendor uses cloud-storage providers or other sub-processors in additional third countries, each onward transfer must also satisfy Chapter V. The SCCs must flow down to sub-processors.
### 2. UK GDPR
If individuals are located in the UK, the UK GDPR imposes parallel requirements. The UK has its own adequacy regulations, its own version of SCCs (the International Data Transfer Agreement, or IDTA), and recognizes the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework through the UK Extension. The analysis mirrors the EU GDPR but requires separate documentation.
### 3. U.S. state biometric statutes — cross-border dimension
U.S. state biometric laws (Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, Washington MHMDA) do not by their terms restrict cross-border transfers, but they impose consent, notice, retention, and destruction obligations that apply regardless of where the data is processed. If the vendor processes data outside the U.S., these statutes still apply to the collection from U.S. residents. The practical cross-border issue under U.S. law is not a transfer restriction but rather ensuring that the vendor's offshore processing does not compromise compliance with destruction timelines, security standards, or the ability to honor deletion requests.
### 4. Sectoral and contractual overlay
- **Card network and processor rules.** Mosaic Relay's acquiring bank and card network agreements may impose data-localization or cross-border transfer restrictions on identity-verification data as part of their KYC/KYB requirements. These are contractual, not statutory, but they are binding and often overlooked.
- **GLBA.** If the identity verification is conducted in connection with a financial service, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act's Safeguards Rule requires that service providers (including the vendor) maintain appropriate safeguards, and the FTC's amended Safeguards Rule (effective June 2023) imposes specific requirements on financial institutions to oversee service providers, including those that process data offshore.
### 5. Data minimization and purpose limitation in the transfer context
GDPR Article 5(1)(c) (data minimization) and Article 5(1)(b) (purpose limitation) apply to the transfer decision itself. Transferring full ID-document images and biometric templates to a third country when a pass/fail result would suffice raises proportionality questions. The proposed architecture — where Mosaic Relay retains only the result and audit metadata — is favorable, but only if the vendor's retention of images is itself justified, time-limited, and disclosed.

## Viable paths
**Path A — Vendor processes exclusively in the U.S.; no EEA/UK data subjects.**
If Mosaic Relay's customer base is U.S.-domestic and the vendor processes and stores all data in the U.S., GDPR Chapter V does not apply. The cross-border analysis simplifies to U.S. state biometric law compliance and contractual safeguards. This is the lowest-friction path but requires confirming that no EEA/UK individuals will be onboarded — a business decision, not a legal one.
**Path B — Vendor processes in the U.S.; EEA/UK data subjects possible.**
Mosaic Relay (or its customer, depending on the controller/processor allocation) must execute SCCs (EU) and/or the IDTA (UK) with the vendor, conduct a transfer impact assessment, confirm the vendor's DPF certification status (if relying on adequacy), and ensure Article 9 conditions are met for biometric data. Sub-processor chains must be mapped and covered. This path is viable but adds 4–8 weeks of contractual and assessment work that must complete before launch.
**Path C — Vendor processes in the EEA/UK or an adequacy jurisdiction.**
If the vendor can process and store data within the EEA, UK, or a jurisdiction with an adequacy decision, Chapter V transfer obligations are avoided for those data subjects. This may require the vendor to offer regional processing, which not all vendors support. It simplifies the transfer analysis but does not eliminate Article 9 or state biometric law obligations.
**Path D — Hybrid: regional processing for EEA/UK data subjects, U.S. processing for U.S. data subjects.**
Some verification vendors offer data-residency options. This is operationally more complex but legally the cleanest for a global customer base.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Vendor processing location confirmed as U.S.-only with no EEA/UK data subjects.** This would collapse the GDPR analysis and leave only U.S. state law and contractual issues.
- **Vendor processing location confirmed as including non-adequacy third countries (e.g., India, Philippines).** This would require SCCs plus a TIA for each destination, and would heighten the *Schrems II* analysis.
- **Customer base confirmed to include EEA/UK-established platforms.** This would make GDPR applicable regardless of where individuals are located, because the controller would be subject to GDPR under Article 3(1).
- **Vendor's DPF certification status.** If the vendor is DPF-certified, the adequacy decision simplifies the transfer mechanism for EU-to-U.S. transfers. If not, SCCs are required.
- **Whether the vendor uses sub-processors in additional jurisdictions.** Each onward transfer requires its own safeguard.

## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Confirm the vendor's processing and storage locations, including all sub-processors and cloud regions.** Request a data-flow map from the vendor.
2. **Confirm whether any current or reasonably anticipated customers or their end-users are located in the EEA, UK, or Switzerland.** This is a business fact that Product and Sales can answer.
3. **Check the vendor's DPF certification status** on the U.S. Department of Commerce's Data Privacy Framework website.
4. **Review the vendor's standard DPA** for SCC incorporation, sub-processor disclosure, deletion commitments, and model-training restrictions.
5. **Verify current SCC module selection** (Module 2 — controller to processor — is most likely, but the controller/processor allocation between Mosaic Relay and its customers affects this).
6. **Check acquiring bank and card network agreements** for data-localization or cross-border transfer restrictions on KYC/KYB data.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — U.S.-only customer base.** The analysis assumes Mosaic Relay's current customer base is primarily U.S.-domestic, per the company profile. If any platform customer onboards EEA/UK individuals, the full GDPR Chapter V analysis in Path B becomes mandatory, and the launch timeline is at risk unless SCCs and a TIA are completed first.
- **Open fork — Controller/processor allocation between Mosaic Relay and its customers.** If Mosaic Relay is the controller (it determines the purpose of verification), it bears direct Chapter V obligations and must execute SCCs with the vendor. If the customer is the controller and Mosaic Relay is a processor, the customer bears the transfer obligation but Mosaic Relay must flow down SCCs to the vendor as a sub-processor. The contractual structure and the answer to "who determines the purpose" — currently unsettled — selects between these paths.
- **Open fork — Vendor DPF certification.** If the vendor is DPF-certified, the EU-to-U.S. transfer can rely on the adequacy decision without SCCs (though a DPA is still needed). If not certified, SCCs are the fallback. This is a binary fact that materially changes the contractual work required.
- **Not examined — Vendor's actual DPA, security certifications, and sub-processor list.** These documents were not provided and were not in the vault. They are necessary to confirm whether the vendor's standard terms already address SCCs, deletion, and model-training restrictions, or whether negotiation is required.
- **Not examined — Acquiring bank and card network agreements.** The vault does not contain these agreements. They may impose data-localization or transfer restrictions that operate independently of privacy law and could override the preferred path.
- **Not examined — Non-EU/UK jurisdictions with transfer restrictions.** Brazil (LGPD), Canada (Quebec Law 25), and other jurisdictions impose their own cross-border transfer requirements. If any data subjects are located in these jurisdictions, additional analysis is needed. The vault was not searched for jurisdiction-specific playbooks because the processing location and data-subject geography are both undecided.
