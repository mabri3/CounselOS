# Under CCPA/CPRA, when a vendor collects biometric data directly from

## Question

Under CCPA/CPRA, when a vendor collects biometric data directly from an individual on behalf of a business, who is the 'business' vs 'service provider', and does sharing a pass/fail result constitute a 'sale' or 'share' of biometric or sensitive personal information?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payments technology company, plans to replace its identity-verification flow with a vendor that extracts data from ID documents and performs facial comparison. The proposed UX collects an ID document and live selfie from merchant applicants, beneficial owners, and payout recipients, then displays only a pass/fail result to Mosaic Relay and its customer. Counsel is involved because the data includes biometric information, government identifiers, and precise addresses, and Product wants to begin implementation next month. Critical privacy roles, retention periods, vendor model training, and cross-border processing details remain unsettled.
## Decision question
Under CCPA/CPRA, when a vendor collects biometric data directly from an individual on behalf of Mosaic Relay, who is the "business" versus "service provider," and does sharing a pass/fail result with Mosaic Relay's customers constitute a "sale" or "share" of biometric or sensitive personal information requiring opt-out rights or consent?
## Open questions
- Where will the vendor process the data (which jurisdiction/location)?
- Who determines the purpose and means of processing the identity-verification data — Mosaic Relay or the customer?
- May customers reuse the pass/fail result for their own compliance programs?
- What are the proposed retention periods and deletion workflows for the verification images and the limited data record?
- May the verification vendor train models on the biometric data (the ID-document images and selfies)?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of CCPA/CPRA and biometric privacy frameworks.
### Privacy-role allocation under CCPA/CPRA
Under CCPA/CPRA, the "business" is the entity that determines the purposes and means of processing personal information. A "service provider" processes personal information on behalf of a business pursuant to a written contract that prohibits the service provider from retaining, using, or disclosing the personal information for any purpose other than performing the services specified.
In this matter, the critical unsettled fact is whether Mosaic Relay or its customer determines the purpose of processing. If Mosaic Relay determines the purpose (e.g., to verify identity for its own compliance or fraud-prevention purposes), Mosaic Relay is likely the business and the vendor is a service provider. If the customer determines the purpose (e.g., the customer uses Mosaic Relay's platform to verify its own users for the customer's compliance program), the customer may be the business, Mosaic Relay may be a service provider to the customer, and the verification vendor may be a sub-processor.
The fact that the vendor collects biometric data directly from the individual does not automatically make the vendor a business. The vendor's role depends on the contractual allocation of control and purpose. However, if the vendor uses the data for its own purposes (e.g., model training), the vendor may be acting as a business or third party, which would trigger additional obligations.
### Biometric privacy requirements
The facial comparison likely produces a biometric identifier or faceprint subject to state biometric statutes. Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, and Washington's biometric privacy law impose notice, consent, retention, and destruction requirements. Illinois BIPA is particularly significant because it provides a private right of action with statutory damages of $1,000–$5,000 per violation.
Under CCPA/CPRA, biometric information is "sensitive personal information" subject to additional protections, including the right to limit use and disclosure. If the vendor collects biometric data directly from the individual, the business (whether Mosaic Relay or its customer) must provide notice at collection and obtain consent where required.
### Sale or share of biometric or sensitive personal information
Under CCPA/CPRA, a "sale" is the exchange of personal information for monetary or other valuable consideration. A "share" is the disclosure of personal information to a third party for cross-context behavioral advertising, whether or not for monetary consideration.
The pass/fail result itself is unlikely to constitute biometric information or sensitive personal information if it is truly a binary outcome with no underlying biometric data or identifiers. However, if the result is linked to an identifiable individual and disclosed to the customer for the customer's own compliance purposes, the disclosure could be a "sale" if Mosaic Relay receives valuable consideration (e.g., as part of a paid service) or a "share" if used for cross-context behavioral advertising.
If customers may reuse the result for their own compliance programs, this suggests the customer is receiving personal information for its own purposes, which could make the customer a third party rather than a service provider. This would require Mosaic Relay to provide notice and potentially offer opt-out rights if the disclosure constitutes a sale or share.
### Required notices and consent
The business must provide a notice at collection that describes the categories of personal information collected (including biometric information, government identifiers, and precise address), the purposes for collection, and whether the information is sold or shared. If biometric information is collected, Illinois BIPA and similar statutes require informed written consent before collection.
If the vendor collects the data directly from the individual, the notice and consent must be provided at the point of collection. The business must ensure the vendor's collection practices comply with these requirements.
### Cross-border processing
If the vendor processes data outside the United States, GDPR or other international transfer obligations may apply. The unsettled fact of where the vendor will process the data is material. If the vendor processes data in the EU or UK, Standard Contractual Clauses or other transfer mechanisms may be required.
### Data minimization and retention
CCPA/CPRA requires that personal information be collected only for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and retained only as long as necessary. The proposed retention of images by the vendor and a limited data record by Mosaic Relay must be proportionate to the verification purpose. Retention periods and deletion workflows are unsettled and flagged as a launch blocker.
Illinois BIPA requires a publicly available retention schedule and guidelines for permanently destroying biometric identifiers when the initial purpose for collecting them has been satisfied or within three years of the individual's last interaction, whichever occurs first.
### Vendor restrictions
The vendor contract must prohibit the vendor from retaining, using, or disclosing personal information for any purpose other than performing the services specified. If the vendor trains models on the biometric data, this is a separate purpose that would require explicit consent and may make the vendor a business or third party.
Sub-processor and cloud-storage controls must be specified in the contract, including audit rights, security requirements, and deletion obligations.
### Pass/fail interface
The pass/fail interface itself is not inherently problematic if it truly limits the data disclosed to a binary result. However, if the result is linked to an identifiable individual and used for purposes beyond the initial verification (e.g., customer compliance programs), it may trigger sale/share obligations or require additional consent.
### Minors and failed matches
Handling of minors and failed matches is unsettled. If minors may be subject to verification, additional consent requirements apply under COPPA and state law. Failed matches must be handled in a way that does not discriminate or create a disparate impact.

## Viable paths
**Path 1: Mosaic Relay as business, vendor as service provider**
Mosaic Relay determines the purpose of processing (identity verification for its own compliance and fraud prevention). The vendor is a service provider under a written contract that prohibits use of data for any other purpose, including model training. Mosaic Relay provides notice at collection and obtains consent. The pass/fail result is disclosed to the customer as a service provider or contractor, not a third party. This path minimizes sale/share risk but requires careful contractual drafting and may not align with the customer's intended use.
**Path 2: Customer as business, Mosaic Relay as service provider, vendor as sub-processor**
The customer determines the purpose of processing (identity verification for the customer's compliance program). Mosaic Relay is a service provider to the customer, and the vendor is a sub-processor. The customer provides notice and obtains consent. This path shifts compliance burden to the customer but requires the customer to have a direct relationship with the individual and may not work if Mosaic Relay has its own compliance obligations.
**Path 3: Joint business arrangement**
Mosaic Relay and the customer are joint businesses, each determining purposes for their own compliance programs. This requires a joint-controller agreement under GDPR and careful allocation of responsibilities under CCPA/CPRA. This path is complex but may reflect the reality that both parties use the verification result.
**Path 4: Delay launch until roles and retention are settled**
Given the unsettled facts and the launch-blocker status of retention and deletion workflows, Mosaic Relay could delay implementation until the privacy roles, retention periods, deletion workflows, and vendor model-training restrictions are resolved. This is the most cautious path and aligns with Mosaic Relay's risk posture.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Where the vendor processes the data**: If outside the U.S., GDPR or other international transfer obligations apply.
- **Who determines the purpose of processing**: This determines who is the business and who is the service provider, which shifts compliance obligations.
- **Whether customers may reuse the result**: If yes, the customer may be a third party, triggering sale/share obligations.
- **Retention periods and deletion workflows**: If the vendor retains images indefinitely or for model training, this creates significant legal risk.
- **Whether the vendor trains models on the data**: If yes, the vendor may be a business or third party, requiring explicit consent and additional contractual protections.
- **Whether minors are subject to verification**: If yes, COPPA and state law impose additional consent requirements.

## Recommended last-mile verification
1. **Confirm the vendor's data-processing location** and whether any data will be transferred outside the U.S.
2. **Draft a data-processing agreement** that clearly allocates privacy roles, prohibits vendor model training on biometric data, and specifies retention and deletion workflows.
3. **Prepare a notice at collection** that describes the categories of personal information, purposes, and whether the information is sold or shared.
4. **Obtain informed written consent** for biometric data collection where required by Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, or Washington law.
5. **Conduct a data-protection impact assessment** to evaluate the proportionality of data collection and retention.
6. **Review the pass/fail interface** to ensure it does not disclose underlying biometric data or identifiers and does not constitute a sale or share.
7. **Establish a process for handling minors and failed matches** that complies with COPPA and anti-discrimination law.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption**: The analysis assumes the pass/fail result is a binary outcome with no underlying biometric data or identifiers. If the result includes or can be linked to biometric data, the sale/share analysis changes materially.
- **Open fork**: If the customer determines the purpose of processing, the customer is the business and Mosaic Relay is a service provider, which shifts compliance obligations. If Mosaic Relay determines the purpose, Mosaic Relay is the business and bears the compliance burden.
- **Not examined**: The vendor's standard contract terms, data-processing location, and model-training practices were not reviewed. These documents are necessary to confirm the privacy-role allocation and vendor restrictions.
- **Working assumption**: The analysis assumes the vendor collects data directly from the individual with notice and consent. If the vendor's collection practices are not compliant, Mosaic Relay may be liable as the business.
- **Open fork**: If the vendor processes data outside the U.S., GDPR or other international transfer obligations apply, which would require Standard Contractual Clauses or other transfer mechanisms. If the vendor processes data only in the U.S., these obligations do not apply.
