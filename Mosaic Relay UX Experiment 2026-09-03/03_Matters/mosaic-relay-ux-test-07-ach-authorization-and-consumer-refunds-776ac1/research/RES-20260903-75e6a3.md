# How are ACH return and dispute handling requirements structured for

## Question

How are ACH return and dispute handling requirements structured for recurring consumer debits, including return codes (R07, R08, R10, R11, R29, R51), Written Statement of Unauthorized Debit (WSOD), return-rate thresholds, reinitiation limits, and allocation of responsibilities between the Originator, ODFI, and third-party service provider?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A Mosaic Relay merchant wants to accept recurring ACH debits from consumers for a monthly-renewing subscription service, with Mosaic Relay submitting debits through its payment partners and managing returns and refunds through its dashboard. Counsel is involved because recurring consumer ACH debits trigger NACHA Operating Rules and Regulation E obligations around authorization, return handling, dispute resolution, and evidence retention. The merchant wants to launch in nine weeks (target 2026-11-05), but the return-handling process, unauthorized-debit claim routing, and Mosaic Relay's NACHA role are not yet decided.
## Decision question
Before the 2026-11-05 launch, can Mosaic Relay structure its ACH return and dispute-handling process — covering return codes R07/R08/R10/R11/R29/R51, WSOD production, return-rate monitoring, reinitiation limits, and the allocation of responsibilities among the merchant (Originator), the ODFI, and Mosaic Relay as a third-party service provider — so that unauthorized-debit claims are resolved within NACHA and Reg E timelines without breaching ODFI return-rate thresholds?
## Open questions
- **Mosaic Relay's NACHA role** — Is Mosaic Relay a Third-Party Service Provider, a Third-Party Sender, or an ODFI agent? Each carries different registration, warranty, and risk-assessment obligations that change the return-handling allocation.
- **Unauthorized-debit claim routing** — Who responds to the consumer's financial institution when an R07 or R10 return arrives, and who bears the loss? Not yet decided.
- **System of record for authorization evidence** — If the dashboard is not the system of record, who produces proof of authorization to the RDFI within NACHA timeframes?
- **ODFI/processor agreement terms** — Return-rate thresholds, evidence-production SLAs, and prohibited practices in the ODFI agreement are often stricter than NACHA baseline and have not been reviewed.
- **Variable vs. fixed amounts** — Determines whether R11 (customer advises entry not in accordance with authorization) returns become a material risk category.

## Likely rules and issues
### Return codes relevant to recurring consumer debits
| Code | Meaning | Practical significance for this feature |
|------|---------|----------------------------------------|
| **R07** | Authorization revoked by customer | Consumer told their bank they revoked authorization. The Originator must stop debiting. Re-presentment is prohibited. If the merchant's cancellation path is slow or unclear, R07 volume rises. |
| **R08** | Payment stopped | Consumer placed a stop-payment order at their bank (Reg E § 1005.10(c) — 3 business days' notice). Re-presentment is prohibited. The dashboard must suppress future debits on R08. |
| **R10** | Customer advises not authorized | The highest-risk return code. Consumer claims they never authorized the debit. Requires a WSOD from the consumer. Can arrive up to 60 days from settlement (tracking the Reg E error-resolution window). Directly counted toward the unauthorized return-rate threshold. |
| **R11** | Customer advises entry not in accordance with authorization | Consumer authorized *something* but disputes the specific entry — wrong amount, wrong date, or debit after revocation. Used when the consumer does not claim the entire authorization is invalid. Requires a WSOD. Also counted toward unauthorized return-rate calculations. |
| **R29** | Corporate customer advises not authorized | Applies to business accounts (non-consumer). If any subscribers are small businesses, R29 replaces R10. The WSOD requirement and return window differ. |
| **R51** | Item is ineligible / RCK-related | Less likely for this use case but can arise if the debit is ineligible for the SEC code used. Relevant if the wrong SEC code is selected at origination. |
### Written Statement of Unauthorized Debit (WSOD)
- For R10 and R11 returns, the RDFI obtains a WSOD from the consumer before returning the entry.
- The Originator's defense to an R10/R11 return is **proof of authorization** — the retained authorization record. If the Originator cannot produce it, the return stands and the debit is charged back.
- NACHA requires the Originator to provide proof of authorization to the ODFI upon request. ODFIs commonly impose short production windows (5–10 business days) because the RDFI is waiting.
- **Practical consequence:** the authorization evidence format (what is captured at checkout, how it is stored, and how quickly it can be retrieved) is the single most important operational control for managing R10/R11 exposure.
### Return-rate thresholds
- NACHA imposes **return-rate monitoring** on Originators. The key thresholds (as commonly applied):
- **Unauthorized return rate (R07, R10, R11, R29, R51 combined):** 0.5% of debit entries. Exceeding this triggers NACHA scrutiny and potential enforcement.
- **Overall return rate:** 15% of debit entries. Exceeding this triggers review.
- **Administrative return rate (R01–R04, etc.):** 3% of debit entries.
- ODFIs enforce these thresholds contractually and often set **lower internal thresholds** (e.g., 0.3% unauthorized) as early-warning triggers. The ODFI agreement controls the practical ceiling.
- **Practical consequence:** the dashboard must track return rates by reason code in near-real time, not just display individual return statuses. A merchant with rising R10 volume needs to be flagged before the 0.5% threshold is breached.
### Reinitiation limits
- NACHA caps reinitiation of a returned debit: generally **no more than two reinitiations** following a return, and **no reinitiation at all** after R07, R08, or R10 returns.
- Reinitiation is only permitted if the original entry was returned for insufficient funds (R01) or a similar correctable reason, and the Originator has a reasonable basis to believe the account now has sufficient funds.
- **Practical consequence:** the dashboard's "returned" status must drive automated re-presentment logic that respects these caps. A manual or uncontrolled retry process will breach NACHA rules.
### Allocation of responsibilities
| Party | Role | Key return/dispute obligations |
|-------|------|-------------------------------|
| **Merchant (Originator)** | Originates the debit entry | Warrants authorization validity to the ODFI. Bears the loss on unauthorized returns. Must retain and produce authorization evidence. Must honor revocations and stop debits. Must monitor its own return rates. |
| **ODFI (Mosaic Relay's payment partner)** | Transmits entries into the ACH network | Enforces NACHA rules on Originators. Monitors return rates. Can request proof of authorization. Can terminate Originator access for excessive returns. Passes WSOD-based returns through to the Originator. |
| **Mosaic Relay (TPSP / Third-Party Sender)** | Submits entries on behalf of the merchant | If a **Third-Party Sender**: must register with NACHA, conduct risk assessments, and may warrant merchant conduct to the ODFI. If a **TPSP**: obligations are lighter but the ODFI agreement will impose contractual duties. Must ensure its systems support return processing, evidence production, and reinitiation controls. |
| **RDFI (consumer's bank)** | Receives the debit entry | Processes consumer WSODs. Initiates returns within NACHA timeframes. Handles Reg E error-resolution on its side. |

## Viable paths
### Path A — Mosaic Relay as Third-Party Service Provider (lightest NACHA footprint)
Mosaic Relay provides the technology platform but does not touch the ACH file directly; the merchant's ODFI relationship is direct or through a separate processor. Mosaic Relay's obligations are primarily contractual (from the ODFI/processor agreement) rather than direct NACHA obligations.
- **Pros:** No NACHA Third-Party Sender registration required. Lighter compliance overhead.
- **Cons:** Less control over return handling and evidence production. The merchant bears more operational burden. Mosaic Relay's dashboard may not have the data needed to manage return rates proactively.
- **Feasibility:** Depends on the existing ODFI/processor agreement structure.
### Path B — Mosaic Relay as Third-Party Sender (moderate NACHA footprint)
Mosaic Relay submits entries on behalf of the merchant through its ODFI relationship. This triggers NACHA Third-Party Sender registration, risk-assessment obligations, and potential warranty exposure for merchant conduct.
- **Pros:** Full control over return handling, evidence production, and reinitiation logic. Dashboard can be the system of record. Better data for return-rate monitoring.
- **Cons:** NACHA registration and annual risk assessment required. ODFI will impose stricter contractual terms. Mosaic Relay may bear warranty liability for merchant authorization failures.
- **Feasibility:** Most likely structure given the described architecture ("Mosaic Relay would submit debits through its payment partners").
### Path C — Hybrid: Mosaic Relay as TPSP with enhanced contractual controls
Mosaic Relay operates as a TPSP but contractually requires the merchant to meet specific authorization, evidence, and return-handling standards as a condition of using the platform. Mosaic Relay builds the controls into the product (automated reinitiation caps, return-rate dashboards, evidence export APIs) without taking on Third-Party Sender registration.
- **Pros:** Balances control and compliance overhead. Product-level controls reduce risk without formal NACHA registration.
- **Cons:** May not satisfy the ODFI if the ODFI views Mosaic Relay as functionally a Third-Party Sender regardless of contractual labels.
- **Feasibility:** Requires careful review of the ODFI agreement and NACHA's functional definition of Third-Party Sender.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Mosaic Relay's NACHA role** — If Mosaic Relay is a Third-Party Sender, the registration, risk-assessment, and warranty sections expand materially. If it is a pure TPSP, the direct NACHA obligations shrink but contractual obligations remain.
- **ODFI/processor agreement terms** — Return-rate thresholds, evidence-production SLAs, and prohibited practices in the ODFI agreement are often stricter than NACHA baseline. A 0.3% unauthorized threshold (vs. NACHA's 0.5%) would change the monitoring cadence.
- **Whether any subscribers are businesses** — If any debits hit business accounts, R29 replaces R10, Reg E drops out, and the WSOD and return-window analysis changes.
- **Variable vs. fixed amounts** — Variable amounts increase R11 exposure (consumer disputes the specific entry, not the entire authorization). Fixed amounts reduce R11 risk but do not eliminate it.
- **Whether the dashboard is the system of record** — If it is not, the evidence-production chain has an additional link that could fail within NACHA timeframes.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — Mosaic Relay's NACHA role.** This analysis assumes Mosaic Relay is a Third-Party Service Provider or Third-Party Sender, not the Originator of record. If Mosaic Relay is the Originator (its name on the ACH file), it bears direct warranty liability for every authorization and the return-rate monitoring obligation shifts entirely to Mosaic Relay. The ODFI agreement has not been read.
- **Open fork — Third-Party Sender vs. TPSP classification.** If Mosaic Relay is functionally a Third-Party Sender (it controls the ACH file format and submission), NACHA registration and annual risk assessments are mandatory regardless of what the contract calls it. If it is a pure TPSP, those obligations fall away. The answer depends on the technical architecture and the ODFI's characterization, neither of which has been confirmed.
- **Working assumption — consumer accounts only.** All analysis assumes Reg E applies. If any subscribers are small businesses using business accounts, R29 replaces R10, the WSOD requirement changes, and Reg E protections (including the 60-day error window) drop out for those debits.
- **Not examined — the ODFI/processor agreement.** This document likely contains return-rate thresholds, evidence-production SLAs, indemnification provisions, and prohibited practices that are stricter than NACHA baseline. It was not available in the vault and was not reviewed. It could materially change the return-handling allocation and the monitoring cadence.
- **Not examined — current NACHA Operating Rules edition.** The 2025/2026 edition may have updated return-rate thresholds, reinitiation limits, or Third-Party Sender requirements. All NACHA citations in this analysis are model-generated and have not been verified against the current edition.
- **Open fork — whether the dashboard will be the system of record.** If it is, Mosaic Relay must guarantee immutability, exportability, and a production SLA for authorization evidence. If it is not, the merchant bears that burden and Mosaic Relay's dashboard is a display layer only. This decision affects the architecture, the ODFI agreement, and the R10/R11 defense capability.
