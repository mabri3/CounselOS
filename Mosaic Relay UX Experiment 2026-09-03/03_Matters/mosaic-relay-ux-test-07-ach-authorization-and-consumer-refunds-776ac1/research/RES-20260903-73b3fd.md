# What are the NACHA return-code timeframes and evidence-production requirements f

## Question

What are the NACHA return-code timeframes and evidence-production requirements for unauthorized-debit claims (R10/R11/R29/R51)?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A Mosaic Relay merchant wants to accept recurring ACH debits from consumers for a subscription service, with Mosaic Relay submitting entries through its payment partners and managing refunds and return status in its dashboard. The launch target is 2026-11-05 (nine weeks from intake). Counsel is involved because consumer ACH debits trigger NACHA authorization, evidence-retention, and return-handling rules, plus Regulation E error-resolution rights, and the allocation of those duties between Mosaic Relay and the merchant is not yet fixed. If unauthorized-debit claims are mishandled, the merchant faces elevated return rates, ODFI scrutiny, and potential loss of ACH access.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay and the merchant launch recurring consumer ACH debits by 2026-11-05 with a checkout flow, evidence-retention architecture, and return-handling process that satisfy NACHA’s WEB-debit authorization, two-year record retention, and R10/R11/R29/R51 response timeframes, and who—Mosaic Relay or the merchant—will be contractually responsible for producing authorization proof to the RDFI within those windows?
## Open questions
- **Mosaic Relay’s NACHA role:** Is Mosaic Relay a Third-Party Service Provider, a Third-Party Sender, or the Originator of record under its ODFI agreement? This determines whether Mosaic Relay must register with NACHA, conduct risk assessments, and warrant merchant compliance.
- **System of record for authorization evidence:** Will the dashboard be the authoritative repository, and can it produce a complete, immutable authorization record (including IP/device data for WEB entries) to the ODFI within the short window the ODFI sets after an RDFI request?
- **Variable vs. fixed amounts:** Will the merchant use fixed recurring amounts, variable amounts, or both? Variable amounts trigger a 10-day pre-debit notice requirement (or a consumer range-election) under NACHA and Reg E § 1005.10(d).
- **Unauthorized-debit claim routing:** Who responds to the consumer’s financial institution when an R10, R11, or R29 return arrives, and who bears the loss if the authorization evidence is insufficient?
- **ODFI/processor agreement terms:** What evidence-production SLAs, return-rate thresholds, and prohibited practices does the ODFI impose beyond NACHA baseline?

### NACHA return-code timeframes and evidence-production requirements
| Return Code | Meaning | RDFI Return Deadline | Originator / Mosaic Relay Response |
|-------------|---------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **R10** | Customer Advises Not Authorized (consumer) | **60 calendar days** from settlement date (Reg E window) | Must produce proof of authorization to ODFI upon request; ODFI typically demands within **5–10 business days** of RDFI request. |
| **R11** | Check Truncation Entry Return (used for ARC/BOC/POP; also used for certain authorization defects) | **60 calendar days** from settlement date | Same evidence-production duty as R10; must show the consumer authorized the specific entry. |
| **R29** | Corporate Customer Advises Not Authorized (non-consumer) | **2 banking days** from settlement date | Much shorter window; if any subscribers are business accounts, R29 applies and the evidence-production clock is tighter. |
| **R51** | Item is Ineligible, Notice Not Provided, Signature Not Genuine, or Item Altered (RCK) | **60 calendar days** from settlement date | Applies to re-presented check entries; less likely here unless the merchant also accepts checks, but included in the research question. |
**Key mechanics:**
- **R10 is the dominant risk.** For consumer WEB debits, the RDFI may return R10 up to 60 days after settlement. The consumer’s bank will typically require a **Written Statement of Unauthorized Debit (WSOD)** from the consumer before returning, but the practical burden shifts to the Originator to prove authorization.
- **Evidence production is ODFI-driven.** NACHA does not set a single universal deadline for the Originator to produce proof; instead, the Originator must furnish it **within the timeframe specified by the ODFI**. ODFIs commonly impose **5–10 business days** because they must respond to the RDFI. If Mosaic Relay’s dashboard is the system of record, it must support rapid export of the full authorization bundle: exact language presented, timestamp, IP address, device fingerprint, consumer authentication event, and account details.
- **Two-year retention.** NACHA requires retention of the authorization (or an accurate record) for **two years after termination or revocation**. For WEB entries, the record must be capable of being **reproduced** in a form that shows what the consumer saw and agreed to.
- **Return-rate monitoring.** NACHA and ODFIs monitor unauthorized return rates. An unauthorized return rate above **0.5%** (calculated as unauthorized returns divided by total debits) typically triggers enhanced scrutiny, potential fines, or termination of the ODFI relationship. The dashboard must track R10/R11 rates separately from administrative returns (R01 insufficient funds, etc.).
- **Reinitiation limits.** After an R10 or R11 return, the entry **must not be reinitiated** unless the consumer provides new authorization. After R07 (authorization revoked) or R08 (payment stopped), reinitiation is prohibited. The dashboard’s “returned” status must drive hard stops, not just display.
### Regulation E overlay
- **§ 1005.11 error resolution:** The consumer has 60 days from the statement date to report an unauthorized EFT. The consumer’s bank investigates, but the practical defense rests on the Originator’s authorization evidence.
- **§ 1005.10(c) stop payment:** The consumer can stop payment through their bank on **3 business days’ notice** before the scheduled debit. The system must expect R08 returns and not re-present.
- **§ 1005.10(b) written authorization:** Preauthorized EFTs must be authorized in writing, and the consumer must receive a copy. The email confirmation can serve as the copy if it contains the full authorization terms.
### Allocation of responsibilities
- **Originator (merchant) warrants authorization validity** to the ODFI under NACHA. The merchant is responsible for obtaining valid authorization, retaining evidence, and honoring revocations.
- **Mosaic Relay’s role depends on structure.** If Mosaic Relay is a **Third-Party Sender**, it must register with NACHA, conduct risk assessments, and may warrant merchant compliance. If it is a **Third-Party Service Provider**, its obligations are narrower but still include data security and cooperation with evidence production. The ODFI agreement will define the exact split.
- **Consumer support:** The merchant should be the first point of contact for cancellation and refund requests; Mosaic Relay support should handle technical or platform issues. The checkout and email must disclose both paths.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Mosaic Relay as Third-Party Service Provider (TPSP) — merchant is Originator
- **Structure:** Merchant signs the authorization, retains evidence, and warrants validity. Mosaic Relay provides the API/dashboard and submits entries through its ODFI partner as a service provider.
- **Pros:** Lower NACHA registration burden for Mosaic Relay; merchant bears primary warranty risk.
- **Cons:** Merchant must build or buy evidence-retention and production capability; Mosaic Relay’s dashboard must still support rapid export.
- **Launch feasibility:** Achievable in nine weeks if the merchant has compliance capacity.
### Path B: Mosaic Relay as Third-Party Sender (TPS) — Mosaic Relay is Originator of record
- **Structure:** Mosaic Relay registers with NACHA as a Third-Party Sender, becomes the Originator, and warrants merchant compliance. The merchant is a “sender” under Mosaic Relay’s umbrella.
- **Pros:** Mosaic Relay controls the authorization flow and evidence architecture; can enforce standardized checkout language and retention.
- **Cons:** Higher regulatory burden (NACHA registration, risk assessment, potential ODFI audits); Mosaic Relay bears direct warranty risk for merchant conduct.
- **Launch feasibility:** Tight in nine weeks unless Mosaic Relay already has TPS registration and risk-assessment infrastructure.
### Path C: Hybrid — Mosaic Relay as ODFI agent with delegated evidence production
- **Structure:** Merchant is Originator, but Mosaic Relay contractually agrees to host the system of record and produce authorization evidence to the ODFI on the merchant’s behalf within agreed SLAs.
- **Pros:** Balances risk; merchant retains legal warranty, Mosaic Relay controls the technical evidence pipeline.
- **Cons:** Requires precise contractual drafting to avoid ambiguity about who bears the loss when evidence is insufficient.
- **Launch feasibility:** Most practical for the nine-week timeline if the ODFI agrees.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Mosaic Relay’s NACHA role** (TPSP vs. TPS vs. ODFI agent): Changes registration, warranty, and risk-assessment obligations materially.
- **Variable vs. fixed amounts:** Variable amounts trigger the 10-day pre-debit notice requirement (or range election), which affects checkout UX and email flows.
- **System of record:** If the dashboard is not the system of record, the merchant must maintain its own evidence repository, which may delay launch.
- **Prenotes vs. instant account validation:** Prenotes add 3–6 days before the first debit; instant validation (e.g., Plaid-style) enables immediate access but requires vendor integration.
- **ODFI agreement terms:** Some ODFIs impose stricter evidence-production SLAs (e.g., 3 business days), lower return-rate thresholds, or prohibit certain merchant categories.
- **Business vs. consumer accounts:** If any subscribers use business accounts, Reg E drops out and R29 (2-day return window) applies instead of R10 (60-day window).
