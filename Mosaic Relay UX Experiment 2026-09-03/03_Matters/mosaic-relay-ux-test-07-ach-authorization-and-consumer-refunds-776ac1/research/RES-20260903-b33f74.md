# What are the cancellation, stop-payment, and refund obligations for recurring

## Question

What are the cancellation, stop-payment, and refund obligations for recurring consumer subscriptions paid by ACH, including NACHA revocation rules, Reg E stop-payment, state auto-renewal laws, and the FTC negative-option/ROSCA framework?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
A Mosaic Relay merchant wants to accept recurring ACH debits from consumers for a subscription service (monthly renewals, some digital goods), with checkout capturing bank details and recurring-debit consent, email confirmation, and Mosaic Relay submitting debits through its payment partners and initiating refunds on cancellation. Counsel is involved because recurring consumer ACH debits sit at the intersection of NACHA authorization/revocation rules, Regulation E stop-payment and error-resolution rights, and the federal/state negative-option framework governing subscription cancellation and refunds. The merchant wants to launch in nine weeks (target 2026-11-05), but authorization language, cancellation process, return handling, and responsibility allocation are all unfinalized.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay and the merchant launch recurring consumer ACH debits by 2026-11-05 with a checkout-and-email authorization flow, and what cancellation, stop-payment, and refund mechanics must be built — in-product cancellation, revocation honoring, pre-debit notices, return-driven re-presentment limits, and refund-to-original-method — to satisfy NACHA, Reg E, and the negative-option framework before the first debit is submitted?
## Open questions
- **Mosaic Relay's NACHA role** — third-party service provider, Third-Party Sender, or Originator of record? This determines registration, warranty, and risk-assessment obligations and who responds to RDFI proof-of-authorization requests.
- **Fixed vs. variable amounts** — if variable, a 10-day pre-debit notice engine (or a precisely drafted range election) becomes a launch blocker.
- **System of record for authorization evidence** — whether the dashboard holds immutable, exportable authorization records and who produces them to the RDFI within NACHA/ODFI timeframes.
- **Unauthorized-debit claim routing** — who responds to the consumer's financial institution, who bears WSOD/R10 losses, and what the ODFI agreement's return-rate thresholds and evidence SLAs require.
- **State auto-renewal law scope** — which states' ARLs apply given the consumer base, and whether any impose cancellation or notice requirements stricter than the federal baseline.

## Likely rules and issues
### NACHA revocation and cancellation
- **Revocation of authorization.** NACHA requires that a consumer be able to revoke a recurring debit authorization by notifying the Originator (merchant) in the manner specified in the authorization. A debit submitted after revocation is subject to return as **R07 (authorization revoked)**. The authorization language must tell the consumer how to revoke, and the operational stack (merchant → Mosaic Relay → ODFI) must actually stop the next scheduled debit.
- **Reinitiation limits.** NACHA caps reinitiation of returned debits — generally no more than two reinitiations following a return, and **no reinitiation after R07, R08, or R10-type returns**. The dashboard's "returned" status must drive re-presentment logic, not merely display status.
- **Return-rate monitoring.** NACHA imposes unauthorized return-rate thresholds (0.5% is the traditional scrutiny line); ODFIs enforce their own, often stricter, contractual thresholds. Elevated R07/R10 rates will draw ODFI attention and potential termination.
### Regulation E stop-payment and error resolution
- **Stop payment (§ 1005.10(c)).** A consumer can stop payment on a preauthorized EFT by notifying their bank at least **3 business days before the scheduled debit**. The system must expect **R08 (payment stopped)** returns and must not re-present them.
- **Error resolution (§ 1005.11).** The consumer's bank handles the Reg E error-resolution clock, but the practical burden lands on the Originator to produce authorization evidence to defeat an "unauthorized" claim. R10 returns can arrive up to **60 days from settlement** (the consumer's Reg E window).
- **Written authorization and copy (§ 1005.10(b)).** Preauthorized EFTs must be authorized in writing, and the consumer must receive a copy. The email confirmation can serve as the copy **only if it contains the full authorization terms**, not just a receipt.
### FTC negative-option / ROSCA framework
- **ROSCA** (15 U.S.C. § 8403) applies to online negative-option features: it requires (a) clear and conspicuous disclosure of material terms before billing information is collected, (b) **express informed consent** before charging, and (c) a **simple mechanism to stop recurring charges**. Monthly-renewing consumer subscriptions for digital goods sit squarely in ROSCA territory.
- **FTC Negative Option Rule ("click-to-cancel").** The FTC's 2024 amended rule — which would have mandated cancellation as easy as signup and separate consent for the negative-option feature — was **vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025** on procedural grounds before its compliance date. The operative federal framework is therefore ROSCA plus FTC Act § 5 enforcement, not the vacated rule. The FTC continues to bring "click-to-cancel"-style cases under ROSCA and § 5, so the substance of easy cancellation remains an enforcement expectation even without the rule.
- **State auto-renewal laws (ARLs).** Roughly half the states have ARLs; **California's** (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17600 et seq., as amended by SB-313 and later bills) is the strictest template: clear and conspicuous pre-purchase disclosure of renewal terms, affirmative consent, an acknowledgment email with cancellation instructions, online cancellation for online signups, and notice of material changes. Several states (e.g., New York, and others with recently amended ARLs) impose similar or additional requirements, and some treat violations as per-se deceptive practices. Because the consumer base is presumably national, the practical standard is to build to the strictest applicable ARL.
### Refund obligations
- **NACHA does not mandate refund timing** for cancellations — refunds are governed by contract and consumer-protection law. The refund obligation flows from the merchant's disclosed cancellation policy, ROSCA/ARL requirements, and state UDAP statutes.
- **Refund mechanics.** Refunds should go to the original payment method (ACH credit reversal), with timing and exceptions disclosed. Mosaic Relay initiating refunds "when the customer cancels" needs defined cutoffs: a cancellation before the next debit's submission cutoff should prevent the debit; a cancellation after submission may require a refund instead.
- **Digital goods wrinkle.** Instant-access digital goods create pressure to skip prenotes and debit immediately — which heightens the importance of account validation and clear first-debit disclosure, and increases R10 exposure if authorization evidence is weak.

## Viable paths
**Path A — Build to the strictest baseline (recommended first pass).**
Checkout uses clickwrap authorization with full recurring terms (frequency, amount or amount-determination method, revocation instructions); email confirmation carries the complete authorization copy; in-product cancellation is as easy as signup; cancellation before the debit-submission cutoff stops the next debit; refunds go to the original method with disclosed timing; no re-presentment after R07/R08/R10; authorization records are immutable and exportable with a production SLA. This satisfies NACHA, Reg E, ROSCA, and the strictest ARLs simultaneously, and is achievable in nine weeks if the variable-amount question resolves quickly.
**Path B — Minimal compliance, state-by-state triage.**
Build only to NACHA + Reg E + ROSCA federal baseline and triage ARL exposure by consumer location. Cheaper up front but creates a patchwork: California and other strict-ARL consumers would need different flows, and enforcement risk shifts to the merchant's disclosure practices. Not recommended given a national consumer base and the nine-week timeline — triage costs more than building to the ceiling.
**Path C — Delay variable amounts.**
Launch with fixed-amount subscriptions only (no 10-day pre-debit notice engine needed), and add variable amounts in a later phase with either the 10-day notice flow or a range election drafted into the authorization. This de-risks the 2026-11-05 date if engineering capacity is tight.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Mosaic Relay's NACHA role.** If Mosaic Relay is a Third-Party Sender rather than a pure TPSP/ODFI agent, it must register with NACHA, perform risk assessments, and may warrant merchant conduct — materially expanding its obligations and the allocation analysis.
- **Variable amounts.** If the merchant will debit variable amounts, the 10-day pre-debit notice requirement (or a precisely drafted range election) becomes a launch-blocking build item.
- **ODFI/processor agreement terms.** Evidence-production SLAs, return-rate thresholds, and prohibited-practice clauses are often stricter than NACHA baseline and could impose shorter response windows or lower return tolerances.
- **Consumer base geography.** If the consumer base is concentrated in strict-ARL states, the cancellation and disclosure build must hit those state specifics; if any subscribers are businesses, Reg E drops out for those debits.
- **Whether the dashboard is the system of record.** If yes, Mosaic Relay owns the immutability, exportability, and production-SLA architecture — an engineering commitment, not just a policy.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — Mosaic Relay's NACHA role.** This analysis assumes Mosaic Relay acts as a third-party service provider / ODFI agent, not the Originator of record or a registered Third-Party Sender. If Mosaic Relay is the Originator or a Third-Party Sender, the warranty, registration, risk-assessment, and evidence-production sections expand materially, and the allocation-of-responsibilities analysis shifts toward Mosaic Relay bearing direct NACHA exposure.
- **Open fork — variable vs. fixed amounts.** If the merchant will use variable amounts, the product must either build a 10-day pre-debit notice engine or capture a range-based election in the authorization — a launch-blocking build item. If fixed only, that requirement drops out and Path C becomes unnecessary.
- **Open fork — system of record.** If the dashboard is the system of record for authorization evidence, Mosaic Relay must guarantee immutability, exportability, and a production SLA to the ODFI/RDFI — an architecture decision with engineering lead time. If the merchant retains evidence, Mosaic Relay's obligation narrows to transmission and the merchant bears the production burden.
- **Not examined — the ODFI/processor agreement.** The vault does not contain the actual ODFI or processor agreement terms. Those agreements routinely impose evidence-production SLAs, return-rate thresholds, and prohibited-practice clauses stricter than NACHA baseline, and could change the operational requirements and loss allocation described here.
- **Not examined — specific state ARL texts.** No state auto-renewal statute was read in this pass. The analysis treats California's ARL as the strictest template based on model knowledge, but the specific disclosure, acknowledgment, and cancellation-mechanism requirements of each applicable state's current statute were not verified against primary sources.
- **Working assumption — consumer accounts only.** The analysis assumes all debits hit consumer accounts subject to Reg E. If any subscribers are small businesses using business accounts, Reg E protections drop out for those debits, different NACHA return codes and timelines apply, and the cancellation/refund analysis shifts to contract and state UDAP law alone.
