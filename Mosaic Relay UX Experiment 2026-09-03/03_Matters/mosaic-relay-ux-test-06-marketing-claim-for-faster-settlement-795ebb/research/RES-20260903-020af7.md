# What does FTC guidance require to substantiate a qualified speed-of-funds-availa

## Question

What does FTC guidance require to substantiate a qualified speed-of-funds-availability claim in payment advertising?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay's marketing team wants to advertise "instant settlement for every merchant" across the website, sales decks, and paid search ads, launching in four weeks. Product confirms some funds can be made available quickly, but timing depends on payment method, risk review, bank processing, reserves, disputes, and merchant configuration — and there is no universal service level. Counsel is involved because the claim as written is an unsubstantiated express factual claim, creating FTC Section 5 deception exposure. Marketing has accepted that a qualified framing ("up to," "as fast as," "for eligible merchants") is acceptable, but no aggregate timing data, eligibility conditions, or geographic scope have been defined.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay run any speed-of-funds-availability claim in paid search, website, and sales materials within four weeks, and if so, what specific qualified language, disclosures, and substantiation data must be in place before launch to avoid FTC Section 5 deception liability?
## Open questions
- What does "settlement" mean operationally — processor settlement, availability in a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow, or receipt in the merchant's external bank account? This determines what must be substantiated and disclosed.
- What aggregate timing data exists (or can be produced in four weeks) showing what share of transactions meet a stated speed, over what measurement window, segmented by payment method and merchant type?
- What are the specific eligibility conditions and exclusions (payment methods, merchant segments, reserve policies, dispute holds, risk review triggers) that determine when faster availability applies?
- What geographic scope does the claim cover — U.S.-only, or does it extend to markets where Mosaic Relay's payout controls and bank partnerships differ?
- Can paid search ad character limits accommodate the required disclosures, or must the claim be restricted to website and sales channels where fuller qualification is feasible?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
The following is model-only analysis based on established FTC advertising principles. It has not been verified against current FTC guidance documents, enforcement actions, or policy statements.
### FTC Section 5 — deception framework
Under FTC Act Section 5, an ad is deceptive if it contains a material representation or omission likely to mislead a reasonable consumer. "Instant settlement for every merchant" is an **express factual claim** — not puffery — because it asserts a specific, measurable performance characteristic. Express claims require **prior substantiation**: the advertiser must possess a reasonable basis for the claim *before* disseminating it.
The claim as written is almost certainly unsubstantiable:
- "Instant" is an absolute temporal claim. Even near-real-time availability for selected transactions does not support "instant" as a universal descriptor.
- "Every merchant" is a universal coverage claim. The known facts confirm timing varies by payment method, risk review, reserves, disputes, and merchant configuration — meaning the claim is false for a material subset of merchants.
- No aggregate timing data exists. There is no denominator, no measurement window, no percentage of transactions meeting any stated speed.
### Substantiation standard for qualified claims
Even a qualified claim ("up to X," "as fast as Y," "for eligible merchants") requires:
1. **A defined, measurable claim.** "Up to same-day" or "funds available in as little as X minutes/hours" — the specific speed must be stated.
2. **Competent and reliable evidence.** For performance claims, this typically means internal testing or transaction data showing the claimed speed is achieved under the stated conditions. Anecdotal examples are insufficient; the data must be representative.
3. **Clear and conspicuous disclosure of material conditions.** If the speed applies only to certain payment methods, merchant tiers, or transaction types, those limitations must be disclosed in a way that is difficult to miss — not buried in footnotes or hyperlinked terms.
4. **The "up to" ceiling must be achievable.** FTC guidance on "up to" claims (developed in the context of energy savings and internet speed claims) requires that the maximum claimed performance be achievable by a meaningful proportion of users under normal conditions — not a theoretical edge case.
### Channel-specific constraints
- **Paid search ads** have severe character limits. If the claim cannot be fairly communicated with necessary qualifications in the ad format, the claim should not run in that channel. A bare "instant settlement" headline in a Google Ad with no room for qualification is the highest-risk placement.
- **Website** allows fuller disclosure — a dedicated page or section explaining eligibility, typical timing ranges, and exclusions. This is the lowest-risk channel for a qualified claim.
- **Sales presentations** sit between the two. Verbal qualification by a salesperson does not cure a misleading slide. The deck itself must carry the qualification.
### "Settlement" ambiguity
The undefined term "settlement" is a material problem. If it means **receipt in the merchant's external bank account**, Mosaic Relay does not control the final leg (bank processing) and cannot substantiate a speed claim for that endpoint. If it means **availability in a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow**, the claim is more controllable but must clearly communicate that "available" does not mean "in your bank account." If it means **processor settlement**, the claim depends on processor timelines that Mosaic Relay does not fully control.

## Viable paths
### Path A — Do not run the claim as written (recommended starting position)
"Instant settlement for every merchant" cannot be substantiated. Full stop. This is not a close call. The claim is false on its face for a material subset of merchants, and no data exists to support even a qualified version.
### Path B — Qualified claim with substantiation (achievable in four weeks, with effort)
If Product and Operations can produce segmented timing data within two weeks, a qualified claim is viable:
- **Website:** "Funds available as fast as [X] for eligible merchants" with a linked or adjacent disclosure listing qualifying payment methods, merchant criteria, and exclusions (reserves, disputes, risk review holds).
- **Sales presentations:** Same qualified claim on the slide, with a speaker-notes disclosure script and a leave-behind document with full conditions.
- **Paid search:** Highest risk. If character limits prevent adequate qualification, either (a) use a softer claim like "Fast access to your funds" (puffery-adjacent, lower risk) or (b) do not run speed claims in paid search.
### Path C — Delay launch until data exists
If Product cannot produce aggregate timing data within two weeks, the campaign should be delayed. Running any speed claim — even a qualified one — without substantiation data in hand before launch violates the prior-substantiation requirement.
### Path D — Non-speed alternative messaging
Shift the campaign to claims that are substantiable today: "Flexible payout controls," "Configurable settlement timing," "Get funds on your schedule." These are feature claims, not performance claims, and carry lower substantiation burdens.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If Product produces robust aggregate timing data** showing, for example, that 80%+ of card transactions for eligible merchants settle within 30 minutes, a much stronger qualified claim becomes viable.
- **If "settlement" is defined as availability in a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow** (not bank receipt), the claim is more controllable and easier to substantiate — but the disclosure must be explicit that this is not bank-account receipt.
- **If the campaign is U.S.-only**, the analysis is simpler. If it extends to other jurisdictions, local advertising standards (e.g., UK CAP Code, EU UCPD) impose similar but not identical requirements.
- **If paid search is dropped from the channel mix**, the disclosure problem becomes much easier to solve.
- **If a competitor has been cited by the FTC or a state AG for similar claims**, enforcement risk increases materially.

- **Working assumption — no aggregate timing data exists.** The facts state only anecdotal examples exist.
