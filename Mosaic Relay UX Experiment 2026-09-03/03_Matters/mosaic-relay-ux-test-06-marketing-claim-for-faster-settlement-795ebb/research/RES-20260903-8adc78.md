# What disclosure placement and character-limit constraints apply to speed claims

## Question

What disclosure placement and character-limit constraints apply to speed claims in paid search ads under FTC and network rules?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay’s marketing team wants to run a campaign claiming “instant settlement for every merchant” across the website, sales decks, and paid search ads, starting in four weeks. Product confirms some funds can be made available quickly, but timing depends on payment method, risk review, bank processing, reserves, disputes, and merchant configuration, and there is no universal service level or aggregate timing data. Counsel is involved because the claim is an express speed claim that currently lacks substantiation, defined eligibility conditions, geographic scope, and even a settled definition of “settlement.” The specific research question here is narrow: what disclosure placement and character-limit constraints apply to speed claims in paid search ads under FTC standards and ad-network rules.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay run any settlement-speed claim in paid search ads within four weeks — given that paid search formats offer roughly 30-character headlines and 90-character descriptions where qualifying disclosures may not fit — and if so, what qualified language and disclosure placement (in-ad versus landing page) satisfies FTC “clear and conspicuous” standards and Google Ads financial-services policies, when no aggregate timing data yet exists to substantiate any specific speed?
## Open questions
- What does “settlement” mean for this claim — processor settlement, availability in a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow, or receipt in the merchant’s external bank account? (Undecided; each meaning requires different substantiation and different disclosure content.)
- What aggregate timing data can Product and Operations produce — dated, segmented records showing what share of transactions meet a stated speed over a defined measurement window? (None exists today; only unverified anecdotal examples.)
- Which merchants, payment methods, and transaction types would qualify for any speed claim, and what exclusions apply (reserves, disputes, risk review, bank processing delays, merchant configuration)? (Not yet mapped.)
- What geographic scope will the campaign cover, and do processor/payout capabilities differ by state or region? (Not yet decided.)
- Will Google Ads’ financial products and services verification requirements apply to Mosaic Relay’s ads, and does Mosaic Relay hold the certifications Google requires for payments-related advertising in target markets? (Not yet confirmed with the ad platforms.)

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The analysis below is model-generated based on general knowledge of FTC advertising standards and ad-network policy frameworks. It has not been verified against current FTC guidance or live Google Ads policy text.
### FTC standards applicable to paid search disclosures
**FTC Act Section 5 — deception and substantiation.** An objective claim like “instant settlement” is an express factual claim that requires a reasonable basis — typically competent and reliable evidence — before dissemination. “Instant settlement for every merchant” is almost certainly unsubstantiable as written: there is no universal service level, and timing depends on multiple variables outside Mosaic Relay’s control. Even a qualified claim (“up to same-day availability”) requires data showing the claimed speed is achievable by a meaningful proportion of merchants under disclosed conditions.
**FTC “clear and conspicuous” disclosure standard.** Under FTC guidance (including the .com Disclosures guidance and the 2022 updated staff guidance on digital disclosures), a disclosure that qualifies a claim must be:
- **Proximate** to the claim it qualifies — on the same screen, ideally adjacent to the triggering claim;
- **Prominent** — in a size, color, and format that consumers will actually notice;
- **Unavoidable** — not buried behind a hyperlink, scroll, or tab;
- **Understandable** — in plain language the target audience will comprehend.
**The paid search problem.** Paid search ads (Google Ads responsive search ads) allow headlines of up to 30 characters and description lines of up to 90 characters. A disclosure like “Available to eligible merchants; timing varies by payment method, risk review, and bank processing” is approximately 100 characters — it will not fit in a single description line alongside a speed claim, and it certainly will not fit in a headline. The FTC has consistently taken the position that **if a disclosure necessary to prevent deception cannot be made clearly and conspicuously within the format, the claim should not be made in that format.** The .com Disclosures guidance specifically addresses space-constrained ads and states that advertisers should not make claims in space-constrained formats if the required disclosure cannot be effectively communicated in that format. A hyperlink to a landing page containing the disclosure is generally **not sufficient** to cure a deceptive claim in the ad itself, particularly for material terms.
**Implication:** If Mosaic Relay cannot fit both a speed claim and its material qualifications within Google Ads’ character limits in a way that is clear and conspicuous, the speed claim should not run in paid search. The claim could run on the website (where space allows full disclosure) and in sales presentations (where a representative can explain qualifications), but paid search may be limited to non-speed-dependent messaging or to a claim so heavily qualified it loses marketing punch.
### Ad network rules (Google Ads)
**Google Ads Financial Products and Services policy.** Google requires advertisers promoting financial products and services to comply with local laws and may require verification (e.g., Google’s financial services verification program in certain markets). Payment processing and money transmission services may fall within scope. Misleading or unsubstantiated claims about speed of service could violate Google’s misrepresentation policy, which prohibits ads that deceive users by omitting material information or making claims unlikely to be fulfilled.
**Google Ads editorial and misrepresentation policies.** Google prohibits ads that make exaggerated or unsubstantiated claims. “Instant settlement for every merchant” — if not universally true — could be flagged as misleading. Google’s policies require that claims in ads be supportable and that landing pages be consistent with ad claims.
**Character limits as a practical constraint.** Google Ads responsive search ads: up to 15 headlines (30 characters each) and 4 descriptions (90 characters each). The system auto-combines headlines and descriptions, meaning a qualifying disclosure in one headline may not appear alongside the speed claim in another headline. This auto-combination feature makes it practically impossible to guarantee that a disclosure always appears proximate to the claim — a significant FTC compliance problem.
### State law overlay
State UDAP (unfair and deceptive acts and practices) statutes mirror FTC Section 5 in most states and may impose additional requirements. Some states (e.g., California under the False Advertising Law and Consumer Legal Remedies Act) have been used to challenge speed and performance claims in advertising. The geographic scope of the campaign has not been decided, so the full state-law overlay has not been mapped.

## Viable paths
**Path 1 — No speed claim in paid search; qualified speed claim on website and in sales materials.**
Run paid search ads with non-speed-dependent messaging (e.g., “Payment infrastructure for marketplaces” or “Fast, reliable payouts — see how”). Put the qualified speed claim (once substantiated) on the website landing page with full disclosures adjacent to the claim. Use sales presentations for detailed speed discussions with verbal qualification. This is the lowest-risk path and achievable within the four-week timeline if substantiation data can be assembled for the website claim.
**Path 2 — Heavily qualified speed claim in paid search.**
Attempt a claim like “Same-day payouts available” (headline, 26 characters) with a description like “For eligible merchants. Terms apply.” (description, 37 characters). This is risky: “terms apply” is generally insufficient as a disclosure under FTC standards because it does not communicate the material conditions. The auto-combination problem in responsive search ads means the qualification may not appear with the claim. This path requires careful legal review of every possible ad combination and is not recommended without aggregate timing data.
**Path 3 — Delay paid search speed claims until substantiation exists.**
Launch the campaign in four weeks with non-speed messaging in paid search. Add speed claims to paid search only after Product and Operations produce aggregate timing data, eligibility conditions are defined, and a claim can be drafted that fits within character limits with adequate disclosure. This preserves the launch timeline while deferring the highest-risk element.
**Path 4 — Challenge the format, not the claim.**
Use paid search to drive to a landing page that makes the qualified speed claim with full disclosure. The ad itself says something like “How fast are your payouts?” (a question, not a claim) or “See our settlement speed” (an invitation, not a representation). This threads the needle but may reduce click-through rates.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If aggregate timing data shows a high percentage of transactions settle same-day** (e.g., 90%+ of eligible transactions), a qualified “same-day” claim becomes more defensible, and the disclosure burden decreases.
- **If “settlement” is defined as availability within a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow** (not receipt in the merchant’s bank account), the claim is more within Mosaic Relay’s control and easier to substantiate — but also less meaningful to merchants and potentially more deceptive if merchants understand “settlement” to mean money in their bank.
- **If the campaign is limited to a single state or a small number of states**, the state-law analysis becomes tractable and may reveal either additional constraints or safe harbors.
- **If Google Ads’ financial services verification applies and Mosaic Relay has not completed it**, the ads may not run at all regardless of claim content.
- **If marketing will accept a non-speed headline in paid search** (Path 1 or 4), the entire disclosure-placement problem in paid search is mooted.

If FTC has signaled acceptance of landing-page-only disclosures for space-constrained formats (which I do not believe it has, but have not verified against the most current guidance), Path 2 becomes more viable. - **Open fork:** The definition of “settlement” is undecided. If it means “receipt in the merchant’s external bank account,” substantiation requires data on bank processing times that Mosaic Relay does not control. Each path produces materially different disclosure language and risk.
- **Open fork:** Whether Google Ads’ financial services verification program applies to Mosaic Relay’s ad account. If it does and verification is incomplete, no ads run regardless of claim content. If it does not apply, the analysis proceeds on FTC and state UDAP grounds alone.
