# What is the legal and practical distinction between processor settlement

## Question

What is the legal and practical distinction between processor settlement, platform-controlled availability, and receipt in the merchant's external bank account for purposes of substantiating and disclosing a speed-of-funds claim in payment advertising? Which meaning must a claim use to avoid FTC Section 5 deception risk, and what does each meaning require to be substantiated?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay’s marketing team proposes advertising “instant settlement for every merchant” across website, sales presentations, and paid search ads beginning in four weeks. Product confirms the platform can make some funds available quickly through processor and payout controls, but actual timing depends on payment method, risk review, bank processing, reserves, disputes, and merchant configuration. Counsel is involved because the claim is an express, universal speed claim that currently lacks substantiation, defined eligibility conditions, and a settled meaning of “settlement.” The matter is closed with a recorded decision to use only interim capability wording until substantiation and channel review are complete.
## Decision question
Can Mosaic Relay substantiate and disclose a speed-of-funds claim using “settlement” to mean processor settlement, platform-controlled availability, or receipt in the merchant’s external bank account, and which meaning must the claim use to avoid FTC Section 5 deception risk given the four-week launch timeline and the absence of aggregate timing data?
## Open questions
- What does “settlement” mean for this claim — processor settlement, availability in a Mosaic Relay-controlled workflow, or receipt in the merchant’s external bank account?
- What aggregate timing data exists to substantiate any speed claim — what share of transactions meet the claimed speed, and over what measurement window?
- What eligibility conditions and exclusions apply to any speed claim — which merchants/payment methods qualify, and what are the exclusions (reserves, disputes, risk review, bank processing, merchant configuration)?
- What geographic scope does the claim cover?
- Can paid-search speed claims be qualified within platform character limits and disclosure constraints?
## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
The controlling framework is FTC Act Section 5’s prohibition on deceptive acts or practices. An objective claim like “instant settlement for every merchant” is an express factual claim that requires a reasonable basis — typically competent and reliable evidence — before dissemination. The claim is deceptive if it misleads a reasonable consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances and is material to the purchasing decision.
Three distinct meanings of “settlement” create materially different substantiation and disclosure obligations:
1. **Processor settlement** — the point at which the card network or ACH operator confirms funds movement between the acquiring bank and Mosaic Relay’s designated account. This is an internal ledger event invisible to the merchant. Substantiation requires processor-level timing data showing when settlement instructions are confirmed. Disclosure must clarify that this is not merchant receipt.
2. **Platform-controlled availability** — the point at which Mosaic Relay’s systems mark funds as available for payout or use within its own workflow. This is the earliest point Mosaic Relay controls. Substantiation requires dated, segmented platform logs showing when funds become available. Disclosure must clarify that availability does not equal bank receipt and that external transfer time applies.
3. **Receipt in the merchant’s external bank account** — the point at which funds are actually credited to the merchant’s account at their financial institution. This is the only meaning a reasonable merchant is likely to understand from an unqualified “settlement” claim. Substantiation requires end-to-end timing data from transaction initiation through external bank posting, including weekends, holidays, and bank-specific processing delays. Disclosure must account for factors outside Mosaic Relay’s control.
The universal “every merchant” framing is independently problematic because timing varies by payment method, risk review, reserves, disputes, and merchant configuration. Even a qualified “up to” claim requires aggregate data showing what percentage of transactions achieve the claimed speed and under what conditions.
## Viable paths
**Path A — Do not launch any speed claim.** Use only capability wording such as “fast access to funds” or “quick payouts” without numeric or temporal promises. This is the lowest-risk path and aligns with the recorded decision.
**Path B — Qualified claim with defined settlement event.** Choose one meaning of settlement (preferably platform-controlled availability as the most controllable), define it precisely, gather dated segmented timing data, map eligibility and exclusions, and craft channel-specific disclosures. Paid search may not accommodate adequate qualification.
**Path C — Universal claim with external-bank-receipt meaning.** Attempt to substantiate “instant settlement” as receipt in the merchant’s external bank account. This is not viable on current facts because there is no universal service level, no aggregate data, and timing depends on factors outside Mosaic Relay’s control.
## Facts that could change the answer
- Product and Operations produce dated, segmented timing data showing a high percentage of transactions achieve a specific speed under defined conditions.
- Processor and acquiring bank partners confirm they can support a defined settlement event with measurable timing.
- Marketing accepts a qualified framing limited to platform-controlled availability with clear disclosure that external bank receipt takes additional time.
- Paid search platform rules allow sufficient characters for meaningful qualification.
- Geographic scope is limited to U.S.-only merchants with uniform banking practices.
## Recommended last-mile verification
Before any speed claim launches, verify: (1) the chosen definition of “settlement” is used consistently across all channels; (2) aggregate timing data covers a representative measurement window and includes a denominator; (3) eligibility conditions and exclusions are mapped and disclosed; (4) paid search ads can accommodate required qualifications within platform limits; (5) any implication of external bank receipt is supported by end-to-end data; and (6) Compliance, Product, Operations, and Processor have signed off on the substantiation file.
## What would change this
- **Working assumption:** The claim is treated as an express factual claim requiring substantiation rather than puffery. If “instant settlement” were deemed puffery, the analysis changes, but the specificity of “instant” and “every merchant” makes puffery unlikely.
- **Open fork:** If “settlement” is defined as platform-controlled availability rather than external bank receipt, the substantiation burden drops significantly but the disclosure burden increases to prevent consumer confusion.
- **Not examined:** The specific processor and acquiring bank contracts were not reviewed for timing commitments or service level agreements that could support or undermine a speed claim.
- **Not examined:** State attorney general guidance or state unfair and deceptive acts and practices statutes that may impose additional requirements beyond FTC Section 5.
- **Working assumption:** The four-week launch timeline is fixed. If the timeline extends, there may be opportunity to gather substantiation data for a qualified claim.
