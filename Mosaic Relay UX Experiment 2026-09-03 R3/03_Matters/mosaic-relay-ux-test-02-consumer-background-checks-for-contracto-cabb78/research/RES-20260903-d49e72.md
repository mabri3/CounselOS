# State-level ban-the-box and consumer-reporting laws affecting contractor screeni

## Question

State-level ban-the-box and consumer-reporting laws affecting contractor screening

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure provider, wants to offer platform customers a feature that screens contractors before they can receive payouts. The feature would collect contractor consent and identifying information, obtain background reports from a vendor, and return "eligible," "manual review," or "not eligible" to the platform customer. The platform customer makes the final eligibility decision. Screening would occur during onboarding, annually, and after certain fraud or dispute events. Counsel is involved because the feature implicates federal FCRA obligations and state-level consumer-reporting and ban-the-box laws that vary by jurisdiction and could restrict when and how criminal history is used.
## Decision question
May Mosaic Relay provide a contractor-screening feature that returns eligibility indicators to platform customers, and if so, what federal FCRA and state-level consumer-reporting, ban-the-box, and fair-chance hiring laws govern the consent, disclosure, adverse-action, and criminal-history-use requirements when the platform customer makes the final payout-eligibility decision?
## Open questions
- Which specific states and localities will the screened contractors be located in? (State and local ban-the-box and fair-chance laws vary significantly and impose different timing, notice, and individualized-assessment requirements.)
- Will the background-screening vendor operate as a consumer reporting agency (CRA) under FCRA, or will it provide non-FCRA data? (This determines whether FCRA disclosure, authorization, permissible-purpose, and adverse-action obligations apply.)
- What specific criminal-record information will the report contain, and what decision rules will map report contents to "eligible," "manual review," or "not eligible"? (State laws restrict use of arrests not resulting in conviction, expunged records, and convictions beyond certain lookback periods.)
- Will Mosaic Relay or the platform customer be the "user" of the consumer report under FCRA, and who will certify permissible purpose to the CRA? (This affects who bears adverse-action and dispute-handling duties.)
- What adverse-action procedures, dispute mechanisms, and retention periods will be implemented? (FCRA and state laws require specific pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notices, dispute rights, and record-retention limits.)

## Likely rules and issues
### Federal FCRA framework
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on model knowledge, the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq., governs when a third party (a consumer reporting agency or CRA) provides a "consumer report" — defined broadly to include criminal-background checks, identity verification, and other personal information used for employment or eligibility decisions — to a "user" who has a "permissible purpose."
Key FCRA obligations likely to apply:
1. **Permissible purpose and certification** (15 U.S.C. § 1681b, § 1681e(a)): The user (Mosaic Relay or the platform customer) must certify to the CRA that it has a permissible purpose (here, likely "employment purposes" which FCRA defines to include independent contractors) and will comply with FCRA.
2. **Disclosure and authorization** (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(A)): Before procuring the report, the user must provide the contractor a clear and conspicuous standalone disclosure that a consumer report may be obtained, and must obtain the contractor's written authorization. The disclosure cannot be buried in an employment application or other document.
3. **Pre-adverse-action requirements** (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)): Before taking adverse action (here, denying or restricting payouts) based in whole or in part on the report, the user must provide the contractor: (a) a copy of the report, and (b) the CFPB's "Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act."
4. **Adverse-action notice** (15 U.S.C. § 1681m): After taking adverse action, the user must provide: (a) notice of the adverse action, (b) the name, address, and phone number of the CRA, (c) a statement that the CRA did not make the decision and cannot explain it, and (d) notice of the contractor's right to obtain a free copy of the report within 60 days and to dispute inaccurate information.
5. **Accuracy and dispute obligations** (15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b), § 1681i): CRAs must follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy. Contractors have the right to dispute inaccurate information, and CRAs must investigate and correct errors.
### State-level consumer-reporting and ban-the-box laws
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on model knowledge, many states and localities impose additional requirements beyond FCRA:
1. **Ban-the-box / fair-chance hiring laws**: Over 35 states and 150+ localities have enacted laws restricting when employers (and in some cases, entities making eligibility decisions about contractors) may inquire about or use criminal history. Common features:
- **Timing restrictions**: Prohibiting criminal-history inquiries until after a conditional offer or initial screening (e.g., California, Illinois, New York City, Philadelphia).
- **Individualized assessment**: Requiring consideration of the nature and gravity of the offense, time elapsed, and relationship to the role before denying eligibility (e.g., California, New York, Hawaii).
- **Notice requirements**: Requiring specific pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notices that identify the disqualifying conviction and provide an opportunity to respond (e.g., California, New York City, Los Angeles, San Francisco).
- **Prohibited records**: Barring use of arrests not resulting in conviction, expunged or sealed records, or convictions beyond a lookback period (often 7 years) (e.g., California, New York, Massachusetts, Washington).
2. **State consumer-reporting laws**: Some states have their own consumer-reporting statutes that mirror or exceed FCRA (e.g., California's Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (ICRAA) and Consumer Credit Reporting Agencies Act (CCRAA), New York's Fair Credit Reporting Act, Washington's Fair Credit Reporting Act). These may impose additional disclosure, consent, accuracy, and dispute requirements.
3. **State-specific criminal-history-use restrictions**: Some states restrict use of certain types of criminal records for employment or eligibility decisions (e.g., California Labor Code § 432.7 prohibits asking about arrests not resulting in conviction; New York Correction Law Article 23-A requires individualized assessment and prohibits blanket exclusions).
### Sanctions screening and identity verification
Sanctions screening (e.g., OFAC list checks) and identity verification are generally **not** governed by FCRA if they do not involve a "consumer report" from a CRA. However, if the vendor bundles sanctions/identity data with criminal-history data in a single report, the entire report may be subject to FCRA. Separating these functions may reduce FCRA exposure but does not eliminate state-law obligations.
### Payout restrictions and consumer-protection issues
Using screening results to restrict payouts may implicate:
- **FCRA adverse-action requirements** (as described above).
- **State fair-chance laws** that restrict use of criminal history for eligibility decisions.
- **Contract and consumer-protection laws** if contractors are not given clear notice of the screening and payout-restriction criteria.
- **Funds-flow and payment-processing rules** if payout restrictions are not disclosed in the platform's terms of service.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Full FCRA compliance with Mosaic Relay as the "user"
**Structure**: Mosaic Relay contracts with a CRA, certifies permissible purpose, obtains contractor consent, provides FCRA disclosures, and handles all pre-adverse-action and adverse-action notices. Mosaic Relay returns only "eligible," "manual review," or "not eligible" to the platform customer, and the platform customer makes the final decision based on that indicator.
**Pros**:
- Centralizes FCRA compliance with Mosaic Relay, reducing burden on platform customers.
- Limits platform customers' exposure to detailed reports, aligning with the business goal.
- Mosaic Relay controls the decision rules and can build in state-law compliance (e.g., individualized assessment, lookback limits).
**Cons**:
- Mosaic Relay bears full FCRA liability for disclosure, authorization, adverse-action, and accuracy obligations.
- Requires Mosaic Relay to implement robust adverse-action and dispute-handling procedures.
- May require state-by-state customization of decision rules and notices.
**Key requirements**:
- Standalone FCRA disclosure and written authorization from contractors.
- Pre-adverse-action notice with report copy and CFPB Summary of Rights.
- Adverse-action notice with CRA contact info and dispute rights.
- State-law compliance (ban-the-box timing, individualized assessment, prohibited records).
- Contract with CRA requiring FCRA compliance and accuracy procedures.
### Path 2: Platform customer as the "user" with Mosaic Relay as a service provider
**Structure**: The platform customer contracts directly with the CRA (or Mosaic Relay acts as an agent of the platform customer), certifies permissible purpose, and bears FCRA obligations. Mosaic Relay provides the technology and workflow but does not make or communicate the eligibility decision.
**Pros**:
- Shifts FCRA compliance burden to the platform customer.
- Mosaic Relay's role is limited to data transmission and workflow management.
**Cons**:
- Platform customers may lack FCRA expertise and compliance infrastructure.
- May expose platform customers to detailed reports, undermining the business goal.
- Mosaic Relay may still face liability if it is deemed a "user" or if it influences the decision rules.
**Key requirements**:
- Clear contractual allocation of FCRA duties to the platform customer.
- Platform customer provides FCRA disclosures, obtains authorization, and handles adverse action.
- Mosaic Relay's role is limited to facilitating the process.
### Path 3: Hybrid model with Mosaic Relay as "user" for screening, platform customer as decision-maker
**Structure**: Mosaic Relay obtains the report, applies decision rules, and returns "eligible," "manual review," or "not eligible." The platform customer makes the final payout-eligibility decision. Mosaic Relay handles FCRA disclosures and adverse-action notices for the screening step; the platform customer handles any additional adverse-action notices if it overrides or supplements the screening result.
**Pros**:
- Balances compliance burden between Mosaic Relay and the platform customer.
- Allows Mosaic Relay to build in state-law compliance at the screening stage.
- Limits platform customers' exposure to detailed reports.
**Cons**:
- Requires clear allocation of FCRA duties and coordination on adverse-action notices.
- May create confusion about who is the "user" and who bears liability.
**Key requirements**:
- Clear contractual allocation of FCRA duties.
- Mosaic Relay handles screening-stage FCRA compliance.
- Platform customer handles final-decision-stage FCRA compliance if it takes adverse action based on additional information.
### Path 4: Non-FCRA data provider (limited scope)
**Structure**: Use a vendor that provides only sanctions screening and identity verification (not criminal-history data), which are generally not "consumer reports" under FCRA. Criminal-history screening is handled separately or not at all.
**Pros**:
- Avoids FCRA obligations for sanctions and identity checks.
- Simplifies compliance.
**Cons**:
- Does not achieve the business goal of criminal-background screening.
- May still implicate state ban-the-box laws if criminal history is used.
**Key requirements**:
- Ensure the vendor is not a CRA and does not provide "consumer reports."
- Separate sanctions/identity checks from criminal-history checks.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Contractor locations**: If contractors are located in states or localities with strict ban-the-box or fair-chance laws (e.g., California, New York, New York City, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Los Angeles), the feature must comply with timing restrictions, individualized-assessment requirements, and specific notice obligations. If contractors are in states with fewer restrictions (e.g., Texas, Florida), compliance is simpler.
2. **Vendor status**: If the vendor is a CRA, full FCRA compliance is required. If the vendor provides only non-FCRA data (e.g., sanctions lists, identity verification), FCRA may not apply, but state laws may still restrict use of criminal history.
3. **Report contents**: If the report includes only sanctions and identity data (no criminal history), FCRA may not apply. If it includes criminal history, FCRA and state ban-the-box laws apply. If it includes arrests not resulting in conviction or expunged records, many states prohibit their use.
4. **Decision rules**: If the decision rules automatically disqualify contractors based on any criminal history (a "bright-line" rule), this may violate state individualized-assessment requirements. If the rules consider the nature, gravity, and age of the offense and its relationship to the role, compliance is easier.
5. **Who is the "user"**: If Mosaic Relay is the "user," it bears FCRA obligations. If the platform customer is the "user," the platform customer bears FCRA obligations. If both are "users," both bear obligations.
6. **Payout-restriction criteria**: If payout restrictions are based solely on the screening result, FCRA adverse-action requirements apply. If they are based on additional factors (e.g., fraud events, dispute history), the analysis is more complex.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, counsel should:
1. **Identify contractor jurisdictions**: Determine which states and localities the screened contractors will be located in, and research the specific ban-the-box, fair-chance, and consumer-reporting laws in those jurisdictions. Key jurisdictions to check: California, New York, New York City, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Philadelphia, Illinois, Massachusetts, Washington, and any other states/localities where contractors are concentrated.
2. **Confirm vendor status**: Verify whether the background-screening vendor is a CRA under FCRA. Review the vendor's contract, certifications, and compliance procedures. If the vendor is a CRA, confirm it complies with FCRA accuracy and dispute requirements.
3. **Review report contents and decision rules**: Confirm what information the report will contain (criminal history, sanctions, identity, address) and how the decision rules map report contents to "eligible," "manual review," or "not eligible." Ensure the decision rules comply with state individualized-assessment requirements and do not use prohibited records (arrests not resulting in conviction, expunged records, convictions beyond lookback periods).
4. **Allocate FCRA duties**: Determine whether Mosaic Relay, the platform customer, or both will be the "user" of the consumer report. Draft clear contractual language allocating FCRA disclosure, authorization, adverse-action, and dispute-handling duties.
5. **Draft FCRA disclosures and notices**: Prepare the standalone FCRA disclosure, authorization form, pre-adverse-action notice (with report copy and CFPB Summary of Rights), and adverse-action notice. Ensure these comply with FCRA and any state-specific notice requirements.
6. **Implement state-law compliance**: Build state-by-state decision rules and notices into the feature. For example, if contractors are in California, ensure the feature complies with California's ban-the-box law (Labor Code § 432.9), ICRAA, and Los Angeles/San Francisco fair-chance ordinances.
7. **Review payout-restriction criteria**: Ensure the platform's terms of service clearly disclose that payouts may be restricted based on screening results, and that the criteria comply with state law.
8. **Test adverse-action and dispute procedures**: Ensure the feature includes workflows for pre-adverse-action notices, adverse-action notices, and contractor disputes, and that these workflows comply with FCRA and state law.
