# OFAC blocking and reporting obligations for a payment facilitator when

## Question

OFAC blocking and reporting obligations for a payment facilitator when a merchant is a confirmed sanctions match

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment facilitator (not a bank), wants a standard process to deactivate merchants whose activity raises sanctions, fraud, chargeback, consumer-harm, or money-laundering concerns. The proposed flow would temporarily restrict new payments or payouts, preserve records, contact the merchant when legally and operationally appropriate, and either restore access or terminate. Counsel is involved because the process must distinguish between mandatory OFAC blocking obligations (for confirmed sanctions matches) and discretionary risk-based deactivation, each with different legal bases, notice rules, and funds-handling consequences. A restriction may need to occur within minutes of an alert, while investigation and final disposition may take several days.
## Decision question
When Mosaic Relay's monitoring confirms a merchant is a sanctions match, what specific steps must the deactivation process include to satisfy OFAC blocking and reporting obligations, and how do those mandatory steps differ from the discretionary suspension-and-termination path for fraud, chargeback, or AML risk signals?
## Open questions
- What do Mosaic Relay's merchant agreements actually say about suspension, termination, and funds-holding rights? (Not yet reviewed.)
- Which jurisdictions must the deactivation process cover — US only, or also UK/EU and other markets where Mosaic Relay serves customers?
- Does Mosaic Relay operate as a payment facilitator under partner bank licenses, or does it hold its own money-transmitter licenses? (Assumed payfac under partner licenses; not confirmed.)
- Are there acquiring-bank or processor contractual obligations that impose additional notice, timing, or funds-handling requirements beyond OFAC minimums?
- What is the current volume and nature of consumer orders in progress at the point a restriction would be triggered?

## Likely rules and issues
### 1. OFAC blocking obligations for a confirmed sanctions match
**No external authority retrieved.**
The analysis below is model-generated based on general knowledge of US sanctions law. It should be verified against current OFAC regulations and guidance before reliance.
**Who is covered.** OFAC regulations apply to "US persons," which includes any entity organized under US law. Mosaic Relay, as a US-headquartered company, is a US person subject to OFAC sanctions programs regardless of whether it is a bank, a money transmitter, or a payment facilitator. The obligation attaches to the entity, not to a license type.
**Blocking vs. rejecting.** When a US person comes into possession or control of property (including funds) in which a blocked person has an interest, the obligation is generally to *block* (freeze) those funds — not to reject or return them. This is a critical distinction:
- **Blocking** means the funds must be placed in a blocked, interest-bearing account and cannot be released, transferred, or dealt with in any way without OFAC authorization (a license). The funds remain the property of the blocked person but cannot be accessed.
- **Rejecting** applies when a transaction is merely prohibited but the party is not a blocked person — in that case the funds may be returned to the sender.
For a confirmed SDN (Specially Designated Nationals) match, Mosaic Relay would generally be required to block any funds in its possession or control in which the SDN has an interest. This includes pending payouts, reserve balances, and potentially funds in transit if Mosaic Relay has control over them.
**Reporting obligations.** OFAC's Reporting, Procedures and Penalties Regulations (31 C.F.R. Part 501) require:
- **Initial blocked-property report** — filed within 10 business days of the blocking action, identifying the blocked property, the blocked person, the date of blocking, and related information.
- **Annual report of blocked property** — filed by September 30 each year, reporting all blocked property held as of June 30.
- **Rejected-transaction reports** — if transactions are rejected rather than blocked, a report must be filed within 10 business days.
These reports are filed through OFAC's online reporting system (or by the method OFAC specifies at the time).
**No notice to the merchant before blocking.** OFAC regulations do not require advance notice to the blocked person before blocking funds. In fact, providing advance notice could constitute a violation if it facilitates the blocked person's evasion of sanctions. The process should be designed so that blocking occurs immediately upon confirmation, without tipping off the merchant.
**Post-blocking communication.** After blocking, Mosaic Relay may inform the merchant that funds have been blocked pursuant to OFAC regulations. However, care must be taken not to provide information that could constitute prohibited facilitation or that conflicts with any ongoing law-enforcement investigation. OFAC's SDN listing is public, so informing the merchant of the match itself is generally permissible, but the timing and content of that communication should be coordinated with legal counsel.
**Strict liability.** OFAC sanctions operate on a strict-liability basis for civil penalties. Good faith, reasonable screening procedures, and prompt voluntary self-disclosure are mitigating factors but do not eliminate liability. This means the deactivation process must be designed to catch and block confirmed matches reliably — a process failure that allows a blocked person's funds to move is a potential violation regardless of intent.
### 2. Distinction between confirmed sanctions matches and discretionary risk signals
This is the most important structural design point for the deactivation process:
| Element | Confirmed sanctions match (SDN) | Discretionary risk signal (fraud, chargeback, AML) |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal basis** | Federal law (OFAC regulations) — mandatory | Contractual rights under merchant agreement — discretionary |
| **Timing** | Immediate blocking required upon confirmation | Business judgment; may allow brief investigation before restriction |
| **Funds handling** | Block (freeze) — cannot return or release without OFAC license | Hold, return, or release per contract terms and applicable state money-transmission law |
| **Notice to merchant** | No advance notice; post-blocking notice permissible but coordinate with counsel | Contract may require notice; tipping-off rules may restrict disclosure if SAR-related |
| **Reporting** | OFAC blocked-property reports (10 business days; annual) | SAR filing if suspicious activity identified (FinCEN); no OFAC report unless sanctions nexus |
| **Appeal / recourse** | Merchant may apply to OFAC for a specific license or seek removal from SDN list | Contractual dispute resolution; potential wrongful-termination claims |
| **Reversibility** | Only with OFAC authorization | Business decision; can restore access if risk is resolved |
The process must not conflate these two tracks. A merchant who is a confirmed SDN match cannot simply be "deactivated" and have funds returned — that would be an OFAC violation. Conversely, a merchant deactivated for high chargebacks should not have funds treated as blocked under OFAC rules.
### 3. SAR confidentiality and tipping-off
If the deactivation is triggered by or results in the identification of suspicious activity, Mosaic Relay (or its partner bank, depending on the BSA/AML program structure) may need to file a Suspicious Activity Report. Under 31 U.S.C. § 5318(g)(2) and implementing regulations:
- A financial institution and its directors, officers, employees, and agents are prohibited from disclosing to any person involved in the transaction that a SAR has been or will be filed.
- This "tipping-off" prohibition means that if the merchant contacts Mosaic Relay asking why they were deactivated, and the reason relates to a SAR filing, customer support and compliance staff must not confirm or deny the existence of a SAR.
- The prohibition does not prevent Mosaic Relay from informing the merchant that their account has been terminated or restricted — it prevents disclosure of the SAR itself.
Whether Mosaic Relay itself is a "financial institution" with a direct SAR-filing obligation depends on its regulatory status (money transmitter vs. payment facilitator operating under a partner bank's program). This is an open assumption. If Mosaic Relay operates under a partner bank's BSA/AML program, the SAR-filing obligation may rest with the partner bank, but Mosaic Relay would still need internal escalation procedures to refer suspicious activity to the partner.
### 4. Contractual basis for discretionary deactivation
For non-sanctions deactivations (fraud, chargeback, consumer-harm, AML risk), the legal basis is primarily contractual. Key provisions to verify in Mosaic Relay's merchant agreements:
- **Suspension rights** — Does the agreement permit temporary suspension of processing or payouts, and under what triggers?
- **Termination rights** — Can Mosaic Relay terminate for cause (fraud, illegal activity, breach) and/or for convenience with notice?
- **Reserve and hold rights** — Can Mosaic Relay hold funds in reserve, delay payouts, or set off amounts owed?
- **Notice requirements** — What notice must be given before or after suspension or termination?
- **Dispute resolution** — Is there an appeal or escalation process for the merchant?
If the merchant agreement does not clearly grant these rights, Mosaic Relay may face breach-of-contract or wrongful-termination claims when it deactivates a merchant. This is a material gap that must be resolved before the process is finalized.
### 5. Funds handling for non-sanctions deactivations
For discretionary deactivations, funds handling is governed by:
- **Contract terms** — What does the merchant agreement say about pending settlements, reserves, and payout timing?
- **State money-transmission law** — If Mosaic Relay holds funds before transmitting them to the merchant, state money-transmitter statutes may impose permissible-investment, timing, and safeguarding requirements. The specific obligations depend on whether Mosaic Relay is licensed directly or operates under partner licenses.
- **Consumer-protection considerations** — If consumers have paid for goods or services that have not been delivered, Mosaic Relay may face chargeback exposure. Holding funds to cover potential chargebacks is common practice but must be consistent with contract terms.
- **Card-network rules** — Visa and Mastercard rules impose obligations on payment facilitators regarding merchant termination, including reporting terminated merchants to network databases (e.g., Mastercard MATCH system). Failure to report can result in network fines.
### 6. Consistency and wrongful-closure risk
The business goal of reducing wrongful closures and avoiding inconsistent treatment is a process-design issue with legal implications:
- **Arbitrary or inconsistent treatment** across merchants could support claims of breach of contract, tortious interference, or (in some jurisdictions) unfair business practices.
- **Documented criteria and escalation** — The process should define clear triggers, decision authority, and escalation paths. Material exceptions should receive human review (consistent with Mosaic Relay's stated risk posture).
- **Record-keeping** — Each deactivation decision should be documented with the trigger, evidence reviewed, decision-maker, rationale, and timeline. This supports both regulatory examinations and defense against merchant claims.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Dual-track process (recommended structure)
Design the deactivation process with two clearly separated tracks:
1. **Sanctions track (mandatory)** — Triggered by a confirmed SDN or other sanctions-list match. Immediate blocking of funds. OFAC reporting within required timelines. No advance notice to merchant. Post-blocking communication coordinated with legal. Funds cannot be released without OFAC authorization. Merchant recourse is through OFAC licensing or SDN-removal petition.
2. **Risk track (discretionary)** — Triggered by fraud, chargeback, consumer-harm, or AML risk signals that do not involve a confirmed sanctions match. Temporary restriction per contractual rights. Investigation and evidence-gathering. Decision to restore, restrict further, or terminate. Notice per contract terms. Funds handled per contract and applicable law. SAR filing if warranted (with tipping-off protections). Merchant has contractual recourse.
**Advantage:** Clear separation prevents the most dangerous error — treating blocked funds as returnable, or treating a discretionary hold as an OFAC block.
**Risk:** Requires reliable screening to correctly classify triggers. A false positive on the sanctions track creates unnecessary blocking; a false negative creates OFAC exposure.
### Path B: Single-track process with sanctions override
A single deactivation workflow with a mandatory sanctions-check gate. If a sanctions match is confirmed at any point, the process switches to OFAC blocking mode. Otherwise, the discretionary track proceeds.
**Advantage:** Simpler to operate; one workflow for the monitoring team.
**Risk:** The override must be reliable and immediate. If the sanctions check is delayed or the override fails, funds could be released to a blocked person. Also, the single-track design may blur the distinction in staff training and documentation, increasing the risk of tipping-off violations or inconsistent funds handling.
### Path C: Outsource sanctions blocking to partner bank
If Mosaic Relay operates under a partner bank's licenses and the partner bank controls the settlement accounts, the partner bank may already have OFAC blocking obligations and procedures. Mosaic Relay could rely on the partner bank to block funds at the settlement level, while Mosaic Relay handles the merchant-relationship and platform-access aspects.
**Advantage:** Leverages the partner bank's existing OFAC compliance infrastructure.
**Risk:** Mosaic Relay remains a US person with independent OFAC obligations. If Mosaic Relay has possession or control of funds at any point, it cannot fully delegate the blocking obligation. The division of responsibility must be clearly documented in the partner agreement.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Merchant agreement terms.** If the agreement lacks clear suspension, termination, and funds-holding rights, the discretionary track loses its contractual foundation and Mosaic Relay faces higher wrongful-termination exposure.
- **Regulatory status.** If Mosaic Relay holds its own money-transmitter licenses (rather than operating as a payfac under partner licenses), it has direct BSA/AML obligations including SAR filing, and state-law funds-safeguarding requirements apply directly.
- **Jurisdiction scope.** If the process must cover UK/EU merchants, UK OFSI sanctions and EU sanctions regimes impose parallel but not identical blocking and reporting obligations. The process would need to account for differences in lists, thresholds, and reporting timelines.
- **Partner bank requirements.** Acquiring banks and processors may impose contractual obligations (e.g., specific notice periods, MATCH reporting, reserve requirements) that constrain the deactivation process beyond what OFAC and contract law require.
- **Volume of consumer orders in progress.** If a typical deactivation involves significant undelivered consumer orders, the funds-holding period and reserve calculation become more consequential, and consumer-protection risk increases.
