# SAR tipping-off and suspicious-activity confidentiality rules for MSBs when noti

## Question

SAR tipping-off and suspicious-activity confidentiality rules for MSBs when notifying a customer of account restriction

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure company, is designing a standard process to deactivate merchants whose activity raises sanctions, fraud, chargeback, consumer-harm, or money-laundering concerns. The proposed flow would temporarily restrict new payments or payouts, preserve records, contact the merchant when legally and operationally appropriate, and either restore access or terminate the relationship. Counsel is involved because the process must move within minutes of an alert while avoiding wrongful closures, inconsistent treatment, and violations of suspicious-activity confidentiality or sanctions obligations. The specific research question here is how SAR tipping-off and suspicious-activity confidentiality rules constrain what Mosaic Relay can tell a merchant when restricting their account.
## Decision question
When Mosaic Relay restricts or terminates a merchant based on suspected money laundering or other suspicious activity that may require a SAR filing, what can the monitoring and support teams lawfully disclose to the merchant about the reason for the restriction, and what internal controls must exist to prevent tipping-off violations while still providing enough notice to satisfy contractual and consumer-protection obligations?
## Open questions
- Do Mosaic Relay's merchant agreements include contractual suspension and termination rights that allow restriction without disclosing the specific compliance reason? (Not yet reviewed.)
- Is Mosaic Relay operating as a payment facilitator under partner licenses or as a licensed money transmitter with its own BSA/AML obligations? This affects which entity bears the SAR filing and tipping-off liability.
- Which jurisdictions must the deactivation process cover? UK and EU tipping-off rules differ materially from US rules.
- Does Mosaic Relay file SARs directly, or does it rely on its acquiring bank or processor partners to file? This determines who holds the confidentiality obligation and who can be liable for disclosure.
- What is the current practice for handling pending funds and consumer orders in progress when a restriction is imposed?

### US federal framework: SAR confidentiality and tipping off
**SAR confidentiality (31 U.S.C. § 5318(g)(2); 31 C.F.R. § 1020.320(e))**
If Mosaic Relay is a money services business (MSB) or operates under a program where it files SARs (or where its partner bank files SARs based on Mosaic Relay's monitoring), the core rule is that a SAR and any information that would reveal the existence of a SAR are confidential. This means:
- Mosaic Relay cannot tell the merchant that a SAR has been filed or is being considered.
- Mosaic Relay cannot tell the merchant that their activity was reported to FinCEN or any law enforcement agency.
- The prohibition extends to any statement that would indirectly reveal the SAR's existence — for example, "we have reported your activity to the government" or "your account has been flagged in a regulatory filing."
**What can be said**
The merchant can generally be told:
- That their account has been restricted or terminated.
- That the action was taken pursuant to Mosaic Relay's terms of service or merchant agreement.
- General categories of concern (e.g., "activity inconsistent with our acceptable use policy" or "transactions that present elevated risk") — but only if this does not reveal the existence of a SAR.
- Information about the process for appeal or resolution, if one exists.
The critical line is between explaining the business decision (permissible) and revealing the regulatory filing or investigation (prohibited).
**Safe harbor (31 U.S.C. § 5318(g)(3))**
Financial institutions and their directors, officers, and employees have a safe harbor from liability for disclosures made in a SAR or for the underlying suspicious activity reported. This safe harbor protects the filer from claims by the reported customer (e.g., defamation, breach of contract) arising from the SAR itself. It does not protect against tipping-off liability.
**Who is covered**
If Mosaic Relay is an MSB, it is directly subject to SAR filing and confidentiality obligations. If it operates as a payment facilitator under a partner bank's program, the bank may be the SAR filer, but Mosaic Relay's employees could still violate tipping-off rules if they disclose the existence of a SAR filed by the bank or reveal that the bank's compliance team flagged the activity. The practical answer is that Mosaic Relay should treat SAR confidentiality as binding on its own staff regardless of which entity files.
### UK framework (if applicable)
If Mosaic Relay serves UK merchants, the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 (POCA) §§ 333A–333D creates a criminal offense of tipping off. A person commits an offense if they disclose that a suspicious activity report (SAR in the UK context, filed with the NCA) has been made or that an investigation is being carried out or contemplated, and that disclosure is likely to prejudice the investigation. The UK offense is broader than the US rule in some respects — it covers disclosures about investigations, not just the SAR itself. Penalties include imprisonment.
The UK also has a "failure to disclose" offense (POCA § 330) for persons in the regulated sector who fail to report knowledge or suspicion of money laundering. This creates a tension: Mosaic Relay must report internally and to the NCA, but cannot tell the merchant why.
### EU framework (if applicable)
The EU Anti-Money Laundering Directives (currently 6AMLD, with the AML Regulation and 7AMLD in progress) impose similar tipping-off prohibitions. Article 39 of 4AMLD (as amended) prohibits disclosure to the customer or third parties that a SAR has been filed or that an analysis or investigation is being carried out. Member state implementations vary in scope and penalty.
### Interaction with contractual notice obligations
The merchant agreement likely requires some form of notice before or after suspension or termination. The tension is:
- The contract may require Mosaic Relay to state the reason for termination.
- SAR confidentiality may prohibit stating the true reason if it would reveal a SAR.
The standard resolution is to draft the merchant agreement to allow suspension and termination for "violation of this Agreement," "activity presenting unacceptable risk," or "as required by law or regulatory obligation" — categories broad enough to provide contractual notice without revealing a SAR. If the agreement requires specific reasons, Mosaic Relay should amend it.
### Interaction with consumer-protection and funds-handling obligations
When a merchant is restricted, there may be consumer orders in progress, pending payouts, or funds held in transit. The key issues:
- **Pending consumer transactions:** If Mosaic Relay holds consumer funds pending settlement, state money transmission laws and card network rules may require that those funds be returned to consumers or settled to the merchant within a specified period, even if the merchant is deactivated. The restriction process must include a funds-disposition step.
- **Chargebacks and refunds:** If the merchant is terminated, consumers may still initiate chargebacks. Mosaic Relay (or its acquiring partner) remains exposed. The process should address reserve requirements or holdbacks to cover post-termination chargebacks.
- **Sanctions blocking:** If the trigger is a confirmed OFAC sanctions match, the analysis changes entirely. OFAC blocking obligations are strict liability — Mosaic Relay must block the funds and file a blocked-property report within 10 business days. There is no discretion. The merchant can be told the account is blocked due to a sanctions match (OFAC does not prohibit this disclosure in the same way SAR rules do), but the funds cannot be released without an OFAC license.
### Wrongful closure and inconsistent treatment
The business goal of reducing wrongful closures and avoiding inconsistent treatment creates a separate set of risks:
- **Breach of contract:** If the merchant agreement does not clearly authorize the restriction, Mosaic Relay could face breach claims.
- **Discrimination or fair-lending analogues:** While merchants are not consumers, inconsistent application of deactivation criteria could create reputational and regulatory risk, particularly if the criteria disproportionately affect certain categories of businesses.
- **Defamation or tortious interference:** If Mosaic Relay communicates the reason for deactivation to third parties (e.g., acquiring partners, other platforms) in a way that is inaccurate or misleading, the merchant could assert claims. The SAR safe harbor protects SAR-related disclosures to the government, not disclosures to business partners.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Layered notice with SAR-safe language
Design the merchant-facing communication to use pre-approved, SAR-safe language that satisfies contractual notice requirements without revealing the existence of a SAR or investigation. The merchant agreement should authorize suspension and termination for broad categories ("violation of Agreement," "unacceptable risk," "regulatory requirement"). Customer support and monitoring teams use scripted responses. Any deviation requires compliance or legal approval.
**Advantages:** Fast, scalable, consistent. Reduces tipping-off risk.
**Limitations:** Merchants may be frustrated by vague explanations. Requires careful drafting of the merchant agreement and training of support staff.
### Path 2: Dual-track process (sanctions vs. discretionary)
Separate the deactivation process into two tracks:
- **Track A (sanctions match):** Mandatory blocking under OFAC. No discretion. Funds blocked. Blocked-property report filed. Merchant notified of the sanctions basis (permissible under OFAC rules). No SAR tipping-off issue because the disclosure is about the sanctions match, not a SAR.
- **Track B (discretionary risk):** Fraud, chargeback, consumer-harm, or AML concerns that do not rise to a confirmed sanctions match. Restriction is discretionary. SAR may be filed. Merchant notified using SAR-safe language. Funds handled per contract and applicable money transmission rules.
**Advantages:** Clear separation of mandatory and discretionary actions. Reduces the risk of conflating OFAC blocking (where disclosure is permissible) with SAR-related restrictions (where it is not).
**Limitations:** Requires clear internal criteria for which track applies. Edge cases (e.g., a sanctions match that is later determined to be a false positive) need a defined resolution path.
### Path 3: Tiered restriction with escalation
Instead of immediate full deactivation, impose a tiered restriction:
- **Level 1:** Hold new payouts, allow pending transactions to settle.
- **Level 2:** Hold new payments and payouts, allow refunds and chargebacks to process.
- **Level 3:** Full deactivation, funds held pending investigation or legal requirement.
Each level has a defined set of merchant-facing communications, with SAR-safe language at every level. Escalation from Level 1 to Level 3 is based on investigation findings.
**Advantages:** Reduces wrongful closures by allowing partial restrictions. Gives the investigation team time to reach a disposition. Provides a defensible record of proportionate response.
**Limitations:** More complex to administer. May not be fast enough for confirmed sanctions matches (which require immediate blocking).

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Whether Mosaic Relay files SARs directly or relies on partners.** If Mosaic Relay is not the SAR filer, the tipping-off analysis shifts to whether Mosaic Relay's staff could disclose the partner's SAR. The practical controls are similar, but the legal obligation and liability allocation differ.
- **The specific language of the merchant agreement's suspension and termination clauses.** If the agreement requires detailed reasons, the SAR-safe language approach may not satisfy contractual notice requirements without amendment.
- **Whether any merchants are consumers or sole proprietors.** If so, state consumer-protection laws and possibly EFTA/Regulation E could impose additional notice and funds-availability requirements.
- **Whether law enforcement has contacted Mosaic Relay about any specific merchant.** If so, additional confidentiality obligations (e.g., grand jury subpoena, NSL, or 314(b) information sharing) may apply and further restrict what can be disclosed.
- **The jurisdictions covered.** If UK or EU merchants are in scope, POCA and AMLD tipping-off rules apply and may be broader than US rules.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — SAR filing entity.** This analysis assumes Mosaic Relay either files SARs directly as an MSB or is operationally involved in the SAR process such that its staff could tip off a merchant. If Mosaic Relay has no SAR filing obligation and no visibility into partner SARs, the tipping-off risk is lower but not eliminated (staff could still disclose that a partner flagged the activity). If this assumption is wrong, the analysis simplifies but the practical controls remain advisable.
- **Working assumption — merchant agreement terms.** The analysis assumes the merchant agreement contains or can be amended to include broad suspension and termination rights. If the agreement requires specific reasons for termination and cannot be amended unilaterally, the SAR-safe language approach may not work for existing merchants, and Mosaic Relay may need to choose between contractual breach and tipping-off risk.
- **Open fork — jurisdictional scope.** If the process covers only US merchants, the analysis is governed by FinCEN SAR rules and OFAC. If UK or EU merchants are in scope, POCA and AMLD tipping-off rules add criminal exposure and may require different merchant-facing language. The two paths lead to materially different communication templates and training requirements.
- **Open fork — funds handling on restriction.** If Mosaic Relay holds consumer funds in transit at the time of restriction, state money transmission laws and card network rules may require those funds to be settled or returned regardless of the merchant's status. If Mosaic Relay does not hold funds (pure pass-through), the funds-handling analysis is simpler. This fork determines whether a funds-disposition step is legally required or merely best practice.
- **Not examined — specific state money transmission statutes.** The analysis references state money transmission laws generally but does not examine any specific state's requirements for notice, funds return, or license conditions when a merchant is deactivated. If Mosaic Relay holds state licenses, each state's statute and any license conditions should be reviewed.
- **Not examined — card network rules on merchant termination.** Visa and Mastercard have specific rules on merchant termination, MATCH listing, and chargeback liability post-termination. These were not reviewed and could impose additional obligations or timelines.
