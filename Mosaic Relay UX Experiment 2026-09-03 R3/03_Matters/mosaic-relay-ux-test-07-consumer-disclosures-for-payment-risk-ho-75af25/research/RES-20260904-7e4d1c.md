# What payment-facilitator and money-transmission rules govern holding funds pendi

## Question

What payment-facilitator and money-transmission rules govern holding funds pending risk review in the US?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure provider, plans to implement a risk-control feature that automatically delays merchant payouts when transaction-risk scoring identifies unusual activity, high dispute risk, or incomplete verification. The holds could begin within minutes and last from hours to weeks, with merchants seeing a "review required" status and submitting documents through a secure portal. Counsel is involved because the feature implicates money transmission regulations, consumer protection laws, and contractual authority questions that could expose Mosaic Relay to regulatory enforcement or private litigation if mishandled. The key timing issue is that holds could extend for multiple weeks, creating potential harm to merchants and their underlying sellers or consumers who depend on timely payouts.
## Decision question
Should Mosaic Relay launch an automated payout hold feature that delays merchant funds for up to several weeks based on risk-scoring triggers, and if so, what contractual authority, disclosure language, timing limits, and procedural safeguards must be in place to avoid money transmission violations, unfair or deceptive practices claims, and breach of contract exposure?
## Open questions
- Does Mosaic Relay's current merchant agreement grant explicit authority to delay payouts for risk review, and does it specify notice, timing, appeal rights, or fee/interest treatment during holds?
- Whose funds are being held—the merchant's own proceeds, or funds belonging to underlying sellers or consumers paid through the merchant—and does Mosaic Relay have custody or control over those funds in a way that triggers money transmitter licensing?
- Will holds be triggered automatically by the risk-scoring model without human review, or will a human analyst confirm before a hold is placed, and what review standards apply?
- What happens if a merchant cannot provide requested documents—are funds released after a maximum period, forfeited, or subject to escalation—and do interest or fees accrue during the hold?
- Which states' money transmission laws apply based on where merchants and their customers are located, and do any state automated-decision or consumer-protection laws impose additional disclosure or fairness requirements?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on model knowledge and the matter facts, the following legal frameworks are likely implicated:
### 1. Money transmission and payment facilitator rules
**Core issue:** Whether Mosaic Relay's hold feature constitutes "receiving money for transmission" or "holding funds" in a way that requires state money transmitter licenses or triggers federal money services business (MSB) registration.
- **State money transmission laws:** Most states define money transmission as receiving money from one person and transmitting it to another, or holding funds incident to transmission. If Mosaic Relay takes custody or control of funds belonging to merchants or their customers—even temporarily—it may need licenses in states where it operates. The key question is whether Mosaic Relay is acting as a payment facilitator (agent of the merchant) or as a principal holding funds.
- **Federal MSB registration:** Under FinCEN rules, a business that accepts and transmits funds may need to register as an MSB and comply with BSA/AML requirements. Mosaic Relay's company profile indicates it is not a bank and does not hold deposits, but the hold feature could blur that line if funds are delayed for weeks.
- **Payment facilitator / marketplace exception:** Some states have exceptions or lighter regimes for payment facilitators that act as agents of merchants under card network rules. However, these exceptions typically require that funds flow directly from the acquirer to the merchant without the facilitator taking custody. A multi-week hold could undermine that structure.
**Practical risk:** If Mosaic Relay is holding funds without proper licenses, it could face cease-and-desist orders, fines, and requirements to obtain licenses retroactively. If it is operating under a payment facilitator model, the hold feature could violate card network rules or acquirer agreements.
### 2. Unfair or deceptive acts or practices (UDAP) — FTC Act Section 5 and state analogs
**Core issue:** Whether the hold feature, as designed, is unfair or deceptive to merchants or consumers.
- **Deception:** If Mosaic Relay's marketing or onboarding materials promise fast payouts and do not clearly disclose the possibility of multi-week holds, the feature could be deceptive. The "review required" status and "general explanation" may be insufficient if they do not give merchants a realistic understanding of timing, criteria, or appeal rights.
- **Unfairness:** A practice is unfair if it causes substantial injury that is not reasonably avoidable and not outweighed by benefits. Multi-week holds on funds that merchants or their sellers depend on for payroll or operations could cause substantial injury. If holds are triggered automatically without human review, or if merchants have no meaningful way to appeal or expedite review, the practice could be unfair.
- **State UDAP laws:** Many states have their own UDAP statutes that may impose additional disclosure or fairness requirements, and some allow private rights of action.
**Practical risk:** FTC or state attorney general enforcement, private class actions, and reputational harm. The risk is higher if holds disproportionately affect small businesses or if Mosaic Relay does not provide clear, timely, and accurate information.
### 3. Automated decision-making and algorithmic fairness
**Core issue:** Whether the use of automated risk scoring to trigger holds without human review violates emerging state laws or general fairness principles.
- **State automated decision laws:** A growing number of states (e.g., California, Colorado, Illinois) have enacted or proposed laws regulating automated decision systems that affect consumers or workers. While most current laws focus on housing, employment, or credit, some are broad enough to cover financial services or could be extended to merchant services.
- **FTC guidance on AI and algorithms:** The FTC has signaled that it will use Section 5 to challenge biased or unexplainable algorithmic decisions that cause harm. If the risk-scoring model disproportionately flags certain types of merchants (e.g., by geography, industry, or demographics) without a legitimate risk basis, it could be challenged as unfair.
- **Explainability and appeal:** Even if no specific law applies, best practice and general fairness principles suggest that merchants should receive a meaningful explanation of why a hold was placed and have a way to appeal or provide additional information.
**Practical risk:** Regulatory scrutiny, litigation, and reputational harm if the model is perceived as biased or arbitrary. The risk is higher if holds are fully automated and merchants have no human contact.
### 4. Contract law and funds ownership
**Core issue:** Whether Mosaic Relay has contractual authority to hold funds, and whether the funds belong to the merchant or to underlying sellers/consumers.
- **Contractual authority:** If the merchant agreement does not explicitly authorize holds, or if it promises payouts within a certain timeframe, Mosaic Relay could be in breach of contract. The agreement should clearly state the circumstances under which holds may be placed, the maximum duration, the review process, and any appeal rights.
- **Funds ownership:** If the funds belong to underlying sellers or consumers (e.g., in a marketplace model where the merchant is an intermediary), holding those funds could create additional legal exposure. Mosaic Relay could be seen as holding funds in trust or as a fiduciary, which could trigger additional regulatory obligations or fiduciary duties.
- **Bailment or constructive trust:** If Mosaic Relay holds funds that belong to third parties, a court could impose a constructive trust or find that Mosaic Relay is a bailee, which could limit its ability to use or invest the funds and create liability if the funds are lost or misused.
**Practical risk:** Breach of contract claims, unjust enrichment claims, and regulatory action if Mosaic Relay is holding third-party funds without proper authority or safeguards.
### 5. Interest, fees, and escheatment
**Core issue:** Whether interest or fees accrue during the hold, and what happens to funds if a merchant cannot provide documents or abandons the account.
- **Interest:** If Mosaic Relay holds funds for an extended period, merchants may argue that they are entitled to interest, especially if Mosaic Relay is earning interest on the funds. The merchant agreement should address this explicitly.
- **Fees:** If Mosaic Relay charges fees during the hold (e.g., for document review or account maintenance), this could be seen as unfair or as a penalty, especially if the hold was triggered by Mosaic Relay's own risk model.
- **Escheatment:** If a merchant cannot be located or does not respond to document requests, the funds may eventually be subject to state unclaimed property laws, which require businesses to turn over abandoned funds to the state after a dormancy period (typically 3–5 years).
**Practical risk:** Disputes over interest and fees, and compliance obligations under unclaimed property laws.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Launch with strong contractual authority and procedural safeguards
**What it looks like:**
- Amend the merchant agreement to explicitly authorize risk-based holds, specify the triggers (unusual activity, high dispute risk, incomplete verification), state the maximum duration (e.g., 30 days), and describe the review and appeal process.
- Provide clear, conspicuous disclosure at onboarding and in the merchant dashboard about the possibility of holds, the typical duration, and the merchant's rights.
- Implement a human review step before any hold is placed (or at least before it extends beyond a short initial period, e.g., 48 hours).
- Create a clear escalation path and appeal process, with a dedicated contact and a commitment to resolve reviews within a stated timeframe.
- Do not charge fees during the hold, and consider paying interest if the hold extends beyond a certain period (e.g., 7 days) and the merchant is ultimately cleared.
- Maintain detailed records of each hold, including the risk-scoring rationale, the documents requested and received, the review timeline, and the final decision.
**Pros:**
- Reduces legal risk by establishing clear contractual authority and procedural fairness.
- Aligns with FTC guidance on transparency and fairness.
- Reduces the risk of money transmission violations if combined with a payment facilitator structure that avoids custody of funds.
**Cons:**
- Requires contract amendments and potentially re-papering existing merchants.
- May slow down the review process and reduce the effectiveness of the risk controls.
- May require additional staffing for human review and appeals.
### Path 2: Launch with minimal changes and rely on existing general terms
**What it looks like:**
- Rely on general language in the merchant agreement that allows Mosaic Relay to take actions to prevent fraud or comply with law.
- Provide only the "review required" status and a general explanation, without detailed disclosure or a formal appeal process.
- Allow holds to be triggered automatically without human review.
- Do not pay interest or commit to a maximum duration.
**Pros:**
- Faster to implement and requires fewer changes to existing systems and contracts.
- Maximizes flexibility for the risk team.
**Cons:**
- High legal risk: likely to be challenged as unfair or deceptive, and may violate money transmission laws if Mosaic Relay is seen as holding funds without authority.
- High reputational risk if merchants or their customers are harmed by long holds.
- Difficult to defend in litigation or regulatory enforcement.
### Path 3: Delay launch and restructure the feature to avoid holding funds
**What it looks like:**
- Redesign the feature so that funds flow directly from the acquirer to the merchant's bank account, with Mosaic Relay acting only as a technology provider and not taking custody of funds.
- Instead of holding funds, Mosaic Relay could delay the initiation of the payout instruction, so that funds never leave the acquirer's control until the review is complete.
- Alternatively, Mosaic Relay could require merchants to maintain a reserve account with the acquirer, so that funds are held by the acquirer (which is licensed) rather than by Mosaic Relay.
**Pros:**
- Avoids or reduces money transmission licensing risk.
- Aligns with the payment facilitator model and card network rules.
**Cons:**
- May require significant changes to the product architecture and relationships with acquirers.
- May not be feasible for all merchants or all types of transactions.
- Does not eliminate UDAP or contract law risk, but may reduce the severity of harm.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Contractual authority:** If the current merchant agreement already grants Mosaic Relay broad authority to delay payouts for risk review, with clear notice and appeal terms, the legal risk is lower. If the agreement is silent or promises fast payouts, the risk is much higher.
2. **Funds ownership and custody:** If the funds belong to the merchant and Mosaic Relay never takes custody (e.g., funds flow directly from the acquirer to the merchant's bank), the money transmission risk is lower. If Mosaic Relay takes custody or the funds belong to third parties, the risk is much higher.
3. **Human review:** If a human analyst reviews each hold before it is placed (or within a short period after), the fairness and UDAP risk is lower. If holds are fully automated with no human oversight, the risk is much higher.
4. **Duration and appeal:** If holds are typically resolved within a few days and merchants have a clear, fast appeal process, the harm and legal risk are lower. If holds routinely last for weeks and merchants have no meaningful recourse, the risk is much higher.
5. **State law variations:** If Mosaic Relay operates only in states with no money transmission licensing requirement for payment facilitators, or with clear exceptions, the licensing risk is lower. If it operates in states with strict licensing regimes (e.g., New York, California), the risk is much higher.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, counsel should:
1. **Review the current merchant agreement** to confirm whether it authorizes risk-based holds and what notice, timing, and appeal terms it includes. If it does not, draft and implement amendments before launch.
2. **Map the funds flow** to confirm whether Mosaic Relay takes custody or control of funds, and whether the funds belong to the merchant or to third parties. If Mosaic Relay takes custody, confirm that it has the necessary licenses or restructure the flow to avoid custody.
3. **Confirm the review process** with the product and risk teams: Will holds be triggered automatically, or will a human review each one? What are the review standards? What is the escalation and appeal process? Document the answers.
4. **Check state money transmission laws** in the states where Mosaic Relay operates to confirm whether the hold feature triggers licensing requirements. This may require outside counsel with money transmission expertise.
5. **Review card network and acquirer agreements** to confirm that the hold feature complies with their rules and does not violate any contractual obligations.
6. **Draft clear, conspicuous disclosure language** for onboarding and the merchant dashboard that explains the possibility of holds, the typical duration, the review process, and the merchant's rights. Test the language with a sample of merchants to ensure it is understandable.
7. **Implement a records retention policy** that requires detailed documentation of each hold, including the risk-scoring rationale, the documents requested and received, the review timeline, and the final decision. This will be critical for defending the feature in litigation or regulatory enforcement.
