# GDPR/UK GDPR and PECR requirements for B2B marketing email, consent

## Question

GDPR/UK GDPR and PECR requirements for B2B marketing email, consent vs. legitimate interest, and profiling restrictions

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure company, wants to send targeted product emails to merchants and platform administrators using service data (transaction volume, dispute rates, payout behavior, onboarding status, and fraud-risk indicators). The business goal is to increase feature adoption and reduce operational problems through weekly and event-triggered messages. Counsel is involved because the targeting model uses risk/fraud scores, no opt-out or preference controls exist yet, and the legal basis for using service data for marketing is undetermined. The matter is in research status with normal priority and no fixed launch date.
## Decision question
May Mosaic Relay use service data — including fraud-risk scores — to send targeted marketing emails to business contacts at merchants and platforms, and if so, under what legal basis (consent vs. legitimate interest), with what profiling, unsubscribe, suppression, and retention controls, given that recipients' jurisdictions are undetermined and no opt-out mechanism currently exists?
## Open questions
- **Which jurisdictions will recipients be in?** This determines whether GDPR/UK GDPR and PECR apply, or only US CAN-SPAM, or both — and the consent/opt-out rules differ materially between them.
- **What do Mosaic Relay's customer contracts say about using platform-customer data for Mosaic's own marketing?** If contracts restrict secondary use, Mosaic may need its own consent or a contract amendment regardless of what privacy law permits.
- **Do the fraud-risk scores used for targeting reveal or proxy special-category data (e.g., health, ethnicity, financial hardship)?** If so, GDPR Art. 9 restrictions and Art. 22 automated-decision rules are triggered more directly.
- **Are any recipients sole traders or individual partnerships (PECR "individual subscribers") rather than corporate subscribers?** PECR's B2B exemption applies only to corporate subscribers; sole traders get the same protections as consumers.
- **What retention period will apply to targeting data and suppression lists?** No policy exists; suppression lists must be retained indefinitely (or as long as the person remains opted out) to be effective, which creates a tension with data-minimization obligations.

## Likely rules and issues
### 1. Lawful basis for using service data for marketing (GDPR/UK GDPR Art. 6)
Mosaic Relay collected transaction, onboarding, identity, risk, and operations data to **provide its payment services**. Using that same data to select recipients for marketing emails is a **secondary purpose**. Under GDPR/UK GDPR Art. 6(4), Mosaic must assess whether this secondary use is **compatible** with the original collection purpose. Factors include the link between the purposes, the context of collection, the nature of the data, possible consequences for data subjects, and the existence of safeguards.
**Practical read:** Recommending dispute tools to a merchant with rising chargebacks is closely related to the service Mosaic already provides — it is arguably within the merchant's reasonable expectations. Promoting faster onboarding to businesses that have not completed verification is also service-adjacent. However, using **fraud-risk scores** — which the merchant may not know exist — to target marketing messages is further from the original purpose and carries higher risk of being found incompatible.
**Legal basis options:**
- **Legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f))** is the most realistic basis for B2B marketing to existing business contacts. Mosaic must document a **legitimate interest assessment (LIA)** balancing its commercial interest against the recipient's rights. The ICO has acknowledged that B2B marketing can rely on legitimate interest, particularly for corporate subscribers.
- **Consent (Art. 6(1)(a))** is the safer but operationally heavier path. If consent is the basis, it must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous — a pre-ticked box or buried clause in the platform terms will not suffice.
### 2. PECR rules on B2B marketing email
PECR (Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations 2003) governs unsolicited electronic marketing in the UK. The key distinction:
- **Corporate subscribers** (limited companies, LLPs, government bodies): PECR does **not** require prior consent for marketing emails. Mosaic can send B2B marketing emails to corporate email addresses without opt-in consent, provided it identifies itself and provides a valid opt-out.
- **Individual subscribers** (sole traders, some partnerships): PECR treats them like consumers. Prior consent is required unless the **soft opt-in** exception applies (email obtained during a sale or negotiation, marketing relates to similar products/services, and opt-out was offered at collection and in every message).
**Practical read:** If all recipients are employees or administrators at incorporated businesses, PECR's corporate-subscriber exemption applies and Mosaic does not need prior consent for the emails themselves. But if any recipients are sole traders — common among smaller merchants and marketplace sellers — Mosaic needs either consent or a valid soft opt-in for those individuals.
### 3. Profiling and automated-decision restrictions (GDPR/UK GDPR Arts. 4(4), 21, 22)
**Profiling** is defined in Art. 4(4) as automated processing to evaluate or predict aspects of an individual's behavior, performance, or preferences. Using transaction volume, dispute rates, and fraud-risk scores to select email recipients **is profiling**.
- **Art. 22** restricts solely automated decisions that produce **legal or similarly significant effects**. Sending a marketing email is unlikely to constitute a "significant effect" on its own. However, if the targeting model's output also feeds into account decisions (e.g., restricting payouts, flagging accounts), Art. 22 could be triggered by the broader processing chain.
- **Art. 21(2)** gives data subjects an **absolute right to object** to processing for direct marketing, including profiling to the extent it relates to direct marketing. Once someone objects, Mosaic must stop — no balancing test applies.
- **Art. 9** special-category data: Fraud-risk scores are not inherently special-category data, but if the scoring model uses or proxies health data, racial/ethnic origin, political opinions, or other Art. 9 categories, the processing requires an Art. 9 condition in addition to an Art. 6 basis. This is a material risk given that fraud models can inadvertently proxy protected characteristics.
### 4. Message classification: transactional vs. marketing
The distinction matters because **transactional/service messages** (e.g., "your payout has been processed") are generally exempt from marketing consent requirements, while **marketing messages** (e.g., "try our new dispute tool") are not.
**Risk area:** The proposed emails sit in a gray zone. "We noticed your chargebacks are rising — here's a tool that can help" is arguably both service-relevant and promotional. Regulators (ICO, FTC) look at the **primary purpose** of the message. If the primary purpose is to drive feature adoption (a commercial goal), the message is likely marketing, even if it references the recipient's account activity.
**Practical read:** Mosaic should classify these as marketing messages and apply marketing rules (opt-out, identification, lawful basis). Trying to characterize them as transactional to avoid marketing obligations is a common enforcement trigger.
### 5. Controller vs. processor allocation
Mosaic Relay processes merchant and platform data to provide payment services — in that role, it may act as a **processor** (or joint controller) for its platform customers. When Mosaic uses that same data for **its own marketing purposes**, it is acting as a **controller** for that processing.
**Key issue:** If Mosaic's customer contracts restrict secondary use of platform data, or if the platform's privacy notice does not disclose that Mosaic may contact merchants for marketing, Mosaic cannot rely on the platform's consent or notice. Mosaic needs its own lawful basis and its own transparency (privacy notice disclosure) for the marketing use.
### 6. Suppression, unsubscribe, and preference management
No opt-out or preference controls currently exist. At minimum:
- **Every marketing email must include a functioning unsubscribe mechanism** (CAN-SPAM, PECR, GDPR Art. 21).
- **Suppression lists** must be maintained so that opted-out individuals are not re-contacted. Suppression data must be retained as long as needed to honor the opt-out — which may be indefinite.
- **Preference management** (allowing recipients to choose topics or frequency) is a best practice that reduces full opt-outs and demonstrates accountability.
- **Event-triggered emails** (sent after a transaction or account review) need the same controls as batch emails if they are marketing in nature.
### 7. Retention
No retention policy exists for targeting data or suppression lists. GDPR storage-limitation principle (Art. 5(1)(e)) requires that personal data be kept no longer than necessary. Mosaic should define:
- How long targeting-segment data is retained (e.g., segment membership refreshed monthly, old segments purged).
- How long suppression-list entries are kept (indefinitely, or until the individual re-consents).
- How long engagement data (opens, clicks) is retained for marketing analytics.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Legitimate interest + corporate-subscriber exemption (fastest, moderate risk)
Rely on GDPR/UK GDPR Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interest for the data processing, and PECR's corporate-subscriber exemption for the email sending. Document a legitimate interest assessment. Build opt-out and suppression controls before launch. Restrict targeting to corporate email addresses only. Disclose the marketing use in Mosaic's privacy notice.
**Pros:** No consent flow needed; faster to launch; well-suited to B2B context.
**Cons:** Requires confidence that all recipients are corporate subscribers; legitimate interest must be documented and defensible; fraud-risk-score targeting may be harder to justify under a balancing test; does not work for sole traders.
### Path B: Soft opt-in for existing customers (safer for mixed audiences)
Rely on PECR's soft opt-in for recipients whose email was obtained during a service relationship, provided the marketing relates to similar services and opt-out was offered at collection and in every message. Combine with legitimate interest as the GDPR lawful basis.
**Pros:** Covers sole traders and individual subscribers; aligns with existing customer relationship.
**Cons:** Only works if opt-out was offered at the time the email address was collected — if it was not, soft opt-in is unavailable; requires the marketed products to be "similar" to what the customer already uses.
### Path C: Consent-based (highest compliance, slowest)
Obtain explicit opt-in consent before sending any marketing emails. This could be done through a preference center, account settings, or a one-time re-permission campaign.
**Pros:** Strongest legal position; future-proof against regulatory changes; works for all recipient types.
**Cons:** Lower reach (many recipients will not opt in); slower to implement; consent must be as easy to withdraw as to give.
### Recommended approach
**Path A for corporate subscribers, with Path B as a fallback for any sole traders, and Path C as the target state.** Build the suppression and preference infrastructure now (it is required under all paths), document the LIA, update the privacy notice, and segment out any non-corporate recipients until their status is confirmed.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **If any recipients are sole traders or unincorporated partnerships**, PECR's corporate-subscriber exemption does not apply, and consent or soft opt-in is required for those individuals.
- **If the fraud-risk scores proxy special-category data** (e.g., if the model correlates with health conditions, ethnicity, or financial vulnerability), GDPR Art. 9 is triggered and the analysis changes materially.
- **If Mosaic's customer contracts prohibit using platform data for Mosaic's own marketing**, Mosaic cannot proceed without a contract amendment or the platform's agreement, regardless of what privacy law permits.
- **If recipients are in the EU (not just the UK)**, ePrivacy Directive implementations in EU member states vary — some (e.g., Germany) require opt-in consent even for B2B marketing email, which would push toward Path C.
- **If the event-triggered emails are classified as transactional** (e.g., "your account review is complete"), they may be exempt from marketing rules — but this classification must be defensible and the primary-purpose test applied honestly.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption — All recipients are corporate subscribers.** The PECR B2B exemption and the legitimate-interest path depend on this. If even a small percentage of recipients are sole traders (common among marketplace sellers and smaller merchants), Mosaic needs consent or soft opt-in for those individuals, and the soft opt-in is only available if an opt-out was offered when the email was collected — which intake says it was not. This would push a segment of the campaign into Path C (consent required) or require excluding those recipients.
- **Open fork — Jurisdiction determines the strictness of the rules.** If recipients are UK-only, PECR's corporate-subscriber exemption and the ICO's relatively permissive B2B guidance apply. If recipients are in the EU, several member states (notably Germany) require opt-in consent even for B2B marketing email under their ePrivacy implementations, which would push the entire campaign toward Path C. If US-only, CAN-SPAM applies and requires only opt-out (no prior consent), making the analysis much simpler. The answer changes materially depending on which regime governs.
- **Open fork — Whether fraud-risk scores proxy special-category data.** If the scoring model uses or correlates with health data, ethnicity, financial vulnerability, or other Art. 9 categories, the processing requires an Art. 9 condition (which legitimate interest alone cannot satisfy for marketing), and Art. 22 automated-decision restrictions are more likely to apply. This would likely block the use of fraud-risk scores for marketing targeting entirely, or require a fundamentally different legal basis and safeguards.
- **Not examined — Mosaic Relay's customer contracts and platform terms.** The vault does not contain these documents. If they restrict secondary use of platform data for Mosaic's own marketing, or if the platform's privacy notice does not disclose this use, Mosaic may be contractually barred from the campaign regardless of what privacy law permits. This is the single most likely source of an unwelcome surprise.
- **Not examined — The ePrivacy Directive implementations in specific EU member states.** If recipients are in the EU, each member state's transposition of the ePrivacy Directive governs marketing email rules, and they vary significantly. Germany's UWG, for example, effectively requires opt-in for B2B email marketing. No member-state analysis was performed.
- **Working assumption — The event-triggered emails are marketing, not transactional.** If any of the triggered messages are genuinely transactional (e.g., "your account review is complete — no action needed"), they fall outside marketing rules. But if the primary purpose is to promote a feature or drive adoption, they are marketing regardless of the trigger. Misclassification is a common enforcement trigger and should be resolved deliberately, not by default.
