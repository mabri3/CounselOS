# Card network refund vs. reversal rules and refund windows (Visa/Mastercard)

## Question

Card network refund vs. reversal rules and refund windows (Visa/Mastercard)

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure provider, is building a self-service refund tool for merchants that would process full or partial refunds across card, ACH, and local payment methods. The tool would route funds back to original payers where possible and allow manual operations overrides for closed merchant accounts. Counsel is involved because operations has identified a risk that merchants could exploit refunds to move funds between unrelated cards or recipients, creating potential money laundering exposure. The launch timing and target jurisdictions remain undefined, and refund windows by payment method have not been confirmed with acquiring partners.
## Decision question
Should Mosaic Relay launch a self-service merchant refund tool for card, ACH, and local payment methods that defaults to original-payer routing but permits manual operations overrides for closed accounts, given that refund windows are unconfirmed, transaction monitoring coverage for refunds is unknown, and operations has flagged the risk of refunds being used to move funds between unrelated cards or recipients?
## Open questions
- What are the specific refund and return windows for each payment method (card networks, ACH, local methods) under Mosaic Relay's acquiring partner and processor agreements?
- Does Mosaic Relay's existing transaction-monitoring system cover refund and reversal flows, or does this require a new monitoring surface?
- What legal entity and licensing status does Mosaic Relay hold, and how does its funds-flow and settlement model interact with refund obligations?
- Which jurisdictions will the tool launch in, and what local payment method rules apply in each?
- What specific limits, beyond two-person approval and logging, should govern manual overrides for closed accounts?

## Likely rules and issues
### Card network refund vs. reversal rules
**No external authority retrieved.**
Based on general industry knowledge (not retrieved authority), card networks like Visa and Mastercard distinguish between several transaction types that are relevant here:
- **Authorization reversals** cancel an authorization before settlement. These are typically time-limited (often within hours or days of the original authorization) and are generally the cleanest way to undo a transaction before funds move.
- **Refunds (or credits)** are separate transactions that return funds after settlement. Card networks generally require refunds to be processed to the same card used in the original transaction. This is a core network rule designed to prevent exactly the kind of misuse Mosaic Relay's operations team has flagged.
- **Refund windows** vary by network, region, and merchant agreement. Visa and Mastercard typically allow refunds for extended periods (often 120 days or more from the original transaction), but the practical window depends on the acquiring bank's policies and the specific card product. Some acquiring partners impose shorter windows.
- **Partial refunds** are generally permitted by card networks, but each partial refund is typically a separate transaction that must reference the original transaction.
The critical compliance point is that card network rules generally prohibit refunding to a different card or account than the one used for the original purchase. This is both a network rule and an AML control. If Mosaic Relay's tool allows refunds to be routed to a different destination, even through manual override, it risks violating network rules and creating AML exposure.
### ACH return and reversal procedures
ACH transactions operate under NACHA rules (the governing body for the ACH network in the US). Key distinctions:
- **Reversals** in ACH are narrowly defined. NACHA rules permit an originator to reverse an entry only in limited circumstances: duplicate entries, erroneous entries (wrong amount, wrong account, wrong date), or entries initiated in error. Reversals must generally be initiated within five banking days of settlement.
- **Returns** are initiated by the receiving bank (RDFI) and follow specific return reason codes and timeframes. Standard returns must generally be initiated within two banking days of settlement for most reason codes, though some codes allow longer windows (e.g., unauthorized debit returns have a 60-day window from the settlement date for consumer accounts).
- **Refunds** in the ACH context are not a formal NACHA concept in the same way as card refunds. If a merchant wants to return funds to a payer after the return window has closed, the merchant would typically need to initiate a new ACH credit entry, which is a separate transaction, not a reversal or return.
This means that for ACH, the "refund" tool would actually be initiating new credit entries in many cases, which raises different compliance considerations than a card refund. The tool needs to distinguish between true reversals (within the NACHA window), returns (RDFI-initiated), and new credit entries (which are effectively new payments).
### Local payment methods
Local payment methods (e.g., SEPA in the EU, Faster Payments in the UK, Interac in Canada, Pix in Brazil) each have their own rules for reversals, returns, and refunds. These vary significantly:
- **SEPA Credit Transfers** are generally irrevocable once executed. Refunds require a new credit transfer in the opposite direction.
- **SEPA Direct Debits** have specific refund rights for consumers (e.g., 8 weeks for authorized transactions, 13 months for unauthorized transactions under the SEPA Direct Debit scheme).
- **UK Faster Payments** are generally irrevocable. Refunds require a new payment.
- **Pix (Brazil)** has specific refund mechanisms built into the system.
The tool must be designed to handle each method's specific rules rather than applying a one-size-fits-all refund model.
### AML and financial-crime indicators
The operations team's concern about refunds being used to move funds between unrelated cards or recipients is well-founded. This pattern is a recognized money laundering typology:
- **Refund fraud / refund laundering**: A merchant processes a payment from one source and then refunds to a different destination, effectively using the payment platform as a money transfer service. This is a form of layering in the money laundering process.
- **Structuring through partial refunds**: Multiple partial refunds below reporting thresholds could be used to obscure the movement of funds.
- **Refund after payout**: If a merchant has already received a payout and then processes a refund, the funds may need to come from Mosaic Relay's settlement account or the merchant's reserve, creating credit risk and potential AML exposure if the original payment was fraudulent.
- **Closed account overrides**: Manual overrides for closed accounts are particularly high-risk because the normal controls (merchant account standing, ongoing monitoring) are no longer in place.
Key AML indicators to monitor:
- Refunds to a different card or account than the original funding source
- High refund volumes relative to transaction volume
- Refunds processed immediately after payout
- Refunds on closed or suspended accounts
- Patterns of partial refunds that aggregate to amounts just below reporting thresholds
- Refunds where the original payment was flagged for fraud or was from a high-risk jurisdiction
### Consumer protection and disclosure obligations
Depending on the jurisdiction, consumer protection laws may impose specific requirements:
- **US**: Regulation E (for consumer ACH and debit card transactions) and Regulation Z (for credit card transactions) impose error resolution and dispute rights that interact with refund processes. The FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices applies to refund policies and disclosures.
- **EU**: The Payment Services Directive (PSD2) and the Consumer Rights Directive impose refund rights and disclosure requirements. The SEPA Direct Debit scheme provides specific refund rights.
- **UK**: The Payment Services Regulations 2017 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 impose similar requirements.
Mosaic Relay should ensure that its merchant agreements and payer-facing disclosures clearly state refund policies, processing times, and the circumstances under which refunds may be delayed or rejected.
### Recordkeeping
BSA/AML recordkeeping requirements apply to refund transactions. Mosaic Relay should maintain records of:
- Original transaction details (amount, date, funding source, merchant)
- Refund transaction details (amount, date, destination, reason)
- Any overrides applied, including approver identities and justification
- Monitoring alerts generated and their disposition
- Communication with merchants regarding refunds
Card network rules also impose recordkeeping requirements for refunds, typically requiring merchants and processors to retain refund records for specified periods (often 12-24 months minimum, sometimes longer).

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Launch with strict original-source-only refunds and no exceptions
The tool launches with a hard rule that refunds can only go to the original funding source. No manual overrides for destination changes. Manual overrides for closed accounts are limited to processing the refund to the original source, not changing the destination.
**Advantages**: Strongest AML posture. Aligns with card network rules. Simplest to monitor and audit. Lowest regulatory risk.
**Disadvantages**: May not handle legitimate edge cases (e.g., original card expired or closed, merchant needs to refund via check or alternative method). Could create customer service friction.
### Path 2: Launch with original-source default and tightly controlled exceptions
The tool defaults to original-source refunds but allows exceptions through a controlled process. Exceptions require:
- Two-person approval (already planned)
- Documented justification
- Enhanced due diligence on the alternative destination
- Transaction monitoring alert generation
- Post-transaction review
**Advantages**: Handles legitimate edge cases. Maintains strong controls. Provides audit trail.
**Disadvantages**: More complex to build and operate. Requires clear policies on what constitutes a legitimate exception. Risk of exception process becoming a loophole if not tightly managed.
### Path 3: Phased launch starting with card refunds only
Launch the tool for card refunds only (where network rules are clearest and original-source enforcement is most straightforward), then expand to ACH and local methods after confirming rules and building method-specific logic.
**Advantages**: Reduces initial complexity. Allows learning from card refund patterns before expanding. Card networks have the most developed refund infrastructure.
**Disadvantages**: Slower time to full launch. May not meet business goal of reducing support requests across all methods.
### Path 4: Delay launch until monitoring coverage is confirmed
Do not launch until Mosaic Relay confirms whether its existing transaction-monitoring system covers refunds and reversals, and if not, builds or procures that coverage.
**Advantages**: Ensures AML controls are in place before the tool creates new risk surface. Avoids launching a tool that cannot be adequately monitored.
**Disadvantages**: Delays business benefits. May be overly cautious if existing monitoring can be confirmed quickly.

## Facts that could change the answer
1. **Mosaic Relay's legal entity and licensing status**: If Mosaic Relay operates as a money transmitter or holds specific licenses, its obligations for refunds may be more prescriptive. If it operates purely as a technology provider under its partners' licenses, the compliance burden may shift to those partners but Mosaic Relay still has contractual obligations.
2. **Funds-flow and settlement model**: If Mosaic Relay touches funds in transit (even without holding deposits), it may have direct obligations under money transmission laws. If funds flow directly between acquiring banks and merchants without Mosaic Relay in the flow, its role is more limited but still subject to network rules and contractual obligations.
3. **Acquiring partner and processor contracts**: The specific terms of these contracts will determine refund windows, permitted destinations, liability allocation, and operational requirements. These contracts may impose stricter requirements than network rules.
4. **Transaction monitoring coverage**: If existing monitoring does not cover refunds, the launch should be delayed or the monitoring gap must be addressed. If it does cover refunds, the launch can proceed with confidence that AML controls are in place.
5. **Launch jurisdictions**: Different jurisdictions have materially different rules. A US-only launch has different requirements than a US+EU+UK launch. Local payment methods in each jurisdiction have their own rules.
6. **Whether merchants are considered "merchants" or "agents" under applicable law**: This classification affects who bears refund obligations and liability.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before launch, counsel should:
1. **Obtain and review the specific acquiring partner and processor agreements** to confirm refund windows, permitted destinations, liability allocation, and any restrictions on manual overrides. This is the most critical verification step because these contracts will govern the operational reality of the tool.
2. **Confirm with the compliance team whether existing transaction-monitoring covers refunds and reversals**, and if not, what timeline and resources are needed to extend coverage. This should be a launch gate.
3. **Verify Mosaic Relay's legal entity structure and licensing status** in each target jurisdiction to confirm whether it has direct regulatory obligations for refunds or operates under partner licenses.
4. **Confirm the specific refund and return windows for each payment method** with each acquiring partner and processor, as these vary and are not publicly standardized.
5. **Review card network rules directly** (Visa Core Rules, Mastercard Rules) for the specific refund and reversal provisions applicable to Mosaic Relay's merchant category and transaction types. These are typically available to acquirers and their partners but are not fully public.
6. **Draft or review the merchant-facing refund policy and payer-facing disclosures** to ensure they accurately describe the tool's capabilities, limitations, and the circumstances under which refunds may be delayed or rejected.
7. **Establish clear policies for manual overrides** including: what constitutes a legitimate exception, required documentation, approval authority, monitoring alert generation, and post-transaction review procedures.

## What would change this
- **Working assumption**: This analysis assumes Mosaic Relay operates as a payment facilitator or technology provider under partner licenses, not as a licensed money transmitter with direct regulatory obligations. If Mosaic Relay holds its own money transmitter licenses, the analysis changes materially because it would have direct BSA/AML obligations for refund transactions, including potential SAR filing obligations for suspicious refund patterns.
- **Open fork**: The most significant fork is whether the tool will allow refunds to destinations other than the original funding source, even through manual override. If yes, the AML risk profile increases substantially and the tool may violate card network rules. If no, the tool is lower risk but may not handle all legitimate business cases. This decision should be made explicitly, not by default.
- **Not examined**: The specific terms of Mosaic Relay's acquiring partner and processor contracts were not reviewed. These contracts are the controlling documents for refund windows, permitted destinations, and liability allocation. The analysis above is based on general industry knowledge, not the specific contractual terms that will govern this tool.
- **Not examined**: The specific local payment methods that will be supported at launch were not identified. Each local method (SEPA, Faster Payments, Pix, etc.) has distinct rules that could materially affect the tool's design and compliance requirements.
- **Working assumption**: This analysis assumes that the "manual operations overrides" for closed accounts are intended to process refunds to the original payer, not to change the refund destination. If the overrides are intended to allow destination changes, the risk profile is significantly higher and additional controls are needed.
- **Open fork**: Whether Mosaic Relay's existing transaction-monitoring system covers refunds and reversals is unknown. If it does not, the launch should be delayed until monitoring is in place. If it does, the launch can proceed with existing controls. This is a factual question that should be resolved before launch, not after.
