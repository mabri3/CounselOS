      ## Question


      What are the controller/processor allocations and transfer mechanisms in the

      ## Working Analysis


      **No external authority retrieved**


      ## Matter summary

      Mosaic Relay, a payment infrastructure provider, plans to launch a unified operations
      dashboard combining transaction details, identity-verification results, KYB/KYC
      data, fraud scores, dispute records, support notes, and payout information.
      Authorized customer users would search and export these records for reconciliation
      and investigations. Counsel is involved because the design may expose more personal
      information than necessary, implicating GDPR/UK GDPR controller-processor rules,
      data minimization, and cross-border transfer restrictions. The launch timing
      is normal priority, but the architecture decisions made now will determine compliance
      obligations and vendor contract requirements.

      ## Decision question

      Should Mosaic Relay proceed with the unified dashboard under a processor-only
      model (acting on customer instructions) or a hybrid model (processor for customer-directed
      operations, independent controller for fraud/security/compliance), and what
      specific access controls, export restrictions, and vendor contract amendments
      are required before launch to prevent unlawful secondary use and excessive data
      exposure?

      ## Open questions

      - Which specific countries are the data subjects (payers, recipients, merchants)
      located in, and which countries will customer operations users access the dashboard
      from?

      - Do existing vendor contracts (identity verification, fraud scoring, acquiring
      banks, cloud providers) classify Mosaic Relay as a processor, controller, or
      joint controller, and do they permit the data combination and export functionality
      contemplated?

      - What is the specific profiling logic and automated decision-making threshold
      for fraud scores displayed in the dashboard—do they trigger automatic blocks
      or merely flag for human review?

      - Will customers be permitted to use exported dashboard data for their own marketing
      purposes, or is use strictly limited to reconciliation and fraud investigation?

      - What are the retention periods and deletion exceptions for each data category
      (legal hold, accounting, fraud prevention) across the combined dataset?

      ## Likely rules and issues

      **Controller/Processor Allocation (GDPR Art. 4(7)-(8), UK GDPR)**

      Mosaic Relay likely acts as a **processor** when providing dashboard tools to
      customer platforms (who determine purposes/means for their end-user data). However,
      Mosaic Relay is likely an **independent controller** for: (1) its own fraud
      monitoring and security obligations under BSA/AML and payment network rules;
      (2) KYB/KYC data it collects for its own regulatory compliance (not merely on
      customer instructions); and (3) support notes and operational data generated
      through its own customer relationship management.

      **Data Minimization and Purpose Limitation (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c), (b))**

      Combining seven distinct data categories into a single searchable interface
      creates high risk of function creep. The dashboard must enforce purpose limitation
      at the field level—e.g., a customer support agent investigating a dispute should
      not see full identity verification documents or fraud scores unless necessary
      for that specific investigation.

      **Cross-Border Transfers (GDPR Chapter V)**

      If customer users or data subjects are in the EU/UK, and Mosaic Relay or its
      vendors (cloud, fraud scoring) process data in the US or other third countries,
      the dashboard architecture must implement valid transfer mechanisms (SCCs, IDTA,
      or adequacy decisions) and conduct Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) for each
      vendor in the chain.

      **Automated Decision-Making (GDPR Art. 22)**

      If fraud scores or risk logic in the dashboard produce legal or similarly significant
      effects (e.g., automatic payout blocks, account suspensions), Mosaic Relay must
      ensure human intervention rights, provide meaningful information about the logic,
      and offer challenge mechanisms. If scores are merely advisory, Article 22 may
      not apply, but profiling transparency obligations (Art. 13(2)(f), 14(2)(g))
      still attach.

      **Vendor Contract Requirements (GDPR Art. 28)**

      Each vendor feeding data into the dashboard (identity verification, fraud scoring,
      acquiring banks, cloud hosting) must have a Data Processing Agreement (DPA)
      that: (1) permits the specific combination and display of data categories; (2)
      flows down the same access restrictions Mosaic Relay implements; and (3) addresses
      sub-processor chains for cross-border transfers.

      ## Viable paths

      **Path A: Processor-Only with Strict Purpose Binding**

      Mosaic Relay acts solely as processor for all dashboard functions. Customers
      (platforms) are the sole controllers determining all purposes.

      - *Pros*: Simpler contractual position; customers bear primary compliance burden
      for lawful basis and notices.

      - *Cons*: Likely inaccurate for fraud/KYB data where Mosaic Relay has independent
      regulatory obligations; may not reflect actual control over security and risk
      scoring logic.

      - *Requirements*: Robust Data Processing Agreements with all customers; strict
      technical enforcement of customer-configured access controls; no independent
      use of data for Mosaic Relay''s own purposes.

      **Path B: Hybrid Controller/Processor (Recommended)**

      Mosaic Relay acts as processor for customer-directed operations (search, export
      for reconciliation) but as independent controller for: (1) fraud prevention
      and security monitoring; (2) KYB/KYC compliance; (3) legal holds and regulatory
      reporting.

      - *Pros*: Accurately reflects operational reality; allows Mosaic Relay to maintain
      independent fraud databases and comply with BSA/AML obligations without customer
      instruction.

      - *Cons*: Requires Mosaic Relay to provide its own privacy notices for controller
      activities; must implement strict separation between processor and controller
      datasets to avoid commingling.

      - *Requirements*: Dual-track privacy notices; documented legitimate interest
      assessments for fraud/security processing; technical segregation of controller
      vs. processor data stores.

      **Path C: Joint Controller Arrangement**

      Mosaic Relay and customers act as joint controllers (GDPR Art. 26) for specific
      dashboard functions like fraud investigation.

      - *Pros*: Shared responsibility for high-risk processing.

      - *Cons*: Complex to administer; requires transparent allocation of responsibilities;
      rarely used for standard SaaS dashboards.

      - *Recommendation*: Avoid unless specific customer negotiations require shared
      control over fraud logic.

      ## Facts that could change the answer

      1. **Vendor contract terms**: If existing identity verification or fraud scoring
      contracts classify Mosaic Relay as a "controller" or prohibit data aggregation
      with other sources, the dashboard architecture may require renegotiation or
      technical re-architecture to isolate data streams.

      2. **Data subject locations**: If all data subjects and users are outside the
      EU/UK (e.g., US-only), GDPR/UK GDPR obligations drop away, leaving only state
      privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA, etc.) and sectoral rules (GLBA if bank partners require
      it), significantly reducing transfer mechanism and DPO requirements.

      3. **Automated decision thresholds**: If fraud scores trigger automatic actions
      without human review (e.g., auto-block payouts above risk threshold X), Article
      22 GDPR applies, requiring explicit consent or contract necessity plus safeguards.
      If scores are merely informational, standard transparency rules apply instead.

      4. **Customer marketing use**: If customers intend to use exported data for
      marketing (as hinted in "missing facts"), this constitutes a separate purpose
      requiring independent lawful basis (likely consent), rendering the current dashboard
      design unlawful for that use without significant notice and consent architecture.

      5. **Sub-processor locations**: If cloud vendors or fraud scoring engines process
      data in jurisdictions without adequacy (e.g., certain APAC regions), additional
      supplementary measures (encryption, pseudonymization) may be required beyond
      standard SCCs.

      ## Recommended last-mile verification

      Before finalizing the dashboard architecture, verify:

      1. **Vendor DPAs**: Pull the actual Data Processing Agreements for identity
      verification, fraud scoring, and cloud hosting vendors. Confirm whether they
      permit "aggregation," "combination," or "enrichment" of data with third-party
      sources, and whether they allow customer-facing display of vendor data.

      2. **Fraud score logic**: Interview the risk team to document exactly how fraud
      scores are generated and whether any automated actions (blocks, holds) occur
      without human review. If automated, prepare Article 22 GDPR compliance documentation.

      3. **Customer contract templates**: Review current customer Master Services
      Agreements to confirm whether they contemplate Mosaic Relay acting as an independent
      controller for fraud prevention, or if amendments are needed to add controller
      clauses.

      4. **Transfer mapping**: Create a data flow map showing exactly where dashboard
      data is stored (cloud region), where vendors process it, and where customer
      users access it from, to determine which transfer mechanisms (SCCs, IDTA, adequacy)
      apply to each hop.

      5. **Retention schedule audit**: Confirm that retention periods for "legal,
      fraud, accounting, and support purposes" are actually documented and technically
      enforceable in the dashboard''s database architecture, particularly for exported
      copies.
