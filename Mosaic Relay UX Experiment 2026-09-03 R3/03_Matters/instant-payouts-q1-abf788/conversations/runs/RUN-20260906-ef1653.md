  reply: '## The issue: the public $3,000 verification progress bar

    **The saved issue:** Publicly displaying the $3,000 verification threshold via
    a progress bar may facilitate evasion and raises funds-flow/consumer disclosure
    concerns.

    **Why the fact matters:** The progress bar tells every seller — including bad
    actors — the exact dollar amount they can receive before identity verification
    kicks in. That''s an open invitation to structure payouts just below the threshold
    to stay unidentified. It also creates a misleading impression that $3,000 is a
    legally meaningful compliance line, when you''ve already confirmed it has no legal
    basis.

    **One short contrast:** A progress bar that says "$1,240 of $3,000 until full
    verification" is functionally an instruction manual for evasion — a bad actor
    keeps payouts under $3,000 and never gets identified. Compare that to a design
    with no public threshold, where a bad actor can''t tell where the verification
    line is and can''t reliably structure around it.

    ## The applicable test

    **Actor:** Mosaic Relay, as the party initiating payouts and designing the seller-facing
    onboarding.

    **Jurisdiction:** US federal (BSA/AML) plus general consumer-protection/funds-flow
    disclosure principles.

    **The test:** There is no single statute that says "don''t display a verification
    threshold." The concern is twofold:

    1. **AML/structuring:** Under BSA/AML, a financial institution''s AML program
    must be reasonably designed to detect and prevent money laundering and structuring.
    Publicly advertising the exact threshold at which verification triggers is an
    AML program weakness — it facilitates structuring (deliberately keeping transactions
    below a reporting/verification threshold to avoid detection). This is a risk-based
    program deficiency, not a bright-line rule.

    2. **Consumer disclosure/funds-flow:** Displaying a "$X of $3,000 until full verification"
    bar can be misleading if it implies $3,000 is a legal or regulatory threshold,
    or if it misrepresents the funds-flow/verification process. This raises accuracy
    and fair-dealing concerns.

    **Exceptions:** None material. The concern applies regardless of whether the underlying
    $3,000 threshold is itself compliant — the display compounds the problem either
    way.

    ## Known vs. unknown facts

    **Known:**

    - The progress bar reads "$1,240 of $3,000 until full verification" (reported).

    - The $3,000 threshold has no legal basis — it was purely an engineering/product
    choice (confirmed).

    - Identity is deferred until $3,000 cumulative payouts (reported).

    **Unknown:**

    - Whether the bar is shown to all sellers or only some (e.g., only those approaching
    the threshold).

    - Whether the bar is the only place the threshold is disclosed, or whether it
    appears in terms/onboarding docs.

    - Whether any consumer-protection or state disclosure rules apply to Mosaic Relay''s
    specific payout flow (not researched in the vault).

    ## Conditional routes — each as an action the business can take

    **Choice 1 — Remove the progress bar entirely.**

    - **Depends on:** Nothing material; this is a product/UI decision.

    - **Next consequence:** Sellers no longer see the threshold; the structuring cue
    is removed.

    - **Risks:** Minimal. Some sellers may be confused about verification status,
    but that''s a UX concern, not a compliance one.

    - **Trade-off:** Loses a transparency feature, but removes the evasion cue and
    the misleading "legal threshold" implication.

    - **Remaining work:** Product change to remove the bar; confirm no other surface
    discloses the threshold.

    **Choice 2 — Keep the bar but reframe it as a status indicator, not a threshold.**

    - **Depends on:** Whether the underlying deferred-identity design is itself changed.
    If identity moves to onboarding (the recommended fix for the $3,000 issue), the
    bar becomes moot — there''s no threshold to display.

    - **Next consequence:** If the $3,000 threshold is eliminated, the bar either
    disappears or becomes a generic "verification in progress" indicator.

    - **Risks:** If the threshold stays and the bar is merely reworded, the structuring
    cue remains.

    - **Trade-off:** Preserves some transparency while removing the explicit dollar
    threshold.

    - **Remaining work:** Coordinate with the identity-redesign workstream; reword
    the bar to avoid stating a dollar threshold.

    **Choice 3 — Keep the bar as-is.**

    - **Depends on:** Accepting the structuring and disclosure risk.

    - **Next consequence:** Bad actors can structure below $3,000 to stay unidentified;
    the bar misrepresents $3,000 as a legal line.

    - **Risks:** AML program weakness (structuring facilitation), potential consumer-disclosure
    issue, and it compounds the already-non-compliant deferred-identity design.

    - **Trade-off:** No product change, but carries the highest risk.

    - **Remaining work:** None — this is the status quo.

    **Choice 4 — Fix the underlying threshold first, then the bar resolves itself.**

    - **Depends on:** The identity-redesign workstream (collect CIP at onboarding).
    If identity is collected at onboarding, there is no $3,000 threshold and no bar
    to display.

    - **Next consequence:** The progress bar issue disappears as a side effect of
    the larger fix.

    - **Risks:** Depends on the timeline of the identity redesign; if that slips,
    the bar remains a live issue.

    - **Trade-off:** This is the cleanest resolution but is gated on the bigger onboarding
    redesign.

    - **Remaining work:** The identity-redesign workstream; then remove or repurpose
    the bar.

    ## Bottom line

    The progress bar is a **secondary issue that compounds the primary $3,000 problem**
    — it advertises the exact amount a bad actor can receive before verification.
    The cleanest resolution is **Choice 4**: fix the underlying deferred-identity
    design (collect CIP at onboarding), which makes the bar moot. If the threshold
    must remain for now, **Choice 1 (remove the bar)** is the lowest-risk interim
    step, because it removes the structuring cue and the misleading "legal threshold"
    implication without waiting on the larger redesign. **Choice 3 (keep as-is)**
    is not recommended.
