# Harbor 2 matter dossier

## Current position

The Harbor Pay migration needs legal and compliance review before the planned Nov 15 close. This is the accepted working view. No decision has been recorded.

This draft recommends continuing close preparation but making the day-one service migration conditional. Mosaic should not give all Harbor users full access until:

1. Mosaic has documented authority for each post-close service in each affected state.
2. Compliance confirms that Mosaic may rely on Harbor’s know-your-customer (KYC) results, or defines and completes remediation.
3. Authorized reviewers assess Harbor’s open alerts and suspicious activity report (SAR) history.
4. Mosaic tests the replacement transaction-monitoring feed and finds no material coverage gap.

If a condition is not met, Mosaic should hold the affected state launch, restrict affected accounts, or delay full product access. The $50 welcome bonus should also wait for review of its structure, terms, funding, and disclosures.

The saved material does not establish that the acquisition close itself must wait. However, the unread alerts and SAR history could affect deal terms, risk allocation, or the decision to close.

### Assumptions used

- Harbor is subject to the state money-transmission and federal anti-money-laundering requirements discussed below. The licensing, KYC, alert, and monitoring conclusions depend on this unverified legal premise.
- Mosaic will become the operating provider when Harbor users receive a unified Mosaic account, combined balance, and access to Mosaic products. The licensing analysis depends on this inference.
- The analysis does not assume that Harbor’s licenses transfer, that Harbor’s KYC results satisfy Mosaic’s program, or that the monitoring transition will work without a gap.

### Record conflicts

- Harbor reportedly has licenses in 38 states, Mosaic has licenses in 41 states, and the combined footprint is 44 states. A 44-state footprint is three states larger than Mosaic’s 41-state footprint, but the record says the acquisition opens four new markets. The license lists and the intended meaning of “market” are needed. Until then, the states requiring separate authority cannot be identified reliably.
- Matter metadata gives a target date of `2026-09-11`. The reported close is “Nov 15,” approximately ten weeks after the Sept. 9 intake. A saved hypothetical uses November 15, 2026, but that does not establish the actual closing year. The operative close date and year remain unresolved.

## Decision question

Does any part of the Harbor Pay acquisition migration need legal review before the Nov 15 close?

### Money transmission licensing: whether Mosaic Relay can operate under the combined 44-state footprint and whether Harbor's MTLs transfer/survive the acquisition

**Current answer**

Mosaic should not treat Harbor’s money transmitter licenses as assets that automatically move to Mosaic through the reported asset purchase. Mosaic should not start regulated services in a state unless it has documented authority for its complete post-close activity.

The acquisition close and the service launch can be managed as separate milestones. If authority is not established, the present fallback is to hold the affected state launch or restrict regulated functions.

**Rule and support**

Saved research concludes that the answer must be determined state by state. The review must cover Mosaic’s authority, transaction notices or approvals, interim operations, outstanding customer obligations, and Harbor’s license surrender.

Four sources were retrieved, but no operative passage was selected, read, or verified. They do not establish the rule in any affected state:

The model act is not proof of any state’s adopted law. The premise that an asset purchase does not automatically give Mosaic Harbor’s operating authority remains an unverified state-law conclusion.

**Application**

Mosaic plans to use or assume Harbor’s licenses in the new markets. The planned unified Mosaic account, combined balance, and access to Mosaic products indicate that Mosaic may become the actual provider. If so, keeping Harbor’s entity or licenses active might not authorize Mosaic’s conduct.

Harbor could remain the licensed provider during a transition only if customer contracts, funds flow, operating control, licenses, bonds, records, and applicable state rules support that result. The documents and actual operations must match.

A partner bank holding funds would not alone resolve the issue. The analysis still depends on who contracts with users, controls transfers, owes repayment, records balances, resolves errors, and performs regulated duties.

**Next step**

Identify the Harbor-only jurisdictions and prepare a state matrix. For each state, determine Mosaic’s authority, required filings or approvals, operation while an application is pending, treatment of customer obligations, Harbor’s run-off duties, and the launch restriction needed if authority is not established.

**Practical answer**

**Do not treat Harbor’s money transmitter licenses as assets that move to Mosaic in the asset purchase.** Use this closing rule:

> **No confirmed authority means no regulated launch by Mosaic in that state.**

The asset purchase can still close. However, Mosaic should not activate regulated services in the four Harbor-only states unless one of these conditions is met:

1. Mosaic obtains its own license.
2. The state approves a transfer or other license change.
3. The regulator confirms a lawful interim structure.
4. Harbor remains the real licensed provider under a valid transition plan.
5. Mosaic blocks regulated functions in that state.

The planned unified Mosaic account is important. It suggests that Mosaic, not Harbor, will become the service provider. If that is correct, keeping Harbor’s legal entity and licenses alive might not give Mosaic authority to operate.

**State notice and approval**

Do not search only for a rule called “license transfer.” Review each state for:

- sale of substantially all assets;
- change of control;
- change in business plan;
- change in licensed activities;
- assignment of outstanding payment obligations;
- change in responsible persons, locations, or agents;
- license surrender or termination;
- customer agreement assignment; and
- movement of stored balances or customer funds.

An asset purchase might fall outside a technical “change of control” rule because Harbor’s ownership does not change. That does not resolve the issue. Harbor might still need a notice or surrender approval. Mosaic might separately need authority before it starts the regulated activity.

The state matrix should distinguish:

- notice only;
- approval before closing;
- approval before customer migration;
- new license required;
- operation permitted while an application is pending;
- regulator-approved transition available; and
- no supported interim path.

**Interim operation**

Harbor could remain the licensed provider for a transition period. However, the structure must be real, not only contractual language.

A supportable structure would normally need to show that:

- Harbor remains responsible to customers.
- Customer terms name Harbor as the provider.
- Harbor controls the regulated activity as required.
- Harbor’s licenses, bonds, and financial coverage remain active.
- Customer funds and outstanding obligations remain covered.
- Harbor can complete reports, refunds, complaints, and examinations.
- Required records remain available.
- Mosaic’s operational role is permitted.
- The partner bank accepts the structure.
- Each relevant regulator permits or accepts the structure.

The present plan creates tension with this approach. A single Mosaic account, one combined balance, and full access to Mosaic products make it harder to show that Harbor remains the true provider.

**Partner-bank custody**

A partner bank account is not automatically a substitute for a money transmitter license. The key questions concern the complete activity and obligation flow.

For each customer balance and transfer, document:

1. Who receives the customer’s instruction?
2. Who accepts the obligation to transmit or return the funds?
3. In whose name is the bank account held?
4. Is it a custodial or “for benefit of” account?
5. Who can direct withdrawals?
6. Who owns the ledger and resolves errors?
7. Who bears loss if a transfer fails?
8. Which entity owes the customer a refund?
9. Which entity’s license, bond, and permissible investments cover the obligation?
10. Does the bank agreement permit assignment, migration, or a change in the program manager?
11. Is bank consent required before closing or migration?
12. What happens to funds if Harbor, Mosaic, or the bank relationship ends?

A bank-led model might provide a different licensing analysis if the bank is the actual provider and Mosaic performs only permitted services. A bank account that merely holds funds for Mosaic or Harbor is different. The legal documents, customer terms, control rights, and actual operations must all support the claimed model.

Also review:

- account segregation;
- daily reconciliation;
- shortfalls and reserve funding;
- bank setoff rights;
- liens and security interests;
- continuity if the account is frozen;
- treatment of pending transfers;
- return and chargeback duties;
- insolvency treatment;
- customer disclosures; and
- whether any deposit-insurance statement is accurate for the actual account structure.

**Recommended closing position**

Keep the acquisition closing separate from the regulated-service launch.

For the four Harbor-only states, use a state launch hold unless Mosaic has documented authority. For overlapping states, confirm that Mosaic’s existing licenses cover the complete post-close product set and funds flow.

Do not surrender Harbor’s licenses or release its bonds until the applicable regulators permit it and all outstanding obligations have been assigned, completed, or placed into an approved run-off structure.

This investigation does not change the current recommendation. It adds one material point: **partner-bank custody is part of the licensing analysis, but custody alone does not cure an authority gap.**

### BSA/AML and KYC: whether relying on Harbor's prior KYC (with missing underlying documents for pre-2023 users) satisfies Mosaic Relay's independent CIP/AML obligations

**Current answer**

The saved material does not support a full day-one migration with no re-verification. Mosaic has not confirmed whether its anti-money-laundering and customer identification program permits reliance on Harbor’s prior KYC. The missing pre-2023 records may prevent Mosaic from showing that its own requirements are met.

If Mosaic’s program permits reliance and the retained evidence meets its requirements, targeted remediation may be sufficient. If it does not, affected users should be re-verified or restricted before receiving relevant services.

**Rule and support**

This issue has not been researched. The unverified legal premise is that Mosaic must apply its own risk-based customer identification, recordkeeping, and anti-money-laundering controls when it becomes the service provider.

The precise research question is whether Mosaic may rely on identification work completed by Harbor, and what evidence, agreement, certification, or remediation is required when underlying documents are unavailable.

**Application**

Harbor’s former KYC vendor shut down in 2024. Mosaic has pass/fail results for all users, but it lacks underlying documents for users onboarded before 2023. A pass/fail result shows that a process produced an outcome. It does not establish which information was collected, how identity was verified, whether required records were retained, or whether Harbor’s process matches Mosaic’s program.

The affected population is not stated. That count matters because it affects whether remediation can finish before launch and whether restrictions can be targeted.

**Next step**

Compliance should compare Harbor’s KYC process and available records with Mosaic’s written program. The required output is a documented reliance or remediation decision, the affected user count, and account controls for users who do not meet the approved standard.

### SAR and alert handling: obligations to review, file, and manage Harbor's ~40 open alerts and SARs, and successor liability

**Current answer**

Authorized reviewers should assess the approximately 40 open alerts and the SAR history before Mosaic accepts unrestricted activity from the affected users. The saved facts do not establish that Mosaic inherits every Harbor duty, but the unread material could reveal reportable conduct, control failures, customer risk, or facts relevant to the acquisition.

**Rule and support**

This issue has not been researched. The legal treatment may depend on which entity identified the conduct, when relevant deadlines began, which entity remains responsible after the asset purchase, what the purchase agreement assigns, and whether applicable law permits access to specific SAR material.

SAR information is sensitive. Diligence must distinguish lawful access by authorized personnel from disclosure or transfer of restricted material. The precise research question is which entity must review, decide, file, retain, and continue monitoring each pre-close and post-close matter.

**Application**

Diligence identified approximately 40 open alerts and “a handful” of SARs from the last year. None has been reviewed. The record does not identify the alert dates, subjects, conduct, status, prior decisions, or affected accounts.

This is more than a record gap. The unread material could change account migration controls and the deal’s risk allocation. It does not, by itself, prove a violation or successor liability.

**Next step**

Use authorized personnel to create a privileged case inventory. Classify each alert by age, subject, activity, deadline, disposition, required action, and affected account. Separately identify what SAR information Mosaic may lawfully review or receive. Escalate any material findings into migration controls and the deal review.
