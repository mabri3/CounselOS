      ## 1. State money transmission licensing

      **The core problem:** Money transmitter licenses are generally not transferable
      or assignable. State money transmission statutes typically require the licensed
      entity to notify the regulator of a change of control or to apply for a new
      license when the licensed entity is acquired or its assets are transferred.
      In an asset purchase, the buyer does not automatically acquire the seller''s
      licenses.

      **What this means for the four new markets:** Mosaic cannot simply "use" Harbor''s
      licenses in the four states where it lacks its own license. If Harbor''s licenses
      are held in Harbor''s name and Harbor''s corporate entity will not survive the
      asset purchase as a licensed subsidiary, those licenses will likely lapse or
      be revoked. Mosaic would need to apply for new licenses in those states, which
      typically takes 3–6 months or longer. Operating without a license in a state
      where money transmission is regulated can result in cease-and-desist orders,
      fines, and criminal penalties.

      **What this means for the other 40 states:** Even in states where both Mosaic
      and Harbor hold licenses, the acquisition may trigger change-of-control or notice
      requirements. Many states require advance notice (often 30–90 days) before a
      change of control of a licensed entity. Failure to provide notice can result
      in license suspension or revocation.

      **Uncertainty about Mosaic''s regulated status:** The request states that Mosaic
      holds MTLs in 41 states, but it is not confirmed whether Mosaic operates as
      a licensed money transmitter or under a partner-bank model. If Mosaic operates
      under a bank partnership, the licensing analysis may differ—the bank''s licenses
      may cover the activity, but the bank''s consent and regulatory approvals may
      be required.

      ## 2. BSA/AML and KYC/CIP obligations

      **The core problem:** Under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), money transmitters are
      required to maintain a Customer Identification Program (CIP) that includes verifying
      the identity of customers and maintaining records of the information used to
      verify identity. When Mosaic migrates Harbor''s users without re-verification,
      it is relying on Harbor''s prior KYC. However, Mosaic lacks the underlying KYC
      documents for users onboarded before 2023 because Harbor''s KYC vendor shut
      down in 2024.

      **What this means:** Mosaic''s own CIP program must permit reliance on another
      institution''s prior KYC, and Mosaic must have a reasonable basis for believing
      that Harbor''s KYC was adequate. The pass/fail results alone may not be sufficient—Mosaic
      may need the underlying documents to demonstrate that Harbor''s KYC met regulatory
      standards. The absence of underlying documents for pre-2023 users creates a
      compliance gap.

      **FinCEN guidance:** FinCEN has issued guidance on reliance on another financial
      institution''s CIP, but that guidance generally requires a written agreement
      and a reasonable basis for reliance. The absence of underlying documents may
      undermine the reasonableness of reliance.

      **Viable paths:**

      - **Path A: Re-verify pre-2023 users.** Mosaic re-verifies the identity of pre-2023
      users before or shortly after migration. This is the safest path but may be
      operationally burdensome for 240,000 users.

      - **Path B: Risk-based reliance with enhanced monitoring.** Mosaic relies on
      Harbor''s pass/fail results for pre-2023 users but applies enhanced monitoring
      and transaction limits to those users until it can obtain additional verification.
      This path requires a documented risk assessment and compliance sign-off.

      - **Path C: Obtain representations and warranties from Harbor.** Mosaic obtains
      representations and warranties from Harbor that its KYC was adequate and indemnification
      for any BSA/AML liability arising from pre-close KYC failures. This path shifts
      risk but does not eliminate Mosaic''s own compliance obligations.

      **Facts that could change the answer:**

      - Whether Mosaic''s AML/CIP program permits reliance on another institution''s
      prior KYC (currently unknown—compliance must confirm).

      - The number of pre-2023 users and the feasibility of re-verification.

      - Whether Harbor can provide any additional documentation or attestations about
      its KYC process.

      **Recommended last-mile verification:**

      - Confirm with compliance whether Mosaic''s AML/CIP program permits reliance
      on Harbor''s prior KYC.

      - Assess the feasibility of re-verifying pre-2023 users or applying enhanced
      monitoring.

      - Obtain representations and warranties from Harbor about its KYC process and
      indemnification for BSA/AML liability.

      ## 3. Open alerts, SARs, and monitoring continuity

      **The core problem:** Harbor has approximately 40 open alerts and a handful
      of SARs from the last year that no one has reviewed. Open alerts represent potential
      suspicious activity that has not been dispositioned. Unreviewed SARs may indicate
      systemic issues with Harbor''s monitoring or reporting. When Mosaic acquires
      Harbor''s assets and migrates its users, it may inherit liability for Harbor''s
      pre-close BSA/AML failures.

      **SAR confidentiality:** SARs are confidential under federal law. Mosaic cannot
      disclose the existence or contents of a SAR to unauthorized parties. However,
      Mosaic can review SARs as part of diligence and can use the information to assess
      risk and make decisions about the acquisition.

      **Successor liability:** In an asset purchase, the buyer generally does not
      inherit the seller''s liabilities unless it expressly assumes them. However,
      BSA/AML liability can attach to the buyer if the buyer continues the seller''s
      business and the government asserts successor liability under a "continuity
      of enterprise" theory. Mosaic should review the open alerts and SARs to assess
      the risk of successor liability and to determine whether to assume or disclaim
      liability for Harbor''s pre-close BSA/AML failures.

      **Monitoring continuity:** Harbor''s transaction monitoring vendor contract
      terminates at close. The plan is to repoint Harbor''s transaction feed to Mosaic''s
      engine on day one. This transition must be validated to ensure that there is
      no gap in monitoring and that Mosaic''s engine is calibrated to detect suspicious
      activity in Harbor''s user base. A monitoring gap could result in unreported
      suspicious activity and BSA/AML violations.

      **Viable paths:**

      - **Path A: Review alerts and SARs before close.** Mosaic reviews the open alerts
      and SARs as part of diligence and assesses the risk of successor liability.
      This is the safest path but may delay close.

      - **Path B: Assume liability with indemnification.** Mosaic assumes liability
      for Harbor''s pre-close BSA/AML failures in exchange for indemnification from
      Harbor. This path shifts risk but does not eliminate Mosaic''s own compliance
      obligations.

      - **Path C: Disclaim liability and segregate users.** Mosaic disclaims liability
      for Harbor''s pre-close BSA/AML failures and segregates Harbor''s users into
      a separate monitoring queue until it can validate the monitoring transition.
      This path requires careful documentation and may not fully protect against successor
      liability.

      **Facts that could change the answer:**

      - The content of the open alerts and SARs (currently unreviewed).

      - Whether the deal structure allows Mosaic to disclaim liability for Harbor''s
      pre-close BSA/AML failures.

      - The feasibility of validating the monitoring transition before close.

      **Recommended last-mile verification:**

      - Review the open alerts and SARs as part of diligence.

      - Assess the risk of successor liability and determine whether to assume or
      disclaim liability.

      - Validate the monitoring transition before close and document the validation.

      ## 4. $50 welcome bonus

      **The core problem:** Marketing wants to launch a $50 first-transfer welcome
      bonus the day after close. This bonus is a consumer promotion that may trigger
      FTC and state UDAP (unfair and deceptive acts and practices) requirements, as
      well as Regulation E (electronic fund transfer) requirements if the bonus is
      delivered via electronic fund transfer.

      **FTC/state UDAP:** The bonus must be clearly and conspicuously disclosed, including
      any eligibility requirements, expiration dates, and restrictions. Failure to
      disclose material terms can result in FTC enforcement action or state UDAP claims.

      **Regulation E:** If the bonus is delivered via electronic fund transfer, it
      may be subject to Regulation E, which requires disclosures and error resolution
      procedures. However, a one-time bonus is likely exempt from Regulation E''s
      periodic statement requirements.

      **Funds-flow:** The bonus must be funded from a lawful source. If the bonus
      is funded from Mosaic''s operating funds, it is likely permissible. If the bonus
      is funded from customer funds or float, it may raise additional issues.

      **Viable paths:**

      - **Path A: Launch with clear disclosures.** Mosaic launches the bonus with
      clear and conspicuous disclosures of eligibility, expiration, and restrictions.
      This is the safest path.

      - **Path B: Delay launch pending review.** Mosaic delays the bonus launch until
      legal review is complete. This path avoids risk but delays the marketing campaign.

      **Facts that could change the answer:**

      - The structure of the bonus (e.g., cash, credit, or fee waiver).

      - The funding source for the bonus.

      - The states where the bonus will be offered and their specific UDAP requirements.

      **Recommended last-mile verification:**

      - Review the bonus structure and funding source.

      - Draft clear and conspicuous disclosures.

      - Confirm compliance with Regulation E, if applicable.
