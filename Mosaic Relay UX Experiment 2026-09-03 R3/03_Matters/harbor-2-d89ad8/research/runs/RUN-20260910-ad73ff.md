      # The Bank Secrecy Act


      The Currency and Foreign Transactions Reporting Act of 1970, its amendments,
      and the other statutes relating to the subject matter of that Act, have come
      to be referred to as the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA). The BSA authorizes the Department
      of the Treasury to impose reporting and other requirements on financial institutions
      and other businesses to help detect and prevent money laundering. Specifically,
      the regulations implementing the BSA require financial institutions to, among
      other things, keep records of cash purchases of negotiable instruments, file
      reports of cash transactions exceeding $10,000 (daily aggregate amount), and
      to report suspicious activity that might signify money laundering, tax evasion,
      or other criminal activities. The BSA is sometimes referred to as an "anti-money

      ## Likely rules and issues

      ### 1. State money-transmitter licensing and transfer in an asset purchase

      **Federal vs. State:** Money transmission is regulated primarily at the state
      level; there is no federal money transmitter license. State financial regulators
      are the primary regulators of nonbanks operating within the United States [source:SRC-00e489d1e0295d2fad25].

      **The transfer problem:** State MTLs are generally non-transferable. In an asset
      purchase, the buyer (Mosaic Relay) must typically apply for new licenses or
      obtain explicit state approval for a change of control or license assumption.
      The plan to "use/assume Harbor''s licenses" for the four new markets [the internal
      record] is not viable without state-by-state regulatory approval. With close
      targeted in ~10 weeks, obtaining new MTLs or change-of-control approvals in
      44 states is likely impossible; many states require 3–6 months or longer for
      new license applications.

      **Asset vs. stock distinction:** The acquisition is structured as an asset purchase
      [the internal record]. This structure exacerbates the licensing problem because
      licenses do not automatically transfer with assets. A stock purchase (or merger)
      might preserve the corporate entity holding the licenses, but an asset purchase
      requires affirmative regulatory approval for license transfer or new issuance.

      ### 2. BSA/AML and reliance on prior customer identification (CIP)

      **Federal requirement:** The Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) requires financial institutions
      to maintain records sufficient to enable transactions and activity in customer
      accounts to be reconstructed [source:SRC-23325868d05d711df7ea]. This includes
      implementing a Customer Identification Program (CIP) with risk-based procedures
      for verifying customer identity.

      **The reliance gap:** Mosaic Relay cannot simply rely on Harbor’s prior KYC
      pass/fail results. While FinCEN guidance permits reliance on another financial
      institution’s CIP in limited circumstances (e.g., when the customer is opening
      a new account and the other institution provides specific certifications), that
      reliance requires the relying institution to obtain the underlying identity
      documentation or ensure the other institution retains it. Here, the underlying
      documents for pre-2023 users are missing [the internal record], and Harbor’s
      KYC vendor has shut down. Pass/fail results alone do not satisfy Mosaic Relay’s
      independent obligation to maintain records sufficient to reconstruct customer
      activity [source:SRC-23325868d05d711df7ea].

      **Timing:** Mosaic Relay must perform its own CIP on day 1 when these customers
      open accounts with Mosaic. The "no re-verification" plan [the internal record]
      violates this requirement unless Mosaic can obtain the underlying documentation
      before migration.

      ### 3. Open alerts, SAR confidentiality, and transaction-monitoring continuity

      **SAR confidentiality:** SARs and any information that would reveal the existence
      of a SAR are confidential [source:SRC-af5274560289d39360e8]. Unauthorized disclosure
      is prohibited. In an asset purchase, the buyer does not automatically acquire
      the right to review the seller’s SARs. Mosaic Relay cannot "read" Harbor’s SARs
      as part of diligence without violating confidentiality rules unless specific
      safe harbors apply (e.g., certain information sharing under Section 314(b),
      if both are participants, or through a FinCEN-approved process). The ~40 open
      alerts and "handful" of SARs [the internal record] represent unknown BSA/AML
      liability that Mosaic would assume upon migration.

      **Monitoring continuity:** The BSA requires financial institutions to report
      suspicious activity and maintain effective monitoring [source:SRC-af5274560289d39360e8].
      Harbor’s monitoring SaaS terminates at close [the internal record]. Repointing
      the transaction feed to Mosaic’s engine on day 1 creates a high risk of a monitoring
      gap. If the repoint fails or is not validated, Mosaic will be operating 252,000
      accounts without adequate suspicious activity monitoring, creating immediate
      BSA liability.

      ### 4. Consumer-protection requirements for the $50 first-transfer bonus

      **Federal:** The bonus likely triggers Regulation E (Electronic Fund Transfer
      Act) disclosure requirements if it involves electronic fund transfers, and potentially
      Regulation DD (Truth in Savings) if the account is a "savings" or "interest-bearing"
      account. The CFPB has taken enforcement action against deceptive bonus offers
      that fail to disclose material terms (e.g., minimum transfer amounts, time limits,
      or fees).

      **State:** State money transmitter laws and consumer protection statutes (UDAP)
      apply. Some states regulate promotional offers tied to money transmission. The
      bonus structure [Q-BONUS, left open] determines whether it constitutes a "gift"
      or "premium" subject to specific state banking or money transmitter regulations.

        # The Bank Secrecy Act


        The Currency and Foreign Transactions Reporting Act of 1970, its amendments,
        and the other statutes relating to the subject matter of that Act, have come
        to be referred to as the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA). The BSA authorizes the Department
        of the Treasury to impose reporting and other requirements on financial institutions
        and other businesses to help detect and prevent money laundering. Specifically,
        the regulations implementing the BSA require financial institutions to, among
        other things, keep records of cash purchases of negotiable instruments, file
        reports of cash transactions exceeding $10,000 (daily aggregate amount), and
        to report suspicious activity that might signify money laundering, tax evasion,
        or other criminal activities. The BSA is sometimes referred to as an "anti-money
        laundering" (AML) law or jointly as “BSA/AML,” and is codified at 12 U.S.C.
        1829b, 12 U.S.C. 1951-1960, 31 U.S.C. 5311-5314, 5316-5336, and includes notes
        thereto.
