The intake record is saved. Now let me give the lawyer a useful orientation with the strongest first answer and the prioritized questions.
**What you're dealing with.** Marcus is reporting a 5.5-month AML monitoring gap (Mar 14 → Sept 2) caused by three rules not being ported in the rules-engine cutover — structuring-pattern detection, rapid funds movement (in-and-out within 24h), and dormant-account reactivation. Scope: ~2.1M transactions, ~48k accounts. His plan has four parts: ship the fix tonight, backfill + auto-close low-risk alerts this weekend, hold off telling the bank partner ~3 weeks, and email affected sellers. The CFO question is whether this goes in the Series C data room (diligence opens in 6 weeks).
**The core issue is that this is an AML program failure, not just an ops incident.** The three dark rules are classic transaction-monitoring controls. A 5.5-month gap in those controls raises BSA/AML program-adequacy questions, and several of Marcus's plan points carry legal risk independent of the data-room question:
- **Auto-closing backfilled alerts based on the fraud model's low-risk scores** is the most legally fragile step. The model was running *during* the gap — its low-risk scores are exactly what you can't fully trust, because the very rules that would have flagged suspicious behavior were off. Auto-closing tens of thousands of alerts on that basis is hard to defend if a regulator or the bank partner later scrutinizes it. I'd push for human review (or at least a documented sampling/validation) of the backfilled low-risk population rather than blanket auto-close.
- **Holding off telling the bank partner ~3 weeks** may conflict with a contractual notification obligation in your acquiring-bank agreement. That's a fact we need to check — if the agreement requires prompt notification of monitoring failures, the delay itself becomes a second problem.
- **The seller apology email** ("a delay in reviewing some transactions") is a factual-accuracy question. If the gap actually meant transactions weren't reviewed at all for 5.5 months, that framing understates it and could itself be a misrepresentation. The wording needs to be accurate without over-committing.
- **The data room question** — the honest answer is that a material AML monitoring failure of this scope is the kind of thing that surfaces in diligence, and trying to hide it is worse than disclosing it. But *what* goes in and *how* it's framed depends heavily on whether actual illicit activity slipped through, which is still unknown.
**No external authority retrieved** — I have not pulled BSA/AML or bank-partner notification authority, so treat the above as preliminary analysis pending research and the facts below.
The single most decision-changing fact is whether actual fraud/structuring/sanctions activity occurred during the gap. That determines severity, notification obligations, and how the data-room disclosure is framed. My prioritized questions:
1. **Did any actual fraud, structuring, or sanctions activity occur during the gap that went undetected (or is it still unknown)?** — This drives everything: severity of the AML failure, whether regulator/bank notification is required, and how the disclosure is framed.
2. **Does the bank partner agreement or any regulatory framework impose a specific deadline to notify the bank partner of a monitoring failure?** — Directly tests Marcus's 3-week hold.
3. **Is the fraud model's low-risk scoring during the gap reliable enough to support auto-closing backfilled alerts, or should they get human review?** — Tests the most legally fragile part of the plan.
4. **Does Mosaic Relay have a documented AML/transaction-monitoring program, and who owns it?** — Determines program-adequacy characterization and accountability.
Answer these (or tell me which to skip) and I'll build out the analysis and a draft data-room/disclosure position.
