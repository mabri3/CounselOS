    **The core issue is that this is an AML program failure, not just an ops incident.**
    The three dark rules are classic transaction-monitoring controls. A 5.5-month
    gap in those controls raises BSA/AML program-adequacy questions, and several of
    Marcus''s plan points carry legal risk independent of the data-room question:

    - **Auto-closing backfilled alerts based on the fraud model''s low-risk scores**
    is the most legally fragile step. The model was running *during* the gap — its
    low-risk scores are exactly what you can''t fully trust, because the very rules
    that would have flagged suspicious behavior were off. Auto-closing tens of thousands
    of alerts on that basis is hard to defend if a regulator or the bank partner later
    scrutinizes it. I''d push for human review (or at least a documented sampling/validation)
    of the backfilled low-risk population rather than blanket auto-close.

    - **Holding off telling the bank partner ~3 weeks** may conflict with a contractual
    notification obligation in your acquiring-bank agreement. That''s a fact we need
    to check — if the agreement requires prompt notification of monitoring failures,
    the delay itself becomes a second problem.

    - **The seller apology email** ("a delay in reviewing some transactions") is a
    factual-accuracy question. If the gap actually meant transactions weren''t reviewed
    at all for 5.5 months, that framing understates it and could itself be a misrepresentation.
    The wording needs to be accurate without over-committing.

    - **The data room question** — the honest answer is that a material AML monitoring
    failure of this scope is the kind of thing that surfaces in diligence, and trying
    to hide it is worse than disclosing it. But *what* goes in and *how* it''s framed
    depends heavily on whether actual illicit activity slipped through, which is still
    unknown.

    notification authority, so treat the above as preliminary analysis pending research

    Marcus''s 3-week hold.
