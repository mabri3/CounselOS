# How are pre-settlement instant payouts from card-acceptance proceeds legally cha

## Question

How are pre-settlement instant payouts from card-acceptance proceeds legally characterized (settlement/hold vs. loan/advance) under U.S. money transmission and lending law?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger, a fintech working with sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A., wants to let online merchants receive instant payouts from card-acceptance proceeds before the underlying transactions fully settle. The proposed flow would maintain a reserve, evaluate merchant history and fraud signals, and later debit the reserve or merchant account for chargebacks, refunds, reversals, and network adjustments. Counsel is involved because the legal characterization of this arrangement—whether it is a settlement/hold, a loan, or another form of advance—drives regulatory exposure under money transmission and lending law, and because required merchant agreement terms, disclosures, reserve practices, and sponsor-bank approvals remain undefined. Product wants to launch a pilot with 10 established merchants on October 15, 2026, with a $25,000 per-payout cap and a $100,000 aggregate daily cap.
## Decision question
Should Juniper Ledger characterize its instant payout feature as a settlement/hold on unsettled card proceeds, a loan or advance funded by its own capital, or another structure, and what merchant agreement terms, disclosures, reserve and collection practices, and sponsor-bank approvals are required before launching the October 15, 2026 pilot with 10 merchants?
## Open questions
- How will Juniper Ledger characterize the reserve/advance (hold vs. loan vs. other form of advance), and who funds the instant payout before settlement?
- Who bears the credit risk if the merchant's proceeds are insufficient to cover chargebacks, refunds, reversals, and network adjustments?
- How long will merchants remain liable after an instant payout for chargebacks, refunds, reversals, and network adjustments?
- How will Juniper Ledger collect negative balances if the reserve or account is insufficient?
- What does the Cedar Harbor Bank program agreement require for this feature, and has the bank been engaged?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.**
The following is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of U.S. financial regulation. It is not retrieved authority and should be verified before reliance.
### 1. Legal characterization drives regulatory exposure
The core issue is whether the instant payout is:
- **A settlement or hold on the merchant's own funds** — If Juniper Ledger is simply accelerating access to funds the merchant has already earned from card sales, and the payout is net of a reserve for contingencies, the arrangement may be characterized as a payment service or funds settlement rather than credit. This is the most favorable characterization.
- **A loan or advance funded by Juniper Ledger's capital** — If Juniper Ledger is advancing its own funds against future card proceeds, with the merchant obligated to repay regardless of whether the underlying transactions settle, the arrangement looks like credit. This triggers state lending laws, usury limits, licensing requirements, and potentially UDAAP (unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices) exposure.
- **A hybrid or factoring arrangement** — Some structures purchase future receivables at a discount (merchant cash advance / factoring). These may avoid lending characterization in some states but face increasing regulatory scrutiny and recharacterization risk.
### 2. Money transmission
If Juniper Ledger receives funds from the card processor and transmits them to merchants, it may be engaged in money transmission under state money transmitter laws. However, if Juniper Ledger is acting as an agent of the payee (the merchant) under a state-law agent-of-the-payee exemption, or if the sponsor bank is the entity moving funds, money transmission exposure may be reduced. The characterization of the payout (settlement vs. advance) affects this analysis.
### 3. Lending and credit regulation
If the payout is characterized as a loan or advance:
- **State lending licenses** may be required in states where merchants are located.
- **Usury limits** may apply to the fee charged.
- **UDAAP risk** arises if fees, terms, or collection practices are not clearly disclosed or are unfair.
- **True lender risk** if Juniper Ledger is the economic lender but attempts to route through the sponsor bank.
### 4. Reserve and collection practices
- **Reserve as security** — If the reserve is a hold on the merchant's own funds, debiting it for chargebacks is generally permissible with proper authorization.
- **Negative balance collection** — If the merchant's account goes negative, Juniper Ledger's ability to collect depends on the merchant agreement terms, state law on setoff and recoupment, and whether the arrangement is characterized as credit (which may trigger debt collection laws).
- **Duration of liability** — Merchants should remain liable for chargebacks and adjustments for a defined period tied to card network rules (typically 120–180 days for chargebacks, but network-specific).
### 5. Sponsor-bank approvals
Cedar Harbor Bank, as the named bank for deposit accounts and payment rails, will need to approve this feature under the program-management and banking-services agreement. The bank's regulator (likely the OCC if it is a national bank) will expect the bank to oversee Juniper Ledger's compliance. The bank may require:
- Amendments to the program agreement
- Enhanced due diligence and monitoring
- Reserve and capital requirements
- Consumer/commercial protection disclosures
### 6. Card network rules
Visa and Mastercard have rules on settlement timing, chargebacks, and reserves. Accelerating payouts may require network approval or compliance with specific network programs.

## Viable paths
### Path A: Settlement/hold characterization (lowest regulatory risk)
Structure the payout as an acceleration of the merchant's own settled funds, not an advance of Juniper Ledger's capital. Key elements:
- The merchant has already earned the funds from card sales.
- Juniper Ledger holds a reserve from the merchant's proceeds to cover contingencies.
- The payout is net of the reserve and fees.
- The merchant agreement clearly states this is a settlement service, not a loan.
- Fees are disclosed as service fees, not interest.
**Pros:** Avoids lending license requirements, usury limits, and most UDAAP credit risk.
**Cons:** Requires careful drafting to avoid recharacterization; may limit the amount that can be advanced if reserves are large.
### Path B: Loan/advance characterization (higher regulatory risk, more flexibility)
Structure the payout as an advance of Juniper Ledger's capital against future card proceeds. Key elements:
- Obtain state lending licenses or partner with a licensed lender.
- Comply with usury limits and disclosure requirements.
- Treat fees as interest or finance charges.
- Implement UDAAP-compliant disclosures and collection practices.
**Pros:** More flexibility in structuring fees and terms; clearer legal framework.
**Cons:** Requires licenses, compliance infrastructure, and exposes Juniper Ledger to lending regulation.
### Path C: Factoring / merchant cash advance (intermediate risk)
Structure the payout as a purchase of future receivables at a discount. Key elements:
- The merchant sells future card proceeds to Juniper Ledger at a discount.
- The arrangement is documented as a sale, not a loan.
- Repayment is contingent on the merchant generating sufficient card sales.
**Pros:** May avoid lending characterization in some states.
**Cons:** Increasing regulatory scrutiny; some states have recharacterized these as loans; UDAAP risk remains.

## Facts that could change the answer
- **Who funds the payout** — If Juniper Ledger uses its own capital, lending characterization is more likely. If it uses the merchant's own settled funds, settlement characterization is stronger.
- **Whether repayment is contingent on settlement** — If the merchant must repay even if the underlying transactions do not settle, the arrangement looks like credit.
- **The fee structure** — If fees are proportional to the amount and duration of the advance, they look like interest. If they are flat service fees, they look like settlement fees.
- **State law** — Some states have specific statutes on merchant cash advances or factoring that could change the analysis.
- **Card network rules** — Network approval may be required, and network rules may limit reserve practices.
- **Sponsor bank requirements** — Cedar Harbor Bank may impose conditions that affect the structure.

## Recommended last-mile verification
Before approving the pilot, counsel should:
1. **Engage Cedar Harbor Bank** — Determine what the program agreement requires and obtain bank approval for the feature.
2. **Confirm the characterization** — Decide whether the payout is a settlement/hold, loan, or advance, and document the rationale.
3. **Draft or update the merchant agreement** — Include clear terms on fees, timing, risk allocation, reserve practices, payout suspension criteria, and the duration of merchant liability.
4. **Verify state licensing requirements** — If the arrangement is characterized as credit, determine whether state lending licenses are required.
5. **Review card network rules** — Confirm that the payout structure complies with Visa and Mastercard rules.
6. **Implement UDAAP-compliant disclosures** — Ensure merchants receive clear, conspicuous disclosures of fees, timing, risk allocation, and suspension criteria before confirming.
7. **Establish reserve and collection policies** — Define how reserves are calculated, how negative balances are collected, and how long merchants remain liable.
8. **Document the pilot parameters** — Confirm the October 15, 2026 launch date, 10-merchant scope, $25,000 per-payout cap, and $100,000 aggregate daily cap, and ensure these are reflected in the pilot agreement.

- The pilot will involve only U.S.-based merchants.
