## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger wants to launch a unified refunds and disputes console for merchants using its payment-acceptance APIs, letting merchants issue refunds, upload dispute evidence, accept liability, or contest claims. Juniper Ledger would route messages to processors and card networks (Visa and Mastercard only), show status in the merchant dashboard, and debit merchants for valid adjustments. The merchant of record is mixed depending on the merchant/product, and some merchants sell digital goods, subscriptions, and services with delayed delivery. Product needs legal guidance before a planned launch next quarter (within ~3 months) on allocation of duties and liability, required communications, refund timing and practices, record retention, and minimum API and operational controls.
## Decision question
Before launching the refunds and disputes console next quarter, what duties and liability should be allocated among merchants, buyers, Juniper Ledger, the sponsor bank, processors, and card networks; what consumer and merchant communications are required; what refund timing and practices (including provisional credits) are permissible; what records must be retained; and what minimum API and operational controls must be in place to avoid network-rule violations, consumer-protection exposure, and unresolved liability for missed deadlines?

## Likely rules and issues
### Card network rules (Visa and Mastercard) — controlling but not retrieved
The merchant-facing chargeback timeline and evidence obligations are set by the card network rules and the merchant's acquirer agreement. These instruments were **not** included in the supplied materials, so the specific deadlines, representment windows, and compelling-evidence standards cannot be stated from the record. This is the central unresolved legal dependency for the console design.
**What is generally true (model analysis, not retrieved authority):**
- **Visa** uses the Visa Claims Resolution (VCR) framework. Disputes are categorized by reason code into allocation (fraud and authorization) and collaboration (consumer disputes and processing errors) workflows. Cardholders generally have up to 120 days from the transaction date (or expected delivery date for delayed delivery) to dispute. Issuers must submit chargebacks within network deadlines. Merchants typically have a representment window (often 30 days, but varies by reason code and region) to submit compelling evidence. Pre-arbitration and arbitration stages follow if the dispute is not resolved.
- **Mastercard** uses a similar chargeback lifecycle with reason codes, timeframes, and documentation requirements. Cardholder dispute windows and issuer chargeback deadlines are comparable but not identical to Visa. Merchants have representment rights and must submit reason-code-specific evidence.
- **Compelling evidence** standards vary by reason code. For "goods not received" or "services not provided," merchants typically need proof of delivery, proof of service usage, or evidence the cardholder received the benefit. For digital goods and subscriptions, this often includes access logs, download records, IP addresses, device fingerprints, and terms-of-service acceptance. For delayed delivery, the dispute window may run from the expected delivery date rather than the transaction date.
**Critical gap:** The exact deadlines, evidence standards, and procedural stages must be confirmed against the current Visa Core Rules, Visa Claims Resolution materials, Mastercard Chargeback Guide, and Mastercard Standards, and against the sponsor bank and processor agreements that incorporate those rules.
### Federal consumer-protection overlay — Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.13)
The supplied materials include Regulation Z's billing-error rule, which runs between the consumer and the card issuer/creditor, not between the merchant and the network. Key provisions:
- **12 CFR 1026.13(d)(1):** The consumer need not pay (and the creditor may not try to collect) any portion of any required payment that the consumer believes is related to the disputed amount, including related finance or other charges.
- **12 CFR 1026.13(d)(1) autopay carve-out:** For automatic payment plans, the issuer shall not deduct any part of the disputed amount or related charges if a billing-error notice is received any time up to 3 business days before the scheduled payment date.
- **12 CFR 1026.13(g)(1):** After resolution, the creditor must promptly notify the consumer in writing of the time when payment is due and the portion of the disputed amount and related charges that the consumer still owes.
- **12 CFR 1026.6(b)(2)(xv):** Account-opening disclosures must include a statement that information about consumers' right to dispute transactions is included in those disclosures.
**Application to Juniper Ledger:** These are cardholder-facing protections owed by the card issuer (typically the sponsor bank or the bank's card-issuing partner), not merchant-facing chargeback rules. Juniper Ledger's console must not interfere with the issuer's Reg Z obligations. If Juniper Ledger or the sponsor bank is the creditor for any credit extended through the platform, Reg Z duties may apply directly. The console should be designed so that dispute status and communications do not conflict with the issuer's billing-error resolution process.
### FCRA reinvestigation duties (15 U.S.C. § 1681i)
The supplied materials include FCRA provisions governing consumer reporting agencies:
- **15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(1)(A):** A consumer reporting agency must conduct a reasonable reinvestigation within 30 days of receiving a dispute notice from a consumer.
- **15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(2)(A):** The agency must notify the furnisher of the dispute within 5 business days.
**Application to Juniper Ledger:** These provisions become relevant only if a dispute spills into credit reporting — for example, if Juniper Ledger or the sponsor bank furnishes a tradeline on a disputed account and the consumer disputes the tradeline with a credit bureau. This is a distinct regime from card-network chargebacks and is unlikely to be a primary concern for the refunds and disputes console unless the product involves credit reporting.
### State money transmission and payments regulation
Juniper Ledger operates as a fintech without a banking charter and relies on sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A. for deposit and payment services. The company is subject to money transmission and payments regulation. The console's refund and dispute flows involve moving money (debiting merchants, crediting buyers, routing adjustments through processors and networks), which may implicate state money transmitter licensing requirements and the terms of the sponsor bank agreement. The allocation of duties and liability must be consistent with the program-management and banking-services agreement.
### Contractual allocation among parties
The console involves merchants, buyers, Juniper Ledger, the sponsor bank, processors, and card networks acting at different stages. The allocation of duties and liability will be governed by:
- **Merchant agreements** between Juniper Ledger and each merchant (which should specify refund and dispute obligations, evidence requirements, deadline responsibilities, and indemnification).
- **Sponsor bank agreement** (program-management and banking-services agreement) between Juniper Ledger and Cedar Harbor Bank.
- **Processor agreements** between Juniper Ledger (or the sponsor bank) and each processor.
- **Card network rules** (Visa and Mastercard), which are incorporated by reference into acquirer and processor agreements.
The console design must be consistent with all of these instruments. Liability for missed deadlines caused by Juniper Ledger's API or a processor should be allocated by contract, with appropriate indemnification and service-level commitments.

## Viable paths
### Path 1: Launch with conservative defaults and contractual allocation (recommended first pass)
Launch the console with the following design principles, pending confirmation of network rules and acquirer agreements:
1. **Merchant as primary obligor:** The merchant is responsible for refund decisions, dispute responses, evidence submission, and meeting network deadlines. Juniper Ledger provides the console as a tool but does not assume merchant obligations.
2. **Clear contractual allocation:** Merchant agreements specify that the merchant bears liability for missed deadlines, inadequate evidence, and chargeback losses, except where Juniper Ledger's API or a processor causes the failure. Juniper Ledger's terms of service include a service-level commitment and limited indemnification for platform-caused failures.
3. **Deadline calculation:** Use a conservative deadline-calculation rule (e.g., calculate deadlines in the cardholder's time zone or the network's designated time zone, and display countdown timers in the merchant dashboard with buffer time). Confirm the network's deadline-calculation rules before launch.
4. **Evidence authentication:** Require merchants to upload evidence through the console with metadata (timestamp, merchant ID, transaction ID) and retain original files. Juniper Ledger authenticates submission by logging the upload and routing it to the processor with a unique identifier. Do not independently verify the substance of evidence.
5. **Provisional credits:** Do not offer provisional credits at launch. Defer this feature until legal confirms permissibility under network rules, the sponsor bank agreement, and applicable consumer-protection law. If provisional credits are later offered, structure them as advances against the merchant's settlement funds with clear recourse and disclosure.
6. **Consumer communications:** The merchant is responsible for buyer-facing refund and dispute notices. Juniper Ledger provides templates and status updates in the merchant dashboard but does not send notices directly to buyers unless required by law or network rule. Confirm whether any consumer-facing notice obligations apply to Juniper Ledger as a platform or to the sponsor bank.
7. **Record retention:** Retain all refund and dispute records (transaction data, evidence files, communications, status changes, deadline calculations, and routing logs) for at least the period required by network rules and applicable law (typically 7 years for payment records, but confirm). Implement least-privilege access and audit logs consistent with Juniper Ledger's data practices.
8. **Minimum API and operational controls:** Implement idempotent API endpoints, webhook confirmations, deadline countdown timers, evidence-file size and format validation, audit logs, escalation workflows for approaching deadlines, and human review for high-value or high-risk disputes.
### Path 2: Delay launch pending network-rule confirmation
Delay the launch until counsel obtains and reviews the current Visa Core Rules, Visa Claims Resolution materials, Mastercard Chargeback Guide, Mastercard Standards, and the sponsor bank and processor agreements. This path reduces legal risk but may miss the next-quarter target.
### Path 3: Phased launch with limited scope
Launch a limited version of the console (e.g., refunds only, no dispute evidence upload) while counsel confirms network rules and acquirer agreements for the dispute-handling features. This allows the business to ship value quickly while deferring the highest-risk features.
