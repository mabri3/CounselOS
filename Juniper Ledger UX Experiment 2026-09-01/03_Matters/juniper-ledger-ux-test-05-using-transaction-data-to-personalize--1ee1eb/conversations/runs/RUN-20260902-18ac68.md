    # Preliminary Legal Analysis — Personalized Offers Using Transaction Data

    **Matter:** the internal record · Privacy Review · Privileged & Confidential

    **Prepared for:** Product (requester) / Legal (Maya Chen)

    **Status:** Preliminary draft — pending five open design decisions

    ## Labeling key

    - **[SUPPLIED FACT]** — reported by the requester or recorded in the matter.

    - **[ASSUMPTION]** — reasonable assumption used to proceed; not yet confirmed.

    - **[UNVERIFIED LEAD]** — a direction that needs confirmation before reliance.

    - **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** — my analysis based on the above.

    ---

    ## 1. Scope and framing

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** Marketing wants to use Juniper Ledger transaction history
    to show personalized offers for card-linked rewards, financial tools, and merchant
    discounts. The flow analyzes purchase categories, recurring payments, account
    balances, and payment behavior, then displays offers in-app and by email. Customers
    could turn off personalized offers in settings, but the team prefers personalization
    **on by default**. First release is planned for **next quarter**, and the business
    needs the legal answer before launch.

    **[ASSUMPTION]** Juniper Ledger is a U.S.-only fintech without a banking charter,
    relying on sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A. for deposit and payment services.
    U.S. state comprehensive privacy laws (e.g., CCPA/CPRA and other state laws) apply;
    no GDPR/EEA scope is indicated given the U.S.-only customer base.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** This is a **marketing/behavioral-advertising use of personal
    information** derived from financial transaction data. It implicates (a) state
    privacy law (notice, sensitive-data opt-in, opt-out, deletion), (b) financial-privacy
    expectations and the sponsor-bank program-management agreement, and (c) card-network
    rules for card-linked offers. The five undecided design decisions materially change
    the analysis, so this draft proceeds on stated assumptions and flags what each
    decision would change.

    ---

    ## 2. Lawful basis and notice requirements

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** Under U.S. state privacy law (CCPA/CPRA and similar),
    there is no "lawful basis" concept as in GDPR; the operative framework is **notice
    + consumer rights + (for sensitive data) opt-in consent**. The key points:

    - **Notice at or before collection/use.** The privacy notice must disclose the
    categories of personal information collected (transaction data), the purposes
    (personalized offers/marketing), and how consumers can exercise rights (opt-out,
    deletion, access). This must be updated before launch. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **No "opt-in" required for ordinary marketing use** of non-sensitive personal
    information — but a **right to opt out** of the sale/sharing of personal information
    and of cross-context behavioral advertising applies. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **Sensitive data changes the calculus.** If health, religion, or political-activity
    data is used for targeting, CCPA/CPRA and other state laws require **opt-in consent**
    before processing for that purpose. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **Financial data is sensitive by nature.** Transaction data revealing health,
    religion, or political activity is sensitive personal information under state
    law; even absent explicit categories, financial account data carries heightened
    expectations. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    **[UNVERIFIED LEAD]** Whether the sponsor-bank agreement or card-network rules
    impose additional notice or consent obligations for card-linked offers should
    be confirmed with the sponsor bank and network counsel.

    ---

    ## 3. Sensitive-data restrictions

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** Some transactions may reveal health, religion, political activity,
    or other sensitive information. Whether sensitive categories will be used for
    targeting is **not decided**.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** This is the single most decision-changing question. Two
    paths:

    - **If sensitive categories are excluded entirely** from personalization (recommended
    default), the sensitive-data opt-in burden is largely avoided — but you must still
    ensure the targeting logic cannot infer or act on sensitive categories from derived
    segments (e.g., a "pharmacy" or "religious organization" segment). **[GENERATED
    ANALYSIS]**

    - **If sensitive categories are used**, you must (i) obtain **opt-in consent**
    before processing for targeting, (ii) provide a clear, separate consent flow,
    and (iii) implement technical controls so sensitive data is not used without that
    consent. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    **[RECOMMENDATION]** Exclude sensitive transaction categories from personalization
    entirely, and design the segmentation logic so it cannot infer sensitive attributes.
    This is the lowest-risk path and avoids the opt-in burden.

    ---

    ## 4. Sharing and retention limits

    ### Sharing

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** Whether segments or other data will be shared with third-party
    offer providers, and at what detail, is **not decided**.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **No third-party sharing** is the simplest and lowest-risk design: offers are
    selected and displayed by Juniper Ledger itself. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **If sharing occurs**, sharing identifiable data or segments with offer providers
    likely constitutes a "sale"/"sharing" for cross-context behavioral advertising,
    triggering the **right to opt out** and requiring contractual controls (data-use
    limits, no re-identification, deletion obligations). **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **Aggregate/non-identifiable segments** reduce but do not eliminate risk; confirm
    the data cannot be re-identified. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    ### Retention

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** How long personalization profiles remain active is **not decided**.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** Retention should be **purpose-limited and minimized**.
    A defined, disclosed retention period (e.g., 12–24 months) with automatic deletion
    is preferable to indefinite retention. Profiles should be deleted or de-identified
    when the customer opts out or requests deletion, and on account closure. **[GENERATED
    ANALYSIS]**

    ---

    ## 5. Default settings

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** The team prefers personalization **on by default**, with an
    opt-out in settings.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** This is a significant risk point. For **non-sensitive**
    marketing personalization, opt-out-by-default is legally permissible under most
    U.S. state laws (which require a right to opt out, not opt-in). However:

    - **Sensitive data** requires **opt-in**, so if sensitive categories are used,
    default-on is not permissible for that portion. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **Regulatory and reputational risk** is high for a financial institution defaulting
    customers into marketing use of their transaction data. The sponsor bank and card
    networks may require opt-in or stronger disclosure. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    - **Recommendation:** Given the conservative risk posture and the sensitivity
    of financial transaction data, **opt-in (off by default)** is the safer and more
    defensible design, or at minimum a prominent, informed opt-in at onboarding with
    a clear opt-out. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    ---

    ## 6. Exact product controls required before launch

    **[SUPPLIED FACT]** The team lacks final language for consent, the privacy notice,
    opt-out controls, and deletion requests.

    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]** Before launch, the following product controls must be
    in place:

    1. **Privacy notice update** — disclose collection of transaction data, the personalization
    purpose, sensitive-data handling, sharing, retention, and consumer rights. **[GENERATED
    ANALYSIS]**

    2. **Consent flow** — if sensitive data is used or if opt-in is adopted, a clear,
    separate, affirmative consent at onboarding (not buried in terms). **[GENERATED
    ANALYSIS]**

    3. **Opt-out control** — a persistent, easy-to-find setting to turn off personalized
    offers, effective immediately and honored across in-app and email channels. **[GENERATED
    ANALYSIS]**

    4. **Deletion/access** — a mechanism for consumers to request access to and deletion
    of their personalization profile and underlying data, honored within statutory
    timeframes. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    5. **Sensitive-data guardrail** — technical controls preventing sensitive categories
    from being used for targeting (if excluded) or gating them behind opt-in (if used).
    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    6. **Retention automation** — automatic deletion of profiles at the disclosed
    retention period and on opt-out/deletion/account closure. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    7. **Third-party controls** — if sharing occurs, contracts limiting use, prohibiting
    re-identification, and requiring deletion. **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    8. **Sponsor-bank/network approval** — confirm the program-management agreement
    and card-network rules permit the marketing use and obtain required approvals.
    **[GENERATED ANALYSIS]**

    ---

    ## 7. Open decisions that will change this analysis

    These five decisions must be resolved before finalizing the recommendation:

    1. **Sensitive categories** — used for targeting or excluded? (Most decision-changing.)

    2. **Raw vs. derived data** — raw transaction descriptions or only derived categories/segments?

    3. **Third-party sharing** — shared with offer providers, and at what detail?

    4. **Retention** — how long profiles remain active?

    5. **Business users** — treated differently from consumers?

    ---

    ## Recommendation (distinct from the analysis)

    **Recommended path for launch:** Build personalized offers on an **opt-in, off-by-default**
    basis using **derived, non-sensitive segments only**, with **no third-party sharing**
    of identifiable data, a **defined retention period** (12–24 months) with automatic
    deletion, and **sensitive transaction categories excluded** from targeting. Ship
    the updated privacy notice, a clear consent flow, a persistent opt-out, and deletion/access
    mechanisms before launch, and obtain sponsor-bank and card-network approval.

    **Why:** This is the lowest-risk design that still delivers the marketing objective,
    avoids the sensitive-data opt-in burden, minimizes sharing and retention exposure,
    and aligns with the company''s conservative posture for regulated activity. If
    the business insists on default-on or sensitive-category targeting, the opt-in
    and consent requirements escalate materially and should be re-reviewed.
