      ## Matter summary

      Juniper Ledger, a fintech operating through sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank,
      N.A., wants to advertise "fee-free ACH transfers" to consumers and small businesses
      across paid ads, the app, email, and partner landing pages. The proposed flow
      would allow a set number of inbound and outbound ACH transfers without a Juniper
      Ledger fee, but expedited transfers, returns, and certain external-bank actions
      might carry charges, and network or third-party fees may still occur. Counsel
      is involved because the claim''s supportability under advertising law is unresolved
      and the campaign is scheduled to launch in eight weeks (target 2026-10-28).
      Product has not finalized the fee schedule, qualifying conditions, geographic
      scope, disclosure placement, or treatment of returned and unauthorized transactions.

      ## Decision question

      Can Juniper Ledger run the "fee-free ACH transfers" campaign as currently conceived
      by the 2026-10-28 launch date, and if so, what specific qualifiers, fee disclosures,
      and timing disclosures must accompany the claim in each channel (paid ads, app,
      email, partner landing pages) to avoid a deceptive-advertising exposure, and
      does Cedar Harbor Bank need to sign off before launch?

      ## Open questions

      - Which ACH transfer types will actually carry a fee, and what is the final
      fee schedule? This determines whether "fee-free" can be supported at all and
      what qualifiers are needed.

      - What conditions qualify a customer for the fee-free tier (e.g., account type,
      transfer count caps, minimum balance)?

      - What is the geographic scope of the offer and the claim?

      - Has Cedar Harbor Bank reviewed or approved the campaign and the fee structure?

      - Has any consumer research been done on how customers understand "fee-free"
      (e.g., whether they expect it to exclude expedited or third-party charges)?

      ### FTC "free" claims framework

      The FTC''s guidance on use of the word "free" (and similar representations)
      is longstanding. The core principle is that when an advertiser offers something
      as "free," all material conditions, limitations, or charges that a consumer
      must incur to obtain the "free" item or service must be disclosed clearly and
      conspicuously at the outset of the offer, not buried in fine print or revealed
      only at checkout. Key elements:

      - **All material terms must be disclosed.** If a "free" offer is conditioned
      on the consumer purchasing something else, paying fees, or meeting eligibility
      requirements, those conditions are material and must be disclosed in immediate
      conjunction with the "free" claim.

      - **"Free" means the consumer pays nothing for the offered item or service.**
      If the consumer must pay any charge to obtain the "free" item, the claim is
      deceptive unless the charge is clearly and conspicuously disclosed.

      - **Disclosure must be clear and conspicuous.** The FTC evaluates disclosures
      using a "net impression" test: the overall impression created by the ad, including
      the claim and any qualifying disclosures, must not be misleading. Disclosures
      must be in close proximity to the claim, in a font size and format that consumers
      are likely to notice and read, and must not be contradicted by other elements
      of the ad.

      - **Frequency and repetition.** If the "free" claim is repeated in an ad, the
      disclosure should be repeated or clearly associated each time.

      ### Application to "fee-free ACH transfers"

      The proposed claim presents several specific deception risks under this framework:

      1. **Expedited transfers carry charges.** If a consumer sees "fee-free ACH transfers"
      and later learns that expedited transfers cost extra, the claim could be deceptive
      unless the ad clearly discloses that the "fee-free" claim applies only to standard-speed
      transfers. The qualifier must appear with the claim, not on a separate page
      or in a linked disclosure.

      2. **Returns and external-bank actions may carry charges.** If returned ACH
      items or actions by the consumer''s external bank result in fees (even fees
      charged by Juniper Ledger or passed through from third parties), the "fee-free"
      claim is potentially misleading. The FTC has treated undisclosed or inadequately
      disclosed ancillary fees as deceptive in numerous enforcement actions.

      3. **Network and third-party fees may still occur.** Even if Juniper Ledger
      charges no fee, if the ACH network or the consumer''s own bank may impose charges,
      the claim "fee-free" could create a net impression that the consumer will pay
      nothing. This is a material condition that must be disclosed.

      4. **Transfer availability depends on risk and settlement timing.** If the "fee-free"
      transfers are subject to holds, delays, or risk-based restrictions that a typical
      consumer would not expect, those conditions may be material and require disclosure.

      5. **Set number of transfers.** If the "fee-free" offer is limited to a set
      number of inbound and outbound transfers, that cap is a material limitation
      that must be disclosed with the claim.

      ### Consumer vs. business audience

      The request states the claim targets both consumers and small businesses. The
      FTC Act applies to both, but the analysis differs:

      - **Consumers:** The full deception framework applies. The FTC''s "free" guidance
      and its deception policy statement are directly relevant. State UDAP (unfair
      and deceptive acts and practices) statutes also apply and may impose additional
      requirements.

      - **Small businesses:** The FTC Act still applies, but the FTC has historically
      given somewhat more latitude in B2B contexts, assuming businesses are more sophisticated.
      However, the FTC has brought deception cases involving small businesses, particularly
      where the business is unsophisticated or the claim is likely to mislead. State
      UDAP statutes vary in whether they cover business-to-business transactions.

      ### Sponsor bank approval

      Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A. is the named bank for deposit accounts and payment rails.
      Under the program-management and banking-services agreement, the bank likely
      has oversight and approval rights over marketing materials that reference its
      services or that could create regulatory exposure for the bank. Whether the
      bank "must" approve depends on the specific terms of the agreement, but as a
      practical matter:

      - The bank''s regulators (OCC, FDIC, or state banking regulators) will hold
      the bank responsible for third-party marketing that is deceptive or that misrepresents
      the bank''s products.

      - The bank almost certainly has contractual approval rights over marketing that
      references ACH services, deposit accounts, or the bank''s name.

      - Even if the agreement does not explicitly require approval, launching without
      bank sign-off creates significant relationship and regulatory risk.

      ### State law considerations

      State UDAP statutes (e.g., California''s Unfair Competition Law and False Advertising
      Law, New York''s General Business Law §§ 349-350, Washington''s Consumer Protection
      Act) impose similar deception standards and may have specific requirements for
      "free" claims. Some states have specific statutes or regulations governing advertising
      of financial services. The geographic scope of the campaign (which is not yet
      finalized) will determine which state laws apply.

      ## Viable paths

      ### Path 1: Revise the claim to eliminate "fee-free"

      Replace "fee-free ACH transfers" with a more precise claim such as "No Juniper
      Ledger fee on standard ACH transfers" or "Standard ACH transfers at no charge
      from Juniper Ledger." This approach:

      - Eliminates the deception risk associated with the word "free."

      - Allows the company to highlight the absence of its own fee without implying
      that no fees of any kind will ever apply.

      - Still requires disclosure of material conditions (e.g., expedited transfers
      cost extra, third-party fees may apply), but the claim itself is less likely
      to create a misleading net impression.

      **Trade-off:** Less punchy marketing language.

      ### Path 2: Retain "fee-free" with robust qualifiers

      Keep the "fee-free ACH transfers" claim but add clear, conspicuous qualifiers
      in immediate conjunction with the claim in every channel. Required qualifiers
      would include:

      - "Fee-free applies to standard ACH transfers only. Expedited transfers, returned
      items, and certain other transactions may incur fees."

      - "Third-party fees (e.g., from your bank) may still apply."

      - "Limited to [X] inbound and [Y] outbound transfers per [period]."

      - "Transfer availability subject to risk review and settlement timing."

      **Trade-off:** Higher risk. The FTC and state regulators may still find the
      net impression deceptive if the qualifiers are not sufficiently prominent or
      if consumer testing shows that a typical consumer does not understand the limitations.
      This path requires consumer research to validate that the net impression is
      not misleading.

      ### Path 3: Delay launch until fee schedule and conditions are finalized

      Postpone the campaign until Product finalizes the fee schedule, qualifying conditions,
      geographic scope, and treatment of returned and unauthorized transactions. This
      allows counsel to evaluate the claim against the actual product terms and to
      draft precise disclosures.

      **Trade-off:** Misses the 2026-10-28 launch date.

      ### Path 4: Phased launch with limited claim

      Launch with a narrower claim (e.g., "No Juniper Ledger fee on your first [X]
      ACH transfers") that is easier to substantiate, and expand the claim later once
      the fee schedule and consumer research are complete.

      **Trade-off:** Reduces initial marketing impact but allows the campaign to launch
      on time with lower risk.
