# Determine whether advertising "fee-free ACH transfers" to consumers and small

## Question

Determine whether advertising "fee-free ACH transfers" to consumers and small businesses is supportable, what qualifiers must appear with the claim, how fees and timing must be presented across paid ads/app/email/partner landing pages, which consumer and business advertising rules apply, and whether the sponsor bank must approve the campaign. Campaign launches 2026-10-28.

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger, a fintech operating through sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A., wants to advertise "fee-free ACH transfers" to consumers and small businesses across paid ads, the app, email, and partner landing pages. The proposed product would waive Juniper Ledger's fee on a set number of standard inbound and outbound ACH transfers, while expedited transfers, returns, and certain external-bank actions may still carry charges, and network or third-party fees may still apply. Counsel is involved because the fee schedule, qualifying conditions, geographic scope, disclosure placement, and treatment of returned and unauthorized transactions are not finalized, no consumer testing has been done on how "fee-free" is understood, and sponsor bank approval status is unknown. The campaign is scheduled to launch 2026-10-28.
## Decision question
Can Juniper Ledger launch the "fee-free ACH transfers" claim by 2026-10-28 across paid ads, app, email, and partner pages, given that expedited transfers, returns, external-bank actions, and network or third-party fees may still cost the customer money — and if so, what qualifiers, fee and timing disclosures, and sponsor bank approvals must be in place before launch to avoid a deceptive net impression?
## Open questions
- Which ACH transfer types will actually carry a fee, and what is the final fee schedule? This determines whether "fee-free" can be supported and what qualifiers are needed.
- What conditions qualify a customer for the fee-free tier (transfer caps, measurement period, plan, balance, or account limits)?
- What is the geographic scope of the offer and the claim, which drives which state UDAP laws apply?
- Has the sponsor bank (Cedar Harbor) reviewed or approved the campaign and the fee structure, and what does the program-management agreement require?
- Has any consumer research been done on how customers understand "fee-free" (e.g., whether they expect it to exclude expedited or third-party charges)?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The analysis below is model-generated issue-spotting, not retrieved authority. Counsel must verify current primary sources before launch.
**Federal consumer advertising law.** The core risk is deception under FTC Act section 5. The FTC evaluates the overall net impression of an ad, including both express claims and implied claims a reasonable consumer takes away. "Fee-free ACH transfers" is an unqualified "free" claim. The FTC's guidance on use of the word "free" (16 C.F.R. part 251) requires that when a "free" offer is conditioned on other purchases or has material limits, those conditions must be disclosed at the outset and in close conjunction with the claim, clearly and conspicuously. A claim that a transfer is "fee-free" while the customer may still pay expedite fees, return fees, external-bank charges, or network/third-party fees is a classic setup for a misleading net impression unless the qualifiers travel with the claim.
**Clear and conspicuous disclosure standards.** The FTC's ".com Disclosures" guidance and its enforcement history on hidden or buried fees (including "junk fee" and drip-pricing matters) are directly relevant. Material limits cannot live only behind a hyperlink, tooltip, footer, or a screen the user sees after committing. Each channel — paid ad, app, email, partner landing page — must carry the qualifier in proximity to the claim.
**State UDAP laws.** Every state has an unfair or deceptive acts and practices statute. Once geographic scope is finalized, counsel must check the specific states' standards, some of which are broader than the FTC Act and carry statutory damages or attorney's fees. Several states also have specific fee-disclosure or "junk fee" statutes (e.g., California's honest-pricing law) that may bear on how fees are presented.
**Small-business audience.** The FTC Act's deception authority is not limited to individual consumers; small businesses can be protected purchasers. Some state UDAP statutes, however, cover only consumer transactions, so the business-audience analysis is state-specific. Do not assume a small-business recipient understands that "fee-free" excludes another bank's charges.
**Bank-partner oversight.** Because deposit and payment services run through Cedar Harbor Bank, the bank likely has contractual approval rights over marketing that references its services, and federal banking regulators expect banks to oversee third-party fintech marketing for compliance. The program-management and banking-services agreement must be checked for an express marketing-approval clause.
**Nacha and payments rules.** Nacha operating rules and the ACH service provider agreements may impose their own requirements on how ACH services are described and on return/unauthorized-transaction handling, which affects what the disclosures must say.
## Viable paths
**Path A — Narrower claim (lower risk, recommended in the working recommendation).** Use "No Juniper Ledger fee on standard ACH transfers" (or similar). This claim is accurate on its face even if third-party or expedite fees exist, because it attributes the zero fee to Juniper Ledger specifically. It still requires clear disclosure of the transfer cap, eligibility, and the existence of other fees, but it avoids the strongest deceptive-net-impression risk. This path can likely meet the 2026-10-28 date if the fee schedule is finalized promptly.
**Path B — Keep "fee-free ACH transfers" with full qualification (higher risk, more conditions).** Launch only after: (1) the fee schedule is final and confirms standard ACH carries no Juniper Ledger fee; (2) qualifying conditions, caps, and geographic scope are fixed; (3) every channel carries a clear, conspicuous, proximate qualifier stating the cap, eligibility, and that expedited transfers, returns, external-bank actions, and network/third-party fees may still apply; (4) fee and timing information is shown in-app before transfer-type selection and again before confirmation; (5) consumer testing confirms a typical customer understands the claim as qualified; and (6) Cedar Harbor gives written approval. If any condition is unmet at the launch gate, delay or fall back to Path A.
**Path C — Delay launch.** If the fee schedule, scope, or bank approval cannot be finalized in time, hold the campaign rather than launch an unqualified or unapproved claim.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Final fee schedule.** If standard ACH turns out to carry any Juniper Ledger fee in some cases, even the narrower claim fails.
- **Whether "free" transfers are truly unconditional within the cap.** If eligibility requires a paid plan, minimum balance, or other consideration, the FTC "free" rule's conditional-offer requirements apply in full.
- **Geographic scope.** A national campaign triggers more state UDAP and fee-disclosure statutes than a limited one.
- **The Cedar Harbor agreement's marketing-approval clause.** An express clause makes bank approval a hard contractual gate; silence shifts the analysis to regulatory oversight expectations.
- **Consumer testing results.** Evidence that typical customers read "fee-free" as "I pay nothing" would effectively foreclose Path B without a heavily qualified presentation.
- **Treatment of returns and unauthorized transactions.** If customers can be charged in those common scenarios, the qualifier must say so prominently; if they cannot, the claim gets easier to support.
## Recommended last-mile verification
1. Pull and read the marketing/approval provisions of the Cedar Harbor program-management and banking-services agreement; get written bank sign-off on final creative before any publication.
2. Verify current text of 16 C.F.R. part 251 (FTC "free" guide), FTC Act section 5, the FTC ".com Disclosures" guidance, and any recent FTC fee-related enforcement or rulemaking (including junk-fee initiatives).
3. Once geographic scope is set, check each state's UDAP statute and any state-specific fee-disclosure or honest-pricing laws.
4. Confirm Nacha rules and ACH provider contract terms that constrain how transfers, returns, and timing may be described.
5. Obtain the signed fee schedule, eligibility rules, caps, and return/unauthorized-transaction treatment from Product, and lock approved claim language and qualifier placement per channel with version control for partner pages.
6. Run or commission quick consumer-perception testing on the proposed claim and qualifier before the launch gate.
