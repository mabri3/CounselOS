# How do Reg E and card-network rules allocate liability for

## Question

How do Reg E and card-network rules allocate liability for unauthorized use and disputes on a card issued to a minor authorized user?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger, a fintech operating through sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A., is designing a household feature that would let an adult primary account holder issue a virtual or physical card to a teenager who may be under 18. The adult would invite the teen, set spending categories and limits, view transactions, and approve selected merchants; the teen could make purchases and receive refunds but could not initiate ACH or account-to-account transfers. Counsel is involved because the teen's age and undecided customer status drive consent, privacy, disclosure, and liability obligations. Product wants research-ready requirements for a limited beta next summer.
## Decision question
For a limited beta next summer, can Juniper Ledger issue a restricted debit card to a minor as a named authorized user on the adult's account — with the adult bearing primary liability under Reg E and network rules — and what minimum age, consent evidence, and dispute-handling terms must be locked before user testing so unauthorized-use losses and chargebacks are allocated predictably?
## Open questions
- Will the teen be a Juniper Ledger customer in their own right or an authorized user on the adult's account? This drives who is the "consumer" under Reg E and who receives disclosures and error-resolution rights.
- What minimum age will Product set, and will any users be under 13 (triggering COPPA verifiable parental consent for data collection)?
- Have Cedar Harbor's program agreement and the card networks' age/cardholder rules been confirmed to permit minor authorized users, and on what terms?
- How will disputes and unauthorized use be allocated contractually between the adult, Juniper Ledger, and Cedar Harbor — including whether the adult's grant of card access to the teen is treated as furnishing an access device?
- Who receives Reg E initial disclosures, periodic statements, and privacy notices — the adult only, or the teen as well?
## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is model-only analysis based on general knowledge of the regulatory framework; it is not retrieved authority and should be verified against current sources before reliance.
**Regulation E (12 C.F.R. Part 1005, implementing EFTA).** Reg E governs electronic fund transfers on consumer accounts. Key points for this design:
- **Who is protected.** Reg E protects "consumers" — natural persons — with respect to accounts established primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. If the teen is an authorized user rather than the account holder, the adult account holder is the consumer whose account is affected, and error-resolution rights and liability limits run to the adult. If the teen is made a customer with their own account, the teen would hold Reg E rights directly, which is awkward for a minor who may lack capacity to contract.
- **Unauthorized EFTs and liability tiers.** Under the standard framework, consumer liability for unauthorized transfers is capped at $50 if the consumer notifies the institution within two business days of learning of the loss or theft of an access device, up to $500 if notification is later but within 60 days of statement transmittal, and potentially unlimited for transfers occurring after the 60-day window if the consumer failed to report. These caps apply to the account holder.
- **Furnishing the access device.** A transfer initiated by a person to whom the consumer furnished the access device is generally not "unauthorized" unless the consumer has notified the institution that the person is no longer authorized. This is the central design issue: if the adult invites the teen and the card is issued with the adult's consent, the teen's own spending is authorized by definition. "Unauthorized use" claims by the adult against the teen's spending will likely fail under this rule unless the adult first revokes the teen's access (pause/freeze). This makes the adult-facing controls — pause, freeze, merchant blocking, limit changes — the operative loss-control mechanism, and they must work in near real time.
- **Error resolution.** The institution must investigate reported errors within prescribed timelines (generally 10 business days, or 45 days with provisional credit; longer for new accounts and POS/foreign transactions). The dispute flow must route through the adult as account holder, with support permissions defining what the teen can report.
- **Disclosures.** Initial Reg E disclosures (liability, error resolution, contact points), periodic statements, and change-in-terms notices are required. In an authorized-user model these go to the adult; the product must decide whether the teen sees any transaction-level detail and how that intersects with privacy choices.
**Card-network rules.** Network rules (Visa/Mastercard) layer chargeback and zero-liability policies on top of Reg E for signature and PIN network transactions. Zero-liability marketing typically excludes transactions by authorized users, reinforcing that teen spending is the adult's responsibility. Networks also impose issuer-side requirements on cardholder eligibility, and some programs restrict issuance to minors or require the primary cardholder to be 18+. Cedar Harbor, as issuer of record, will have program requirements that may set a minimum age, require the minor to be a family member, or dictate cardholder agreement terms. These have not been reviewed yet and are a gating item.
**Merchant disputes and chargebacks.** For merchandise disputes (non-delivery, not-as-described), chargeback rights run through the issuer under network rules regardless of the cardholder's age. Operationally, the adult would initiate disputes; the product should not expose dispute initiation to the teen without adult visibility.
**Refund handling.** Refunds to the teen's card credit the adult's account. This is straightforward but should be disclosed so the adult understands refunds do not go to the teen personally.
**State law overlay.** State money-transmission and consumer-protection laws generally defer to the Reg E framework for EFT liability, but state minor-capacity doctrine matters for the cardholder agreement: a minor can generally disaffirm contracts, which is a strong reason to keep the teen out of contractual privity and use the authorized-user structure.
## Viable paths
1. **Authorized-user model (recommended in the matter record).** Adult is the sole customer and account holder; teen is a named, restricted supplementary cardholder. Reg E rights and liability sit with the adult; teen spending is authorized use; loss control runs through adult controls and revocation. Simplest for KYC, disclosures, and dispute routing. Requires Cedar Harbor and network confirmation that minor authorized users are permitted and at what minimum age.
2. **Teen as joint or sub-account customer.** Teen becomes a customer with Reg E rights directly. This triggers full KYC/CIP on the minor, direct privacy notices, COPPA analysis if under 13, and contract-capacity problems. Not recommended for a limited beta.
3. **Custodial or trust-style structure.** Over-engineered for this use case; adds fiduciary and state-law complexity without product benefit.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Teen as customer vs. authorized user.** If Product insists the teen be a customer, Reg E disclosures, error-resolution rights, and liability caps attach to the teen directly, and contract-capacity and COPPA issues expand substantially.
- **Minimum age below 13.** Any under-13 user triggers COPPA verifiable parental consent for online data collection and likely child-directed privacy controls; this changes consent flows, notices, and data practices.
- **Cedar Harbor program terms.** If the sponsor bank prohibits minor cardholders or sets a higher age floor (e.g., 16+), the beta scope shrinks or the model must change.
- **Network age rules.** Network-specific restrictions could require the primary cardholder to be 18+ or impose supplementary-card conditions that alter the design.
- **Whether the adult can be treated as having furnished the access device.** If the flow ever lets the teen activate or control the card without adult action, the unauthorized-use analysis shifts.
- **Credit or overdraft features.** Adding any credit element would pull in TILA/Reg Z and ability-to-pay issues and is correctly excluded from the beta.
