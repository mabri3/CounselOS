# What does COPPA require for verifiable parental consent and privacy

## Question

What does COPPA require for verifiable parental consent and privacy notices when a platform collects personal information from children under 13?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger is designing a household feature that would let an adult account holder issue a virtual or physical card to a teenager, with the adult setting spending categories and limits, viewing transactions, and approving selected merchants. The teenager could make purchases and receive refunds but could not initiate ACH or account-to-account transfers. Counsel is involved because the teenager may be under 18, and the design raises questions about age and consent rules, cardholder status, privacy and marketing restrictions, dispute liability, and required disclosures. Product wants research-ready requirements for a limited beta next summer.
## Decision question
Before user testing begins, what minimum age, consent mechanism, cardholder status, privacy-notice recipients, data-use restrictions, and dispute-liability allocation must Juniper Ledger lock down to run a legally compliant limited beta of the teen card feature next summer?
## Open questions
- What minimum age will Product set for teen cardholders, and will any users be under 13 (triggering COPPA)?
- Will the teenager be a Juniper Ledger customer in their own right or an authorized user on the adult's account?
- How will the adult's consent be collected and documented, and who will receive privacy notices (GLBA/Reg P and COPPA)?
- May marketing, transaction data, and behavioral analytics include the teenager, and if so under what consent framework?
- Have Cedar Harbor's program requirements and card-network age rules been reviewed for this feature?

## Likely rules and issues
**COPPA (15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506; 16 C.F.R. Part 312).** If Juniper Ledger collects personal information from children under 13, COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collection, use, or disclosure. The FTC's COPPA Rule prescribes acceptable consent mechanisms (e.g., signed consent form, credit/debit card transaction, toll-free call, video conference, government-ID check) and requires direct notice to parents and a clear online privacy policy. The supplied public research did not contain COPPA's text or the FTC rule, so the specific consent mechanisms and notice content cannot be confirmed from the materials provided.
**Age and contract capacity.** Minors generally cannot enter binding contracts. Card issuance to a teenager requires adult consent and careful structuring of who is the cardholder and who bears liability.
**Account/cardholder status.** Whether the teenager is a customer of Juniper Ledger or an authorized user on the adult's account changes KYC, privacy, and disclosure obligations. An authorized-user model keeps the adult as the primary customer and funder, with the teen as a named restricted supplementary cardholder.
**Privacy (GLBA/Reg P and state laws).** Depending on the data flows, GLBA privacy notices and state privacy laws may apply. The recipients of those notices depend on whether the teen is a customer or authorized user.
**Marketing and analytics.** Using a minor's transaction and behavioral data for marketing raises COPPA and state privacy concerns. Personalized marketing, sale/share, ad targeting, and behavioral analytics based on teen data should be off by default.
**Dispute and unauthorized-use liability.** Regulation E and card-network rules allocate liability, but the teenager's status and the adult's control affect who bears loss. The design must document how disputes and unauthorized use will be handled.
**Sponsor bank and card-network rules.** Cedar Harbor's program requirements and network age rules may constrain the design and must be reviewed before launch.
## Viable paths
1. **Authorized-user model, 13+ only.** The adult remains the primary customer and funder; the teen is a named restricted supplementary cardholder. This avoids COPPA if no personal information is collected from children under 13. Consent is collected from the adult, and privacy notices go to the adult. This is the lowest-risk path for a limited beta.
2. **Authorized-user model, under-13 included.** Same structure, but Juniper Ledger implements COPPA-compliant verifiable parental consent and child-directed privacy controls. This adds significant compliance overhead and is not recommended for an initial beta.
3. **Teen as customer.** The teen becomes a Juniper Ledger customer in their own right. This triggers full KYC, privacy, and disclosure obligations for the teen and likely requires the teen to be 18 or older (or emancipated), which defeats the product purpose.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Minimum age.** If Product sets the floor at 13+, COPPA likely does not apply. If any users are under 13, COPPA compliance becomes mandatory.
- **Cardholder status.** If the teen is an authorized user rather than a customer, KYC and privacy obligations shift to the adult.
- **Data flows.** If the teen's transaction or behavioral data is used for marketing or analytics, COPPA and state privacy laws impose additional restrictions.
- **Sponsor bank and network rules.** Cedar Harbor or the card networks may impose age floors or other constraints that override Product's preferences.
- **Consent mechanism.** The method of collecting adult consent must satisfy COPPA's verifiable-parental-consent standard if under-13 users are included.
