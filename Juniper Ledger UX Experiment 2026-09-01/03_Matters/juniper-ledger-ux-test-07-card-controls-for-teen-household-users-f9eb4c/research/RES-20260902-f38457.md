# What are the card-network (Visa/Mastercard) age requirements and rules for

## Question

What are the card-network (Visa/Mastercard) age requirements and rules for issuing cards to minors and authorized users under 18?

## Working Analysis

**No external authority retrieved**

## Matter summary
Juniper Ledger, a fintech operating through sponsor bank Cedar Harbor Bank, N.A., is designing a household feature that would let an adult primary account holder issue a virtual or physical card to a teenager who may be under 18. The adult would invite the teen, set spending categories and limits, view transactions, and approve selected merchants; the teen could make purchases and receive refunds but could not initiate ACH or account-to-account transfers. Counsel is involved because issuing cards to minors implicates card-network rules, sponsor-bank program requirements, age and consent law, privacy and marketing restrictions, and dispute-liability allocation. Product wants research-ready requirements for a limited beta next summer, and Cedar Harbor's program requirements and card-network age rules have not yet been reviewed.
## Decision question
Before the limited beta next summer, can Juniper Ledger structure teen card issuance as an authorized-user model under the adult's account — with the teen as a named restricted supplementary cardholder starting at age 13 or older — in a way that satisfies Visa/Mastercard network rules and Cedar Harbor's program requirements, or must the teen be a full customer, and what minimum age, consent, and control conditions do the networks and sponsor bank impose?
## Open questions
- What minimum age, if any, do Visa and Mastercard network rules and Cedar Harbor's card-program agreement impose for supplementary or authorized-user cardholders, and do those rules differ for virtual versus physical cards?
- Does Cedar Harbor's program agreement require the teen to be treated as a customer (with independent KYC/CIP and disclosures) or permit a pure authorized-user/supplementary-cardholder structure under the adult's account?
- If any teen under 13 is included, can Juniper Ledger implement COPPA-compliant verifiable parental consent and child-directed privacy controls in time for the beta, or should the beta be restricted to 13+?
- How do the network rules and Cedar Harbor allocate liability for unauthorized use and disputes on a minor's card when the adult controls the account and funding?
- What disclosures, privacy notices (GLBA/Reg P and, if applicable, COPPA), and marketing/data-use restrictions apply to teen transaction and behavioral data under the chosen structure?

## Likely rules and issues
**No external authority retrieved.** The following is generated analysis based on general legal and industry knowledge, not retrieved authority. It should be verified against current network rules and Cedar Harbor's program documents.
**Card-network framework.** Visa and Mastercard generally do not set a universal minimum age for cardholders in their public core rules; instead, they require the issuer (here, Cedar Harbor as the bank of record) to ensure the cardholder is legally able to enter into the cardholder agreement or that an appropriate authorized-user structure is used. For minors, the standard industry pattern is:
- **Primary cardholder must be an adult** (typically 18+, or the age of majority in the relevant state) who enters the cardholder agreement and bears primary liability.
- **Minors may be added as authorized users or supplementary cardholders** on the adult's account. Networks generally permit this, but the issuer remains responsible for ensuring the arrangement complies with applicable law (contract capacity, consumer-protection statutes, privacy law).
- **Issuer-specific and program-specific age floors** are common. Many issuers set internal minimum ages (often 13, 15, or 16) for supplementary cardholders even where network rules are silent. These are typically found in the issuer's card-program agreement, BIN-sponsor terms, or program-management agreement rather than in public network rules.
- **Virtual vs. physical cards.** Network rules generally treat virtual and physical cards the same for age and cardholder-status purposes, but issuer processors and program managers sometimes impose different operational controls.
**Contract capacity and consent.** Because minors generally lack capacity to enter binding contracts, the teen cannot be the primary obligor. The adult must be the primary account holder and funder, and the cardholder agreement should name the adult as responsible for all teen transactions. The teen's use should be framed as permissive use under the adult's account, not as an independent contractual relationship, unless Juniper Ledger deliberately structures the teen as a customer with adult co-signature or consent.
**KYC/CIP and customer status.** If the teen is a mere authorized user, Customer Identification Program (CIP) obligations generally run to the adult as the account holder. If the teen becomes a customer in their own right, CIP, OFAC screening, and potentially beneficial-ownership or minor-specific rules may apply. The authorized-user model is the lower-friction path and aligns with the recorded recommendation.
**Privacy and marketing.** For teens under 13, COPPA applies to online collection of personal information and requires verifiable parental consent; behavioral analytics and targeted marketing to under-13s are heavily restricted. For teens 13–17, COPPA does not apply, but state privacy laws (e.g., California's CCPA/CPRA and other state comprehensive statutes) may restrict sale/share of teen data, targeted advertising, and profiling without consent. GLBA/Reg P privacy notices should be delivered to the adult as the customer; whether the teen also receives a notice depends on whether the teen is treated as a consumer under the chosen structure.
**Dispute and unauthorized-use liability.** Regulation E (for debit/EFT) and card-network zero-liability or limited-liability rules allocate loss, but the adult's control over the account and the teen's status as an authorized user generally mean the adult bears primary responsibility for teen transactions. Network rules typically require the issuer to handle disputes through the primary cardholder; the teen's lack of contractual capacity reinforces that the adult is the proper party for dispute resolution.
**Sponsor-bank overlay.** Cedar Harbor, as the named bank for deposit accounts and payment rails, will have its own program requirements, risk appetite, and compliance expectations. These may impose a minimum age, require specific consent language, mandate adult controls, or restrict marketing to minors. The fact that these requirements have not yet been reviewed is a material gap.
## Viable paths
1. **Authorized-user / supplementary-cardholder model (recommended starting point).** Adult remains primary customer and funder; teen is a named restricted supplementary cardholder. Set a minimum age of 13+ (or an older floor if Cedar Harbor or the networks require). Keep the product debit-only, prohibit ACH/account-to-account transfers, credit, overdraft, and teen-controlled cash-out. This path minimizes KYC, privacy, and contract-capacity friction.
2. **Teen-as-customer with adult co-signature or consent.** The teen becomes a Juniper Ledger customer with the adult as co-signer or consenting party. This path increases KYC/CIP, disclosure, privacy-notice, and potentially state minor-contract law complexity. It may be necessary only if Cedar Harbor or the networks require the teen to be a direct customer, or if Product wants the teen to have independent account features later.
3. **Age-restricted beta (13+ only).** Exclude under-13 teens entirely for the beta. This avoids COPPA verifiable-parental-consent obligations and child-directed privacy controls, simplifying the privacy and marketing analysis. If Product later wants under-13 users, a separate COPPA-compliant track would be needed.
4. **State-by-state age floor.** If the age of majority or minor-contract rules vary materially by state, Juniper Ledger could set the beta age floor at the highest relevant state age (e.g., 19 in Alabama and Nebraska, 21 in Mississippi for some purposes) or restrict the beta to states with a uniform 18+ majority age. This is likely overkill for a limited beta but may matter for a national launch.
## Facts that could change the answer
- **Cedar Harbor's program agreement and card-network BIN-sponsor terms.** If these impose a minimum age above 13, require the teen to be a customer, or mandate specific consent/disclosure language, the recommended structure and timeline could shift.
- **Whether the teen is treated as a customer or authorized user.** This drives KYC/CIP, privacy-notice recipients, dispute-liability allocation, and whether the teen can be marketed to directly.
- **Minimum age chosen.** Including under-13 teens triggers COPPA and requires verifiable parental consent and child-directed privacy controls; restricting to 13+ avoids COPPA but still implicates state privacy laws for minors.
- **Virtual vs. physical card differences.** If Cedar Harbor or the networks impose different age or control requirements for physical cards, the product may need to launch virtual-only or add physical-card controls.
- **State privacy-law scope.** If Juniper Ledger has users in states with strong minor-privacy protections (e.g., California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah), marketing and analytics uses of teen data may require opt-in consent or be prohibited.
- **Refund and chargeback handling.** If the teen can receive refunds, the flow of funds and dispute rights must be mapped to the adult's account to avoid Reg E and network-rule conflicts.
## Recommended last-mile verification
Before finalizing the beta requirements, counsel should:
1. **Obtain and review Cedar Harbor's card-program agreement, BIN-sponsor terms, and any program-management or banking-services agreement provisions** governing supplementary cardholders, minimum age, authorized users, and minor cardholders.
2. **Review current Visa and Mastercard issuer/program rules** (including any fintech or prepaid/debit program guides) for age, authorized-user, and minor-cardholder requirements. Confirm whether network rules are silent and defer to issuer policy or impose specific floors.
3. **Confirm with Cedar Harbor's compliance team** whether the proposed authorized-user model is permitted, what consent language they require, and what minimum age they support for virtual and physical cards.
4. **Map the data flows** for teen transaction and behavioral data against COPPA (if under-13), GLBA/Reg P, and applicable state privacy laws to determine notice recipients, consent requirements, and marketing/analytics restrictions.
5. **Draft the cardholder agreement and disclosures** so the adult is the primary obligor, the teen's use is permissive, and dispute/liability allocation is clear under Reg E and network rules.
6. **Document the product decisions** (age floor, customer status, consent mechanics, notice recipients, data-use defaults, dispute handling) in a requirements memo that Product can use for user-testing design.
This recommendation is provisional until Cedar Harbor and network requirements are confirmed. The lawyer's decision on whether to proceed, and on what terms, should be based on the verified program documents and network rules rather than this first-pass analysis.
